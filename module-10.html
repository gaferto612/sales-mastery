10Commitment

A close is a decision, and an honest no is a result.

Closing techniques built on social pressure produce signatures that churn, references that never happen, and sellers nobody calls back. This module treats closing as decision readiness: recognizing it, asking clearly, and being willing to hear no early rather than never.

1. What closing is and is not

Closing has the worst literature in the profession. Decades of training material treat it as a moment of applied pressure — the assumptive close, the alternative close, the puppy-dog close, the Ben Franklin close — as though the seller's job at the end is to extract a signature the buyer would not otherwise give.

That framing was always ethically shaky. What has changed is that it is now commercially wrong as well, for two reasons. First, in subscription and relationship businesses the signature is not the end of the transaction but the start of a cost — a customer who signed under pressure churns, does not expand, will not act as a reference, and consumes support disproportionately. Second, the dominant reason deals fail is not that buyers resist; it is that they cannot decide. Applying pressure to that condition makes it worse, as Module 09 established.

The professional definition: closing is helping a buyer reach and act on a decision they can defend — including the decision not to proceed. Under this definition an honest, early "no" is a good outcome. It returns your capacity to something real, it preserves the relationship for a later cycle, and it is enormously more valuable than the six months of polite non-response that most sellers accept as the alternative.

Iannarino's work on commitment sequences (class F) makes the useful structural point: a complex sale is not one close but a series of small, genuine commitments — to explore, to involve others, to invest time, to test, to decide. A seller who has never asked for any of the intermediate commitments has no basis for expecting the final one.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Assess decision readiness against five conditions and identify which is missing before asking for commitment.

Grade common closing techniques by whether they aid or subvert a defensible decision.

Ask for commitment in clear, unambiguous language without softening it into invisibility.

Build a mutual action plan with named owners, dates, and reciprocal obligations.

Elicit an honest no, and treat it as a legitimate and valuable outcome.

3. Decision readiness: the five conditions

Asking for commitment before readiness produces one of three bad outcomes: a no you did not need to hear yet, a soft yes that unravels, or an evasion that costs you the ability to ask cleanly again. Check the five conditions first.

Table 1: The readiness checklist

ConditionEvidence it is metEvidence it is notWhat to do if missing
1. The problem is ownedA named person has described the consequence of not solving it, in their own words and numbers.You are the one who keeps restating the problem.Return to diagnosis (Module 33). You do not have a deal.
2. The solution is understoodThe buyer can explain, without you, what will change and how.They describe your product's features rather than their changed situation.Rebuild the value articulation (Module 01) and let them explain it back.
3. The economics are agreedSomeone with budget authority has seen and accepted the numbers, including the assumptions.The business case exists but has only been seen by your champion.Get the case in front of the economic buyer, ideally as a document that travels (Module 06).
4. The process is knownYou can name every remaining step, its owner, and its duration."It just needs sign-off." Sign-off by whom, requiring what, taking how long?Ask how the last comparable purchase actually got approved — a past-event question beats a process question.
5. The risks are boundedThe buyer can articulate what happens if it fails, and it is survivable.Agreement plus repeated delay — the signature of indecision.Shrink the bet: phased scope, pilot criteria, exit terms (Module 09).
The diagnostic that replaces "are they ready?"

Ask the buyer to explain the decision back to you: "If you were briefing your board next week, how would you describe what we're proposing and why?" If they can do it fluently, conditions 1–3 are met and you can hear it in their language. If they hesitate or reach for your slides, you have your answer and you have not spent a close to get it.

4. Closing techniques, graded

Not all closing techniques are equivalent. The dividing line is whether the technique helps the buyer make a decision or engineers agreement around one.

Table 2: Techniques graded by whether they support a defensible decision

TechniqueHow it worksGradeAssessment
Direct ask"Based on everything we've covered, I think this is the right move. Are you ready to go ahead?"UseClear, honest, easy to decline. Underused because it risks a no — which is exactly its value.
Summary closeRecap the agreed problem, the agreed value, the agreed terms, then ask.UseHelps the buyer see the whole decision at once. Only works if each element was genuinely agreed, so it doubles as a readiness check.
Recommendation close"I'd take option B, not C, and here's why — and here's what I'd be worried about."UseThe strongest move against indecision. Requires standing earned by having recommended against yourself before.
Next-step close"The next step is a security review — shall we get that scheduled for the 14th?"UseAppropriate mid-cycle. Progresses the process without a premature final ask.
Negative / take-away close"I'm not sure this is right for you — your data quality may not be ready."ConditionalLegitimate only when true. A genuine concern honestly raised. As a manufactured tactic it is manipulation and buyers recognise it.
Assumptive close"I'll get the paperwork over — shall we start on the 1st?"AvoidRequires the buyer to actively resist a stated fact to say no. Converts declining into a social confrontation.
Alternative close"Would you prefer to start in March or April?"AvoidPresents a false choice that omits the real option. Transparent to any experienced buyer and damages credibility.
Urgency / deadline close"This pricing ends Friday."Avoid unless realA genuine constraint stated neutrally is information. A fabricated one is a deceptive practice and makes indecision worse.
Puppy-dog / trial"Just try it for 30 days."ConditionalExcellent as genuine risk reduction with agreed success criteria. Manipulative when the trial is engineered to create switching costs the buyer was not told about.

The four "Use" techniques share a property: the buyer can say no without a fight. The three "Avoid" techniques share the opposite one. That is the whole grading rule, and it generalizes to any technique you encounter that is not on this list.

5. Incremental commitment and the mutual action plan

In a complex sale the final close is the least interesting one. What determines the outcome is a sequence of smaller commitments, each of which is a genuine test: a buyer who will not commit two hours of a technical evaluator's time will not commit £400k.

Table 3: The commitment ladder

CommitmentWhat it testsWhat refusal tells you
Time for a diagnostic conversationWhether the problem is real enough to spend an hour on.It is not yet a priority. Nurture, do not push.
Access to another stakeholderWhether your contact has standing and belief.Either they are a coach, not a champion, or they are not convinced. Ask which.
Sharing internal dataWhether they will invest effort in their own case.They expect you to build the case alone — a strong predictor of a deal that stalls.
Time from a technical evaluatorWhether the organization is spending real resource.The evaluation is notional. Forecast accordingly.
Agreement on success criteria in writingWhether they intend to judge this on evidence.They are avoiding a commitment they would be measured against.
A named date for a decisionWhether a decision process exists at all.Very often: there is no process, only interest.

The mutual action plan makes this ladder explicit. It is a shared, dated document listing every remaining step to a decision, with an owner on each — and, critically, obligations on both sides. A plan with only seller tasks is a project schedule; a plan with buyer tasks is a commitment.

The MAP failure mode

Most mutual action plans fail because they are written at the buyer rather than with them, sent as a PDF, and never referenced again. A working MAP is co-authored in the meeting, contains dates the buyer proposed, includes at least three buyer-owned tasks, and is reviewed at the top of every subsequent call. If your buyer has never edited it, it is not mutual — and its predictive value is zero.

6. Worked example: two closes, two aftermaths

Composite Case Study

Scenario: Two comparable £180k deals close in the same quarter, one via pressure and one via readiness. Both count identically in the quarter's bookings. Eighteen months later they have produced opposite results.

Takeaway: the two deals were indistinguishable in the quarterly report and differed by roughly 6.7× in realized value. This is the central argument against pressure closing, and it is economic rather than moral. It also explains why the behavior persists: the costs land outside the period in which the seller is measured. Module 41 treats the compensation design that makes Deal P rational for an individual seller.

The Brayford thread · Week 27 · signing four sites instead of eighteen

The close that reduced the deal

Readiness at week 25, scored honestly: problem owned (met — Priya's own figures), solution understood (met — she presented it to the capital committee without Cadence in the room), economics agreed (met — Elaine challenged the capacity-to-cash assumption and accepted the revised version), process known (met — committee date, papers deadline, IG dependency), risks bounded (not met).

The unbounded risk was the Marlow estate. Four acquired sites with no API, unresolved IG questions, and an implementation nobody could scope. The eighteen-site proposal required all of it.

The close, therefore, argued against the seller's own quarter: "I'd take the four Yorkshire homes, not all eighteen. The Marlow sites can't have the mechanism until their system is replaced, and I'd rather tell you that now than discover it in month four. That's a smaller decision and it's the one I'd make." Followed by the direct ask, and: "if the answer is no or not now, I'd genuinely rather know today."

£312k signed against a £480k proposal. The eighteen-site version had no IG clearance for Marlow and would have failed in implementation. At month 18 it expanded to £510k.

7. Weak versus improved close

Composite Case StudyScenario: Final proposal meeting. The buyer has been positive throughout. The seller needs to ask for the decision.

Weak execution (assumptive close, false choice, manufactured deadline)

Seller: "Great, I think we're all aligned. So what I'll do is get the contract over to you this afternoon — and did you want to start on the 1st or would the 15th work better? Oh, and just a heads-up, the pricing we discussed is Q3 pricing, so I'd need to get it signed by the 30th to protect it for you."
Buyer: "Let me talk to a few people and come back to you."

Why it fails: The assumptive close requires the buyer to resist a stated fact in order to say no, which turns declining into a confrontation — so they evade instead. The alternative close offers two start dates while omitting the real option, which any experienced buyer reads as a manipulation and quietly downgrades the seller for. The deadline is a manufactured constraint. And critically, none of it establishes readiness: "we're all aligned" is the seller's assessment, not the buyer's, and the vague deferral is what an unready buyer sounds like.

Improved execution (check readiness, summarize, recommend, ask, and make no easy)

Seller: "Before I ask you anything — if you were briefing your board next week, how would you describe what we're proposing and why?"
Buyer: "That we're losing about £230k a year to detection delay on line three, this cuts it to near zero within a quarter, and the payback is under two years."
Seller: "That's exactly it, and you said it better than I would. So: we agreed the problem in June, agreed the numbers with your finance partner in July, and security signed off last week. The one thing I'd still flag is that our reporting module is weaker than what you have now — you'd lose two of the custom views. I don't think that's decisive, but you should decide it rather than discover it.

My recommendation is that you go ahead with line three only, not all four lines, and expand once you've seen a quarter of data. That's a smaller decision and it's the one I'd make.

So — are you ready to go ahead, or is there something still in the way? And genuinely, if the answer is no or not now, I'd rather know today than chase this for three months."
Buyer: "The reporting thing I need to check with the plant manager. Give me until Thursday and if he's fine with it, we're going."

Why it succeeds: Readiness was tested before the ask, and the buyer's own articulation confirmed conditions 1–3. The summary referenced agreements that actually happened. The disclosed weakness cost something and bought credibility. The recommendation shrank the decision. The ask was direct and unambiguous. And the explicit permission to say no is what produced a specific, actionable answer with a date, instead of a polite deferral.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

The experienced failure is not pressure. It is de-risking the deal instead of the decision. A seller who has learned to shrink the bet starts shrinking whatever is easiest to shrink — the price, the term, the commitment — rather than the thing the buyer is actually afraid of.

A discount does not reduce the risk of choosing wrongly; it reduces the cost of having chosen wrongly by a few percent, which is not the fear. A shorter term does not help a sponsor whose exposure is reputational rather than financial. Both feel like risk reduction and neither addresses a buyer who is worried about being the only name on a project that fails.

The correction requires knowing which risk is live. Priya's risk was isolation, and the instruments that worked were a criteria workshop that put three other names on the recommendation and a written decision record — neither of which cost anything. Had the risk been technical, the pilot scope would have been right. Had it been financial, the term would have been.

Reducing the wrong risk is expensive and looks like generosity.

The mechanism: Risk-reduction instruments are not interchangeable. Sellers default to the commercial levers they control unilaterally, which are precisely the ones that address financial exposure — while most late-stage stalls in complex sales are driven by reputational and organisational exposure, which requires distributing the decision rather than discounting it.

8. Practice: commitment and next-step plan

Exercise goal: assess a live deal's readiness honestly, and build a mutual action plan the buyer would actually co-sign.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Take a deal you expect to close this quarter. Score all five readiness conditions as met / partial / not met, citing the specific evidence for each. "Partial" and "not met" are the useful answers.
  2. For every condition below "met," write the specific action that would close the gap and who must do it.
  3. Write the explain-it-back question you will use, and predict the buyer's answer. Then ask it and compare.
  4. List every commitment you have asked for so far and whether it was granted. Look for the pattern — a deal where you have never asked for anything is not advancing, it is drifting.
  5. Build the mutual action plan: every remaining step to a decision, with owner, date, and dependency. At least three tasks must be buyer-owned.
  6. Write your close: the summary, the disclosed weakness, the recommendation, the direct ask, and the explicit permission to decline.
  7. Write the language you will use to elicit an honest no, and mean it.

Evaluation rubric: Honest readiness scoring with cited evidence and at least one "not met" (25%); specific gap-closing actions with named owners (15%); explain-it-back question used and the answer compared to prediction (15%); mutual action plan with three or more buyer-owned tasks and buyer-proposed dates (20%); close containing a genuine disclosed weakness and a recommendation (15%); credible language for eliciting an honest no (10%).

Beginner variation: Score readiness on one deal and use the explain-it-back question. Most sellers discover that condition 4 — the process — is unknown on deals they are forecasting confidently.

Advanced variation: Score readiness across your whole pipeline and re-forecast on the results. Then compare your new forecast against your CRM stage-based forecast. The gap between them is a measure of how much of your pipeline is drifting rather than advancing, and it is usually a difficult but valuable number to show your manager.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why is the assumptive close ("I'll send the contract over — shall we start on the 1st?") graded avoid?

Because it makes declining a social confrontation. The buyer must actively resist a stated fact in order to say no, so the low-cost path is evasion — "let me talk to a few people" — which is exactly what you get. You have spent your close and learned nothing, and you have lost the ability to ask cleanly again. The four techniques worth using all share the opposite property: the buyer can decline without a fight, which is what makes their yes informative.

2. What is the fastest test of decision readiness?

Ask the buyer to explain the decision back to you as if briefing their board. If they can describe the problem, the change, and the economics fluently and in their own language, conditions 1–3 are met. If they hesitate or reach for your slides, they are not ready — and you have learned it without spending a close. It also frequently surfaces a reframing: buyers often lead with a different driver than the one you have been emphasizing, which tells you what the internal case actually rests on.

3. A mutual action plan contains eight steps, all owned by you. What is wrong?

It is not mutual, so it has no predictive value. A plan with only seller tasks is a project schedule the buyer has no stake in; its completion tells you nothing about whether a decision is coming. A working MAP is co-authored in the meeting, uses dates the buyer proposed, contains at least three buyer-owned tasks, and is reviewed at the top of every call. Buyer-owned tasks are the actual instrument: they are small commitments, and refusal or slippage on them is the earliest reliable signal that the deal is not real.

4. Why is an honest early "no" a good outcome?

Because the realistic alternative is not a yes — it is six months of polite non-response that consumes your capacity, corrupts your forecast, and ends in the same place. An early no returns your time to opportunities that can close, preserves the relationship for a later cycle when circumstances change, and often produces the most useful information you will get about why your offering did not fit. Sellers avoid asking for it because a no feels like a failure in the moment; the pipeline arithmetic says otherwise.

5. Two deals of the same size close in the same quarter, one under pressure. They count identically in the bookings report. What does that tell you about the report?

That bookings are a leading indicator with a long and invisible tail. A pressure-closed deal that churns at month 14, generates three times the support load, refuses to reference, and never expands can realize a small fraction of a comparable readiness-closed deal — the difference is easily several-fold. The report is not wrong, it is simply measuring one moment. The organizational consequence is that any compensation system paying fully at signature makes the worse deal rational for the individual seller, which is a design problem, not a character problem (see Module 41).

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Closing is the least mysterious part of selling once you stop treating it as a moment of extraction. Check readiness, summarize what was genuinely agreed, disclose what is weak, recommend what you would actually do, ask clearly, and make it easy to be told no.

  • Closing is helping a buyer reach a decision they can defend — including a decision not to proceed.
  • Check the five readiness conditions before asking. The process condition is the one most often unmet on confidently forecast deals.
  • Ask the buyer to explain the decision back. It is the fastest readiness test available and it costs nothing.
  • Use direct, summary, recommendation, and next-step closes. Avoid assumptive, alternative, and fabricated urgency.
  • The grading rule generalizes: can the buyer say no without a fight?
  • A mutual action plan with no buyer-owned tasks has no predictive value.
  • Ask for the honest no. The alternative is not a yes; it is a slow, expensive silence.

Immediate action: On your next late-stage call, ask the explain-it-back question before you ask for anything else. Then add one sentence to your close: "and if the answer is no or not now, I'd rather know today." Both take seconds and both change what you learn.

17Product-Led Sales

A login is not a signal. Realized value is.

Product-led sales fails when teams treat activity as intent and interrupt users who were succeeding without them. This module defines qualification signals that actually predict revenue, matches signals to motions, and designs assistance a user would thank you for.

1. What product-led sales actually changes

In a product-led motion the buyer evaluates by using rather than by being shown. That single change has three consequences that most teams under-appreciate.

Evidence replaces assertion. You no longer have to claim the product works — the user has already found out. This is enormously powerful and it removes most of the demo's purpose. A seller who arrives and pitches capabilities the user has been operating for three weeks is not merely unhelpful; they are signalling that nobody looked.

Attention becomes the scarce resource, and it is easy to destroy. A self-serve user who did not ask for a conversation experiences unsolicited outreach as an interruption to work they are already doing. The bar for contact is therefore higher than in outbound, not lower — you must be able to say something they could not learn from the product.

The qualification problem inverts. In traditional sales the hard part is finding people with the problem. In product-led sales they arrive by themselves, and the hard part is distinguishing the ones with a commercial opportunity from the many who are curious, evaluating, studying, or perfectly happy on the free tier forever. Most PLS programs fail here, by treating volume of activity as evidence of intent.

The failure mode is worth naming precisely: a sales team that interrupts successful self-serve users converts a working revenue channel into an annoyance, and the damage is invisible in the sales metrics because the users who quietly stop expanding do not appear in any report.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Define a product-qualified lead using signals validated against actual revenue rather than against intuition.

Distinguish usage, value, and constraint signals, and explain why only two of the three predict expansion.

Match a signal to the appropriate motion — automated, assisted, or full sales engagement.

Design outreach that offers something the product cannot tell the user.

Produce a PQL specification with thresholds, validation method, and suppression rules.

3. Defining a PQL that predicts revenue

The most common PQL definition in circulation is some version of "used the product a lot." It performs poorly because heavy usage is compatible with a user who will never pay, and light usage is compatible with an account about to standardize on you company-wide.

Table 1: Three classes of signal, and what each predicts

ClassExamplesPredicts?Why
Usage signalsLogins, sessions, time in app, features clicked.Weakly. Often not at all.Measures activity, not benefit. A student, a competitor, and a customer all generate logins. Correlates with engagement, not with willingness to pay.
Value signalsCompleted the core workflow end to end; produced an output used outside the tool; invited a colleague; connected a system of record; imported real production data.Strongly.Each indicates the user has received something they would miss. Real data and real colleagues mean real dependency.
Constraint signalsHit a seat, volume, or feature limit; requested an admin function; viewed pricing three times; started a security or SSO configuration; invited a user from a different department.Strongly, and with timing.The user has encountered the boundary of the free or lower tier. This is the moment a conversation is genuinely useful rather than interruptive.

A usable PQL definition combines a value signal with a constraint signal and an account-fit signal, because a single enthusiastic user at a company you cannot serve is not an opportunity. The combination form matters more than any individual threshold:

PQL = (value signal reached) AND (constraint signal within 14 days) AND (account matches ICP)

Validate against revenue, not against intuition

Every PQL definition must be tested the same way: take the last two quarters of converted accounts, and check what proportion of them would have fired your proposed definition, and how many accounts that fired it did not convert. A definition that catches 80% of converters with a 20% false-positive rate is useful. One that fires on 40% of all signups is a mailing list. Most teams never run this check, which is why so many PQL definitions are elaborate and useless.

4. Matching signals to motions

Not every signal justifies a human. Matching motion to signal strength protects both the user's attention and your team's capacity.

Table 2: The motion ladder

Signal strengthMotionWhat it looks likeWhy not more
Activity only (usage, no value signal)NothingProduct education in-app; no human contact.Contact here is pure interruption. This is the largest population and the one most often mis-handled.
Value reached, no constraintAutomated helpIn-product guidance to the next useful capability; a genuinely useful email with no ask.The user is succeeding. Interrupting a working experience to sell is the definitive PLS error.
Value + soft constraint (approaching a limit, viewed pricing)AssistedOne message from a real person offering something specific the product cannot supply — a configuration answer, a benchmark, an offer to review their setup.A full sales cycle is disproportionate. One useful message either lands or does not.
Value + hard constraint (hit a limit, needs SSO, admin controls, invoicing)Sales engagementA real conversation: procurement, security, rollout, commercial terms.This is where humans genuinely add value, because the remaining questions are organizational rather than product.
Multi-team spread inside a target accountAccount motionMap the org, find the economic buyer, consolidate scattered self-serve usage into an enterprise agreement.The highest-value PLS motion, and the one most often missed because each individual signal looks small.
The signal most teams miss entirely

Departmental spread. Three separate paid teams inside one large organization, each on a small plan, each invisible to the others — that is a consolidation opportunity worth more than any of the individual accounts, and it usually surfaces only if someone deliberately aggregates by email domain. Companies routinely discover six-figure enterprise deals sitting inside their self-serve base as a dozen unconnected subscriptions.

5. Designing assistance users would thank you for

The test for any product-triggered outreach is simple and demanding: could the user have learned this from the product? If yes, do not send it. Everything worth sending falls into a small number of categories.

Table 3: What is worth interrupting a user for

CategoryExampleWhy it is welcome
Configuration insight"You've set this up per-project. Teams your size usually regret that at about 40 projects because permissions get unmanageable — here's the alternative structure, and here's how to migrate if you want to."It is expertise about the product's use over time, which the product itself cannot convey at the moment of setup.
Peer benchmark"Teams with your data volume usually run the nightly sync rather than the hourly — it's the same result at a third of the load."Comparative knowledge you have and they cannot.
Constraint navigation"You hit the seat limit twice this week. Before you buy more seats, one option is a shared service account for the read-only users — that might be cheaper for you."Advice against your own short-term revenue is the strongest trust signal available.
Organizational help"Two other teams at your company are using this separately. Do you want me to introduce you — or would a single agreement be easier for procurement?"Information the user genuinely cannot see, and which usually solves a problem they were about to hit.
Honest limitation"You're trying to do X. We handle that badly and I don't want you to discover it in month three — here is what we do instead, and here is when it isn't enough."Prevents a churn event and buys credibility for everything else.

And the corresponding prohibition list: no "I noticed you signed up," no "just checking in," no "wanted to see if you had any questions," no calendar links before value has been offered, and no contacting a user whose only signal is activity. Each of these teaches the user that messages from your company are not worth opening, which is a durable and expensive lesson.

The Brayford thread · Month 6 · the expansion signal Cadence nearly missed

Two Brayford sites that had signed up on their own

At month 6, a weekly domain-aggregation job flagged something nobody in the account team knew: two Brayford homes outside the four-site pilot had independently signed up for Cadence's self-serve tier. Two home managers, a card each, £89 a month.

Neither had reached the account team. Both had crossed the value signal — a real roster connected, a published rota someone else viewed — and one had hit the seat limit twice in a fortnight, which is a hard constraint signal.

The wrong response was available and tempting: contact both managers and pitch expansion. It would have cut across an active enterprise motion, alarmed Priya, and put two junior managers in the position of having gone around their own procurement.

What actually happened was the account motion: the aggregation was raised with Priya as information she could not otherwise see — "two of your homes are paying us separately; one agreement is cheaper for you and simpler for your finance team." That conversation is what put the eighteen-site expansion on the table four months before the renewal.

7. Weak versus improved product-triggered outreach

Composite Case StudyScenario: A user at a 200-person company has connected their data warehouse, invited four colleagues, and hit the workspace limit twice this week.

Weak execution (noticed you signed up)

Email: "Hi Maya — I noticed you've been exploring [product] and wanted to reach out! I'd love to jump on a quick 15-minute call to understand your use case and show you what else the platform can do. Here's my calendar link. Also, are you the right person to speak to about your company's data strategy?"

Why it fails: "I noticed you've been exploring" tells Maya she is being watched, without offering anything in return for that discomfort. She has already found the use case — she connected a warehouse and invited four people — so "understand your use case" reads as not having looked. "Show you what else the platform can do" offers information the product already provides. The calendar link precedes any value. And "are you the right person" asks her to do the seller's qualification work, after implying she may not matter.

Improved execution (say something the product cannot)

Email: "Maya — you've hit the workspace limit twice this week, so before you buy more workspaces I want to flag something, because the obvious move is probably the wrong one for you.

You've got four people in one workspace with a warehouse connection. Teams that grow past that usually split by team rather than by project — splitting by project looks tidier at first and becomes unmanageable at around 40 projects because permissions stop mapping to anything. Migrating later is possible but tedious. Here's the structure most teams your size land on eventually: [link, no gate].

Two other things you can't see from inside the product. First, there are two other teams at your company already on paid plans — a marketing workspace and something in finance. If you're heading toward a company-wide rollout, one agreement is usually easier for your procurement than three renewals, and it's cheaper. Second, your warehouse connection is on the hourly sync; at your row volume the nightly gives the same result at about a third of the load.

Happy to just answer questions by email — no call needed unless you want one.

(If you'd rather I didn't reach out about usage, reply 'no' and I'll turn it off for your account.)"

Why it succeeds: Every paragraph contains information Maya could not get from the product: forward-looking structural advice, an organizational fact she cannot see, and a configuration optimization. The advice about workspace structure is offered before she spends money, including a suggestion that may reduce what she buys. No calendar link, no ask, and an explicit low-friction opt-out. If she replies, she will do so with a real question rather than out of politeness.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced product-led teams build a good PQL definition and then apply it without checking whether the account is already in another motion. The signal fires, the routing rule executes, and a self-serve user inside an active enterprise account receives an automated expansion nudge from a system that does not know a negotiation is in progress.

The damage is disproportionate to the message. A home manager who receives a pitch about seat limits while their operations director is negotiating a group agreement will, quite reasonably, forward it to their operations director — who now has evidence that the vendor's left hand does not know what its right is doing, at precisely the moment they are deciding whether to trust it with eighteen sites.

The failure is architectural, not judgmental: the signal is correct, the routing is correct, and the two systems have no shared view of the account.

Domain-level suppression is the fix, and it has to be a hard gate rather than a convention — if an account has an open enterprise opportunity, product signals from that domain route to the account owner as intelligence, never to the user as outreach.

The mechanism: Product signals are user-scoped and commercial relationships are account-scoped, so a system optimised on the first is structurally blind to the second. Without domain-level suppression, correct per-user logic produces incorrect per-account behaviour — and the error is most likely precisely where the account is most valuable.

8. Practice: the PQL signal specification

Exercise goal: produce a PQL definition validated against real conversion data, with motions matched to signal strength and suppression rules that protect the channel.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Define first value for your product — the specific action after which a user has received something they would miss. Use the Module 11 definition if you have already written it.
  2. List candidate signals in the three classes: usage, value, constraint. Be honest about which of your current signals are usage-only.
  3. Validate. Take the last two quarters of converted accounts. For each candidate signal, compute what proportion of converters fired it (recall) and what proportion of accounts that fired it converted (precision).
  4. Write the combination rule using value AND constraint AND fit. State every threshold and the time window.
  5. Match motions to signal strength using Table 2. Define what happens at each rung, including the rung where nothing happens.
  6. Write the assistance content for the assisted rung: three specific things you can tell a user that the product cannot.
  7. Write suppression rules: who is never contacted, how opt-out works across all channels and product lines, and what the maximum contact frequency is.
  8. Add domain aggregation: a weekly job identifying companies with multiple separate accounts or teams.
  9. Define the review cadence for re-validating the definition, since product changes invalidate signals.

Evaluation rubric: First value defined as a specific action with an external beneficiary (15%); signals correctly classified with usage signals honestly identified (15%); precision and recall computed against real conversions (30%); combination rule with thresholds and time window (15%); motions matched including a genuine do-nothing rung (10%); suppression rules with global permanent opt-out (10%); domain aggregation (5%).

Beginner variation: Do step 3 for one signal only — usually "logins." Computing its actual precision against converted accounts is, for most teams, the finding that ends the argument about whether activity is a qualification signal.

Advanced variation: Model the cost of the do-nothing rung. Estimate how many self-serve accounts convert without any human contact, and what would happen to that number if you contacted them. Most teams have never measured the counterfactual and therefore systematically over-attribute self-serve revenue to sales touches.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why is "logged in 10 times this week" a poor PQL signal?

Because it measures activity rather than benefit, and activity is generated equally by customers, students, competitors, and people who are stuck. Validated against real conversions it typically shows high recall and terrible precision — it catches most converters because most converters log in, while also catching almost everyone else. The signals that predict are value signals (the user got something they would miss: real data connected, output used by someone else, a colleague invited) combined with constraint signals (they have hit the boundary of what the current tier allows).

2. A user has reached first value and shows no constraint signal. What should sales do?

Nothing, or send genuinely useful automated guidance with no ask. The user is succeeding without you, and interrupting a working experience to sell is the defining product-led sales error — it converts a functioning revenue channel into an annoyance, and the damage shows up as quiet non-expansion rather than in any sales report. Wait for the constraint signal, which is the moment a conversation is actually useful because the user has encountered a boundary you can help with.

3. What is the highest-value signal most PLS teams never look for?

Departmental spread — multiple separate teams inside one organization, each on their own small plan, invisible to one another and to each other's procurement. Each individual account looks trivial, so nobody routes it, while together they represent a consolidation opportunity often worth more than any single new logo. Surfacing it requires nothing more than a weekly job aggregating accounts by email domain, which is why it is such a common and expensive omission.

4. What is the test for whether a product-triggered message is worth sending?

Could the user have learned this from the product? If yes, do not send it. What passes are things you know and they cannot see: how their configuration will behave at ten times the scale, how comparable teams have solved the same thing, an organizational fact such as other teams at their company already paying, or an honest limitation that will bite them in month three. Advice that reduces what they will spend is the strongest of all, because it is the clearest possible signal that the message was not about your quota.

5. Your rebuilt PQL definition routes 80% fewer leads to sales. How do you justify that internally?

With the precision and recall numbers and the revenue outcome. A definition that routes 600 accounts at 1.8% conversion produces less revenue than one routing 112 at 19% — and it consumes far more capacity doing it. Add the invisible cost: opt-out and unsubscribe rates from product-triggered outreach, which the loose definition inflates and which degrade the channel permanently. Also state honestly what the tighter definition misses (recall of 78% rather than 91%) and what separate motion covers those accounts, so nobody discovers the gap later and treats it as a mistake.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Product-led sales rewards restraint. The product does the demonstrating; the seller's job is to be the person who knows the thing the product cannot say, and to appear at the moment the user has hit a wall rather than at the moment a dashboard turned green.

  • Usage signals predict little. Value and constraint signals predict revenue, and only in combination with account fit.
  • Validate every definition against real converted accounts. Compute precision and recall or you are guessing.
  • Match motion to signal strength, and defend the rung where the correct action is nothing.
  • Only send what the product cannot say. Configuration insight, peer benchmarks, organizational facts, honest limits.
  • Advice that reduces what a user spends is the strongest trust signal available in this motion.
  • Aggregate by domain weekly. The largest deal in your self-serve base is usually already there in pieces.
  • Make opt-out global, permanent, and easy — a degraded channel does not recover quickly.

Immediate action: Take your current qualification signal and compute its precision against the last two quarters of converted accounts — what share of accounts that fired it actually converted. If the number is under 5%, you are not qualifying, you are broadcasting.

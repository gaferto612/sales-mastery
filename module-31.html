31Voice of Customer

Learn the market before you pitch it.

Turn interviews, lost deals, support conversations, and market evidence into a defensible view of customer progress, constraints, and alternatives.

45 min lesson + practiceEvidence-led professional curriculumLevel 3
In this module

  • 3. Map progress and forces of change
  • 4. Synthesize without inventing certainty

1. Build a research system

Separate four questions: who experiences the problem, in what circumstance, how they solve it now, and what evidence would justify change. Use interviews for depth, behavioral and CRM data for patterns, win/loss review for decision evidence, and surveys only after you know which questions deserve scale. Write a research brief: decision, participants, assumptions, interview guide, and stopping rule. Research is not a disguised sales call.

2. Run non-leading interviews

Ask for a recent concrete event rather than opinions about an imagined future. “Tell me about the last time…” produces a sequence you can inspect. Follow the trigger, search, alternatives, trade-offs, people, decision, implementation, and result. Avoid pitching or asking whether someone “would buy.”

Evidence ladder

Observed behavior and committed resources are stronger than remembered behavior; remembered behavior is stronger than stated intention; stated intention is stronger than a compliment.

3. Map progress and forces of change

Jobs-to-be-Done is a useful practitioner lens: buyers seek progress in a circumstance, with functional, social, and emotional dimensions. Treat it as a structured hypothesis, not a universal scientific law. Map the push of the current problem, pull of a new outcome, anxiety about change, and habit of the present solution. Verify each force with examples.

4. Synthesize without inventing certainty

Code notes by trigger, desired progress, current workaround, cost, risk, criterion, stakeholder, and exact language. Look for repeated mechanisms, preserve negative cases, and segment by meaningful differences in buying behavior. Tag every conclusion as observation, interpretation, or hypothesis; record sample limits and missing voices.

5. Run credible win/loss reviews

Interview customers and non-buyers soon after a decision. Ask how the shortlist formed, what changed, which criteria mattered, who influenced the choice, and what nearly reversed it. Do not reduce every loss to price or features; no decision, internal priority, implementation risk, and trust can dominate.

6. Artifact: an evidence repository

Create a research brief, five interview records, a coded evidence table, and a decision memo. State what changed, what remains uncertain, and which next experiment could disconfirm the conclusion.

7. Field guide: from question to decision

Customer research is useful only when it changes a decision. Begin with a decision such as which segment to prioritize, which workflow to investigate, why customers abandon an evaluation, or which assumption must be tested before building. A broad request to “learn about customers” creates attractive notes but weak action.

Choose evidence that fits the question

  • Interviews reveal language, sequence, context, trade-offs, and explanations. They do not estimate prevalence reliably.
  • Observation and product behavior show what happened, but often not why it happened.
  • CRM, support, implementation, and loss data reveal patterns only if definitions and recording quality are trustworthy.
  • Surveys can estimate a known pattern across a larger sample; they are poor tools for discovering what you have not yet learned to ask.
  • Experiments test a specific causal change when assignment, measurement, ethics, and sample size are adequate.

Triangulation means comparing different evidence types, not counting the same opinion three times. A customer interview, the account executive’s summary of that interview, and a CRM field copied from the summary are one evidence chain.

Interview a real event

Ask about a recent decision while details can still be reconstructed: “Take me back to the first moment this became worth addressing. What happened next?” Follow chronology. Ask what the participant did, who became involved, what information they sought, which alternatives survived, what resources they committed, and what almost stopped the decision. Distinguish direct experience from second-hand interpretation.

Weak versus improved

Weak: “Would an automated dashboard save you time?” This supplies the solution and invites politeness.
Improved: “Tell me about the last weekly report you prepared. Walk me through it from the first data request to the final review.” The second question produces a sequence that can be checked.

Do not correct, educate, or pitch during a research interview. Neutral follow-ups include “What happened then?”, “Can you show me?”, “How did you decide?”, and “What makes you say that?” If a participant asks for a product explanation, park it until the research portion is complete.

Synthesize without erasing disagreement

For every excerpt, preserve participant, date, context, and source. Code first at the descriptive level: trigger, workflow, workaround, consequence, stakeholder, criterion, alternative, risk, and exact language. Then state an interpretation separately. A useful finding has a traceable chain: evidence → pattern → interpretation → decision → uncertainty.

Negative cases matter. If seven customers report one mechanism and two do not, investigate the difference rather than deleting the two. The difference may reveal a segment boundary, a different maturity level, or a mistaken conclusion. Stop when new interviews no longer change the decision materially, not when the team has reached an arbitrary round number.

Common failure modes

  • Recruiting only enthusiastic customers and calling the result “the market.”
  • Asking future-intention questions and treating optimism as demand.
  • Letting the seller defend the product during a loss interview.
  • Converting vivid quotations into prevalence claims.
  • Combining users, buyers, administrators, and executives without recording whose job each statement describes.
  • Reporting themes without contradictory evidence, sample limits, or a decision owner.

8. Practice: customer evidence repository

Objective: produce a defensible customer insight that can guide a real commercial decision.

Composite educational scenario: A small software company believes independent accounting firms need automated client-document reminders. Five friendly customers praise the idea, but none has agreed to pilot it. The team must decide whether to build, narrow the segment, or stop.

  1. Write the decision and three assumptions that would change it.
  2. Recruit two recent adopters, two non-buyers, one churned customer, and one firm using a manual workaround.
  3. Write eight neutral questions anchored in recent behavior.
  4. Conduct or role-play two interviews and capture exact evidence.
  5. Create a table separating observations, interpretations, contradictions, and unanswered questions.
  6. Write a 200-word decision memo: proceed, narrow, test again, or stop.

Expected output: research brief, interview guide, six-row evidence table, and decision memo.

Evaluation criteria: decision relevance 25%; neutrality and behavioral specificity 25%; traceability 20%; treatment of negative cases 15%; honest limitations and next test 15%.

Advanced variation: compare the interview conclusion with CRM or product evidence and explain any disagreement.

Partial exemplar

“Four participants described chasing documents, but only two controlled the workflow and neither had budget authority. The evidence supports a workflow problem, not yet a purchasable market. We will test the office-manager segment and require a time commitment before building.”

9. Applied knowledge check

Scenario: Eight customers say they “love” a proposed reporting feature. Usage logs show that only one currently exports related data, and no one has agreed to test the feature. The strongest next action is to investigate the last real reporting workflow and ask for a concrete pilot commitment. Building immediately overweights compliments; surveying hundreds of users scales an undefined question.

Immediate action: Rewrite one hypothetical customer question into a recent-event question and use it in your next conversation.

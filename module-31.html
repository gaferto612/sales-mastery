31Voice of Customer

Compliments are not evidence.

Customer research fails when it collects opinions about an imagined future instead of behavior in a remembered past. This module builds an evidence ladder, non-leading interviewing, honest synthesis, and win/loss reviews that survive contact with what actually happened.

1. Why most customer research produces nothing

Most customer research fails in a way that is invisible to the people conducting it, because it produces a large volume of positive, articulate material. The failure is that almost none of it is evidence.

Three mechanisms do the damage. Leading questions supply the answer inside the question — "would an automated reminder save you time?" invites politeness, and politeness is free. Hypothetical framing asks people to predict their own future behavior, which humans do badly and optimistically. And recruiting enthusiasts produces a sample of people who already like you, whose views are then reported as "the market."

The result is a research programme that reliably confirms whatever the team already wanted to build, and a set of quotes that will be cited for two years in slide decks. Meanwhile the decision it was supposed to inform is made on instinct anyway, because nobody quite trusts the findings and nobody can say why.

The correction is structural rather than a matter of asking better questions in the moment. Start from a decision, ask about remembered behavior rather than imagined futures, recruit for variation rather than for enthusiasm, and preserve the cases that disagree with you. Research that cannot change a decision should not be commissioned; research that cannot be wrong has not been conducted.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Rank evidence types by strength and select a method that fits the decision at hand.

Convert a hypothetical question into a recent-event question and run a non-leading interview.

Code evidence so that observation, interpretation, and hypothesis remain distinguishable.

Run win/loss reviews that produce causes rather than the default answer of price.

Produce an evidence repository and a decision memo that states its own limits.

3. The evidence ladder and method selection

Not all customer evidence is equal, and the differences are large enough to reverse conclusions. The ladder below is the single most useful thing in this module.

Table 1: The evidence ladder, strongest first

RankEvidence typeWhy it ranks hereExample
1Committed resourcesMoney, headcount, or calendar time already allocated. The costliest signal to fake.They hired a contractor to do this manually last quarter.
2Observed behaviorWhat they actually did, seen or logged. No recall error.Product telemetry showing 40 exports a week.
3Remembered behaviorA specific past event they can reconstruct. Subject to recall bias but anchored in reality."Last month-end took us four days and two people stayed late."
4Stated intentionWhat they say they will do. Systematically optimistic; weakly predictive."We'd definitely use that."
5ComplimentPoliteness. Carries essentially no information about demand."That's a great idea."
The most common research error, stated precisely

Treating rung 4 or 5 as though it were rung 1. Ten people saying they "would definitely buy" is not evidence of demand; one person who paid a contractor to do the job manually is. If your research findings consist mostly of enthusiastic quotations, you have collected rung-5 material and should say so in the memo rather than presenting it as validation.

Table 2: Matching method to question

MethodAnswers wellAnswers badlyMain risk
Depth interviewsSequence, context, language, trade-offs, why.Prevalence — how common is this?Small non-random samples read as market truth.
Observation / telemetryWhat happened, at what frequency.Why it happened.Instrumenting what is easy rather than what matters.
CRM, support, and loss dataPatterns across many accounts.Anything, if field definitions are inconsistent.Garbage in. Rep-entered loss reasons are especially unreliable.
SurveysEstimating the prevalence of a pattern you already understand.Discovering what you did not know to ask.Scaling a badly-framed question to a large sample and calling it rigour.
ExperimentsWhether a specific change causes a specific effect.Anything requiring rich explanation.Underpowered designs; optional stopping (Module 03).
Win/loss interviewsHow a real decision was actually made.Anything, if conducted by the rep who lost the deal.Self-serving attribution on both sides.

Triangulation means comparing different evidence types, not counting the same opinion three times. A customer interview, the account executive's summary of that interview, and a CRM field copied from the summary are one piece of evidence wearing three hats — and teams routinely present that chain as three converging sources.

4. Interviewing a real event

The central technique is a substitution: replace every question about opinions or futures with a question about a specific recent event. The reason is mechanical — people cannot invent a coherent sequence on demand, so a chronological account is checkable in a way an opinion is not.

Table 3: The substitution, applied

Weak questionWhat is wrongStrong substitute
"Would an automated reminder save you time?"Supplies the solution and invites agreement."Walk me through the last time a document came in late. What happened first?"
"How important is reporting to you?"Everything is important in the abstract."When did you last build a report by hand? How long did it take and who asked for it?"
"What features would you want?"Asks the customer to do design, which they cannot."What did you do instead, the last time this came up?"
"Would you pay £500 a month for this?"Hypothetical willingness to pay, with no budget constraint attached."What have you spent on this problem so far — tools, contractors, overtime?"
"Do you like this?"Invites a compliment."Show me how you do it today." (Observation beats both.)

Four further rules govern a research interview. Follow chronology, not topics — trigger, search, alternatives, trade-offs, people involved, decision, implementation, result. Do not pitch, correct, or educate; if asked for a product explanation, park it until the research portion is over, or you have converted the interview into a sales call and contaminated the sample. Use neutral follow-ups: "what happened then?", "can you show me?", "how did you decide?", "what makes you say that?" And allow silence — the same habit as Module 28, and the same reason.

Recruit for variation, not for enthusiasm

A sample of happy customers tells you why happy customers are happy. Recruit deliberately across the range: recent adopters, non-buyers who chose someone else, non-buyers who chose nothing, a churned customer, and someone still using a manual workaround. The last two categories are the most informative and the least often interviewed, precisely because they are uncomfortable to approach.

5. Synthesis that preserves disagreement

Synthesis is where good fieldwork is most often destroyed. The failure is compression: nine hours of nuanced interviews become four confident themes, the contradictions vanish, and nobody can reconstruct what any conclusion rests on.

Table 4: The three-layer coding discipline

LayerWhat belongs thereExampleRule
ObservationWhat was said or done, with participant, date, and context."P4, 12 Mar: 'I chase documents for about two days every month-end.'"Verbatim where possible. Never merged with interpretation.
InterpretationWhat you think it means, stated separately and attributably."Document chasing appears to be an owner-less task that falls to whoever notices."Must cite the observations it rests on.
HypothesisWhat follows, and what would disconfirm it."If we automate chasing, the time returns to the office manager, not the partner. Disconfirmed if partners report doing this themselves."Must be testable, and must name the test.

Two disciplines protect the synthesis. Preserve negative cases. If seven participants report a mechanism and two do not, the two are the interesting ones — they usually mark a segment boundary, a maturity difference, or an error in the conclusion. Deleting them to produce a clean finding is the single most common act of self-deception in customer research.

And record whose job each statement describes. Combining users, buyers, administrators, and executives into one undifferentiated "customer" hides the fact that they want different things — which is exactly the information a segmentation exercise needs (Module 32).

Finally, stop when new interviews stop changing the decision, not at a round number. Five interviews that reverse your view are worth more than twenty that confirm it, and "we did twenty" is not a methodology.

6. Worked example: a win/loss review that changed the model

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A company's CRM shows 61% of losses attributed to price. Leadership is considering a 15% price reduction. An independent win/loss programme is run first: 24 post-decision interviews, conducted by someone other than the deal owner, within six weeks of the decision.

Table 5: Rep-reported reason versus buyer-reported reason

Rep-reported loss reasonCountWhat buyers actually saidImplication
Price15 of 24Only 4 described price as decisive. Of the other 11: 5 said implementation risk (they did not believe the timeline), 3 said the decision died internally with no owner, 2 said a required integration was missing, 1 had signed with an incumbent under an existing framework.A 15% price cut would have addressed 4 of 24 losses and destroyed margin on every win.
"Went with a competitor"53 of these had actually made no decision at all and were still using the status quo six months later."Lost to competitor" and "no decision" require completely different responses and were being conflated.
"Bad timing"32 described a specific missing stakeholder who was engaged too late.A process failure recorded as an act of God.
"No budget"1Confirmed.—
Aggregate finding: implementation-risk disbelief was the largest single cause (5 of 24) and appeared nowhere in the CRM. The chosen intervention was not a price cut but a phased-pilot default with pre-agreed success criteria and a published implementation track record. Win rate rose 9 points over two quarters at unchanged pricing.

Why the CRM was wrong: not dishonesty. "We lost on price" is the explanation a buyer offers because it is quick, impersonal, and requires no criticism of the seller — and it is the explanation a seller accepts because it attributes the loss to a factor outside their control. Both parties are comfortable, and the resulting data is systematically misleading in the same direction.

The Brayford thread · After the deal · the win/loss interview Cadence nearly skipped

What Brayford said that the CRM did not

Cadence's CRM recorded the Brayford outcome as "won — reduced scope, competitor: Stafford." An independent post-decision interview, conducted by someone who had never spoken to the account, produced a different account of the same events.

Priya's version: Stafford was never seriously in contention after week 6. The reporting criterion that Tom weighted at 25% was, in her words, "Tom's requirement, not the group's" — a technical evaluator's preference that had been recorded as an organisational criterion, and that nobody had challenged because challenging it would have meant challenging Tom.

The genuine near-loss was week 18, when Dev Osei's information-governance review surfaced. Priya's assessment was that had it appeared four weeks later, with papers already submitted to the capital committee, the deal would have been withdrawn rather than delayed.

None of that was in the CRM. The recorded competitor was the visible one; the actual threat was a governance function nobody had mapped, and the recorded decision criterion belonged to one person rather than to the organisation.

7. Weak versus improved research interview

Composite Case StudyScenario: Interviewing an accounting-firm office manager about document collection, to decide whether to build an automated reminder feature.

Weak execution (leading, hypothetical, and a sales call in disguise)

Researcher: "So we're thinking about building something that automatically chases clients for missing documents. Would that be useful for you?"
Manager: "Oh, definitely. That sounds great."
Researcher: "And how much time do you think that would save?"
Manager: "Loads. Hours, probably."
Researcher: "Would you pay for something like that?"
Manager: "I'd have to check, but I think so, yes."
[Recorded as: "Strong validation — customer confirmed pain, estimated hours saved, and willingness to pay."]

Why it fails: Every question supplied its own answer. The solution was described before the problem was explored, so the participant is now reasoning about the researcher's idea rather than describing her own work. "Hours, probably" is a rung-4 guess presented as a measurement. "I think so" about payment is rung 4 at best. And nothing was learned about who actually does this task, whether they control the budget, or what they do today — which are the three things that decide whether this is a market.

Improved execution (recent event, chronology, no solution mentioned)

Researcher: "I'm not going to describe anything we're building — I'd rather understand how you work. Can you take me back to the last time a client's documents came in late? When was that?"
Manager: "Last week, actually. Two of them."
Researcher: "Walk me through the first one. What was the first moment you knew?"
Manager: "The preparer flagged it on Tuesday — she couldn't start the return without the bank statements."
Researcher: "What happened then?"
Manager: "I emailed the client. No reply. Emailed again Thursday. Rang on Friday, got the office, they said they'd sent it — they hadn't, they'd sent it to the wrong person here."
Researcher: "How long did all that take you, across the week?"
Manager: "Maybe an hour and a half? But the real cost is the preparer sitting idle, and we bill her time."
Researcher: "Who else in the firm does this chasing?"
Manager: "Just me and one other person. The partners never touch it."
Researcher: "Has the firm ever spent money to make this better — a tool, a temp, anything?"
Manager: "We looked at a portal two years ago. The partners wouldn't sign it off — they didn't see the problem because it doesn't hit them."
Researcher: "That's really useful. What made you say that last part?"

Why it succeeds: No solution was mentioned, so nothing was validated by politeness. The chronology produced a checkable sequence and a specific cost the manager volunteered herself — idle billable time, which is worth far more than her own 90 minutes. And the last two questions surfaced the finding that decides the whole product: the person with the problem is not the person with the budget, and the budget holder does not experience the pain. That is a rung-1 fact — a previous purchase attempt that failed — and it would never have appeared in the weak version.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced teams commission win/loss programmes and then run them only on losses, which removes the control group and makes the findings uninterpretable.

If you interview twenty losses and find that implementation risk was decisive in seven, you have learned nothing until you know how often implementation risk was also raised in deals you won. A factor present in 35% of losses and 40% of wins is not a cause of losing; it is a feature of the category, and a team that reorganises its proof strategy around it has spent a quarter optimising noise.

Loss-only programmes also systematically over-sample articulate dissatisfaction. Buyers who chose you rarely volunteer what nearly went wrong; buyers who did not are unusually willing to explain, at length, and their explanations are more coherent in retrospect than the decision was at the time.

Interview the wins with the same independence and the same questions. The Brayford interview — a win — surfaced the two most important findings in the deal, and neither would have appeared in a loss-only programme.

The mechanism: A sample selected on the outcome cannot identify a cause of the outcome. Without wins in the sample there is no base rate, so any factor mentioned frequently in losses looks causal — and the more common a factor is in the category generally, the more decisive it will falsely appear.

8. Practice: the customer evidence repository

Exercise goal: produce a defensible customer insight that can guide a real commercial decision, with its limits stated.

Composite Case Study

Composite scenario: A small software company believes independent accounting firms need automated client-document reminders. Five friendly customers praise the idea; none has agreed to pilot it. The team must decide whether to build, narrow the segment, or stop.

  1. Write the decision and three assumptions that, if wrong, would change it.
  2. Recruit for variation: two recent adopters, two non-buyers, one churned customer, one firm using a manual workaround. Note who you could not reach — that is a sample limit.
  3. Write eight neutral questions anchored in recent behavior. No question may name a solution.
  4. Conduct or role-play two interviews and capture verbatim evidence with participant, date, and context.
  5. Code in three layers — observation, interpretation, hypothesis — keeping them visually separate.
  6. Record every contradiction and what it might indicate. Do not resolve them prematurely.
  7. Place each piece of evidence on the ladder (rungs 1–5) and note how much of your case rests on rungs 4–5.
  8. Write a 200-word decision memo: proceed, narrow, test again, or stop — with the next disconfirming test named.

Assumptions that would change it(1) The person chasing controls or influences the budget. (2) The time cost is large enough to fund a purchase. (3) Firms will change a client-facing process to adopt it.

Evaluation rubric: Decision relevance and assumptions stated up front (20%); recruitment for variation including non-buyers and churn (15%); neutrality and behavioral specificity of questions (20%); three-layer coding with traceable observations (15%); contradictions preserved and analysed (15%); honest evidence-ladder audit and a named next test (15%).

Beginner variation: Rewrite five of your existing research questions as recent-event questions and use them in one conversation. The difference in what you learn is immediate and obvious.

Advanced variation: Compare your interview conclusions against CRM or product telemetry for the same accounts and explain every disagreement. The disagreements are where the real learning is — and where you discover which of your instruments is lying to you.

Completing the customer evidence repository is not the same as producing a good one. Grade your own against these dimensions before you show it to anyone.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Eight customers say they "love" a proposed feature. Usage logs show one exports the relevant data. What do you do?

Treat the enthusiasm as rung-5 evidence — essentially uninformative — and investigate the behavior. Ask each of the eight to walk you through the last time they actually needed this, and ask for a concrete commitment: a paid pilot, two hours a week, a named person. Building immediately over-weights compliments; surveying hundreds of users scales a question you have not yet learned to ask. The one customer who exports data is worth more than the eight who admire the idea.

2. Why is "would you pay £500 a month for this?" a bad question?

Because hypothetical willingness to pay carries no budget constraint and no trade-off — the respondent is not giving anything up by saying yes. It sits at rung 4 and predicts poorly. The substitute is a rung-1 question about the past: "what have you already spent on this problem — tools, contractors, overtime, temporary staff?" Money already committed to a workaround is the strongest available demand signal, and it is a fact rather than a forecast.

3. Your CRM says 61% of losses are on price. Should you cut prices?

Not on that evidence. Rep-entered loss reasons default to price because it is comfortable for both parties — the buyer avoids criticising the seller, the seller attributes the loss to something outside their control. Independent post-decision interviews routinely find that price is decisive in a small minority, with implementation-risk disbelief, internal no-decision, missing stakeholders, and integration gaps making up the rest. Run the interviews before touching pricing; a cut that addresses four losses in twenty-four while destroying margin on every win is a very expensive mistake.

4. Seven interviewees describe a mechanism and two do not. What do you do with the two?

Investigate them, never delete them. Negative cases usually mark something structural — a segment boundary, a different maturity level, a different operating model — or they reveal that the conclusion is wrong. In the worked example, the single firm that had abandoned a comparable tool because clients would not adopt it was the most valuable data point in the study, and it directly disconfirmed a load-bearing assumption. Deleting inconvenient cases to produce a clean theme is the most common act of self-deception in customer research.

5. What does genuine triangulation require?

Different types of evidence, not multiple retellings of the same one. A customer interview, the account executive's summary of that interview, and a CRM field copied from the summary form a single evidence chain presented as three sources — and teams do this constantly without noticing. Real triangulation compares an interview against telemetry against committed spend, and treats the disagreements as the finding rather than as noise to be reconciled.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Customer research is only useful when it can change a decision and can turn out to be wrong. Everything in this module serves those two conditions: start from the decision, ask about the past, recruit for variation, keep the contradictions, and state your limits.

  • Rank evidence: committed resources, observed behavior, remembered behavior, stated intention, compliment.
  • Substitute recent-event questions for every hypothetical or opinion question.
  • Never describe your solution during a research interview.
  • Recruit non-buyers, churned customers, and workaround users — the uncomfortable categories are the informative ones.
  • Code observation, interpretation, and hypothesis separately, and keep them traceable.
  • Preserve negative cases. They mark segment boundaries or errors.
  • Run win/loss independently of the deal owner, or the answer will be price.
  • Stop when new interviews stop changing the decision, not at a round number.

Immediate action: Take the question you ask customers most often and rewrite it as a recent-event question. Use it in your next conversation and note how much longer the answer is — length is a rough proxy for how much of it is reconstruction rather than opinion.

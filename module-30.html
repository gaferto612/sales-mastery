30Contested Lens · Level 6

Status and risk are real. The explanations are not evidence.

Buyers do weigh reputation, coalition, and personal exposure alongside financial return. Evolutionary storytelling adds nothing to that observation except unfalsifiable confidence — and a short route to stereotyping. This module keeps the observation and discards the narrative.

1. Why this lens needs the tightest guardrails

Evolutionary psychology is a legitimate research field with contested internal debates — the modularity thesis, the reliability of reverse-engineering ancestral environments, and the testability of many adaptationist hypotheses are all actively argued in the primary literature. Its popular sales adaptation is a different thing entirely: a set of just-so stories used to explain buyer behavior after the fact.

The characteristic move is to observe something ordinary — a buyer resisted a change that threatened their department's headcount — and explain it with an ancestral narrative about coalition and resource defence. The narrative adds no predictive power. It cannot be disconfirmed. And it converts a specific, checkable organizational fact into a claim about human nature, which is a downgrade disguised as depth.

It also carries the worst downside risk of any lens in this course. Evolutionary framings in commercial settings slide easily into claims about groups — about how "men" or "women" or "senior people" or particular cultures behave. Those claims are unsupported, they are discriminatory in effect, and in most jurisdictions acting on them in a hiring or customer-treatment decision is unlawful.

So the module keeps a narrow, useful observation: organizational decisions have social and personal stakes alongside financial ones, and a seller who ignores them will lose deals they cannot explain. Everything about ancestral environments is discarded, because it is not needed and it does harm.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Distinguish an observable organizational fact from an evolutionary explanation of it, and explain why only the first is usable.

Identify status, reputation, and coalition dynamics from evidence rather than from theory.

Surface personal risk respectfully, and reduce it without exploiting it.

State the guardrails that prevent this lens from becoming stereotyping.

Produce an organizational-risk hypothesis map with disconfirming tests.

3. Status and reputation as organizational facts

Everything worth taking from this lens can be stated without any theory: people in organizations are evaluated by other people, and decisions affect how they are evaluated. That is a plain description of how institutions work, it is observable, and it generates checkable hypotheses.

Table 1: Observation, unfalsifiable explanation, and usable hypothesis

ObservationEvolutionary framing (unusable)Organizational hypothesis (usable)How to test it
The IT director resists a change that would reduce his team's remit."Coalition defence and resource protection.""His headcount and budget case for next year may depend on owning this function."Ask what he is accountable for next year, and what the change would do to it. He will usually just tell you.
The sponsor wants a competitor's brand on the shortlist even though they are a poor fit."Costly signalling of status.""Being able to show a recognisable name may make the recommendation easier to defend upward."Ask what the paper needs to contain to be approved without argument.
A junior analyst's objections carry unusual weight."Dominance hierarchy inversion.""She may have been right about something expensive before, or she may be someone's trusted proxy."Ask the champion how decisions like this usually get made and who is listened to.
Nobody will be the first to endorse the project."Risk-averse conformity.""Whoever moves first carries the blame if it fails, and there is no shared criterion yet."Propose written shared criteria so the decision is collective and defensible (Module 36).
The test for whether an explanation is doing work

Ask: what would I do differently if this explanation were false? "Coalition defence" produces no different action from "his headcount case depends on it" — except that the second is checkable in one question and the first is not. An explanation that changes no action and cannot be tested is decoration, and decoration in a deal review is expensive because it feels like understanding.

4. Personal risk: the variable nobody puts in the business case

The genuinely useful contribution of this lens is a reminder that every organizational decision has a personal risk column that never appears in the model. A business case may show a 3.2-year payback and a strong NPV, and the person approving it is separately computing what happens to them if it fails.

Table 2: The personal risk column

Question the buyer is silently askingWhat it means commerciallyHow to reduce it legitimately
"If this fails, whose name is on it?"The sponsor bears asymmetric downside — a failure is attributable, a success is shared.Shared written criteria; a documented process; alternatives considered and recorded. Give them the artifact that makes the decision defensible.
"Will I look naive for believing a vendor?"Vendor claims are discounted partly because accepting them is reputationally risky.Provide checkable evidence, references who will speak candidly, and volunteered limitations. Make believing you a defensible act.
"Does this reduce my team's scope?"A change that shrinks someone's remit will be resisted regardless of its merits.Surface it early and honestly. Sometimes the answer is yes, and the honest conversation is better than the silent block.
"Do I have to admit the current process was my decision?"Replacing something the buyer chose requires them to revise their own earlier judgment publicly.Frame around what has changed since — new volume, new regulation, new scale — so the earlier decision was right then.
"What happens if I'm the only one who wanted this?"Isolation is the largest personal risk in consensus decisions.Broaden the buying group; convert an individual advocacy into a group decision with shared criteria.
The ethical line, stated precisely

You may surface and reduce personal risk. You may not manufacture or exploit it. "Here's the documentation that would let you defend this decision if it goes wrong" is legitimate and useful. "Your competitors are doing this — imagine explaining to your board why you didn't" is manufactured career threat, and it fails both the compression test (Module 02) and the disclosure test (Module 12). The distinction is whether you are reducing a risk that exists or creating one that does not.

5. The guardrails

This lens requires more explicit constraints than any other in the course, because the failure mode is not merely ineffective — it is discriminatory.

Table 3: The five guardrails

GuardrailRuleWhy
1. Never generalize to a groupNo claims about how men, women, age groups, nationalities, or cultures behave in buying situations.Unsupported, discriminatory in effect, and in hiring or customer-treatment decisions frequently unlawful.
2. Describe the situation, not the person"This decision reduces his team's remit" not "he's territorial."The first is checkable and changeable; the second is a character judgment that stops inquiry.
3. Every hypothesis needs a testIf you cannot say what would disconfirm it, do not act on it.This is the whole basis of Level 6, and this lens generates untestable stories faster than any other.
4. Ask before inferringMost of these hypotheses can be checked with one direct, respectful question.Buyers describe their own incentives readily when asked plainly. Inference is slower and worse.
5. Reduce, never exploitPersonal risk may be surfaced and lowered, never manufactured or leveraged.Exploiting career fear is manipulation, and it is remembered long after the deal.

A practical consequence of guardrail 4 is worth stating: almost everything this lens speculates about can be obtained by asking. "What are you accountable for next year?" "What would this change about your team's scope?" "What does the paper need to contain to get approved without an argument?" These are ordinary, respectful questions that produce better answers than any amount of theorizing — and asking them is also how you find out you were wrong.

6. Worked example: an organizational risk map

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A deal has strong economics, an enthusiastic sponsor, and has not moved in three months. The seller maps organizational risk rather than adding urgency.

Table 4: Hypotheses, tests, and what was actually true

StakeholderObservableHypothesis (situational)TestResult
Ops Director (sponsor)Enthusiastic; has not escalated in eight weeks."Being the only advocate is a personal risk — if it fails, it is attributably his.""Who else would need to be visibly behind this for it to feel like a shared decision rather than yours?"Confirmed. He had championed a failed project two years earlier and was not willing to be the sole name again.
IT DirectorPolite, unresponsive, has raised three technical objections that were each answered."The change moves a function his team currently owns; his headcount case may depend on it.""What are you accountable for next year, and what would this change about that?"Confirmed and different from expected. Not headcount — he had committed to a platform consolidation to his own board and this looked like a contradiction of it.
Finance Business PartnerAsked for the model three times, never commented."She may be uncomfortable endorsing a vendor's model.""Would it be more useful if you rebuilt this in your own template with your own assumptions? I'll give you every input."Confirmed. She rebuilt it, arrived at a slightly worse but defensible number, and then advocated for it — because it was now hers.
Head of ComplianceNever engaged; not on anyone's list."Unmapped veto holder."Asked the sponsor who signs off systems touching personnel data.Confirmed. Would have blocked at signature. Engaged in week four instead of week forty.

Actions taken: a joint criteria workshop that converted the sponsor's individual advocacy into a group decision with four names on it; a direct conversation with the IT Director about how this fits the consolidation commitment, which produced a scope change rather than a fight; handing the model to Finance to rebuild; and engaging compliance thirty-six weeks earlier than would otherwise have happened.

The methodological point: every hypothesis was situational and testable, every test was a single respectful question, and one of the four was wrong in an important way — the IT Director's issue was a public commitment, not headcount. An evolutionary framing would have labelled him "territorial," which would have been unfalsifiable, slightly insulting, and would have produced entirely the wrong response.

7. Weak versus improved handling of personal exposure

Composite Case StudyScenario: The sponsor has been enthusiastic for months and has not taken the proposal upward.

Weak execution (manufacture the career threat)

Seller: "I get it — these things are political. But honestly, the risk of doing nothing is bigger than the risk of doing this. Two of your competitors have already moved on it. If this comes up at your board in six months and you haven't done anything, that's a harder conversation than any of this. Sometimes you've just got to back yourself, right?"

Why it fails: It manufactures a career threat rather than reducing one — the competitor claim is unevidenced, and "a harder conversation at your board" is an invented consequence being used as leverage. "Back yourself" reframes a legitimate structural problem (he would be the sole name on a risky decision) as a personal failing of nerve, which is both inaccurate and insulting. And it does nothing about the actual obstacle, so the deal remains exactly where it was, with the relationship slightly worse.

Improved execution (name the structural risk, then reduce it)

Seller: "Can I check something that might be off, and tell me if it is. My read is that this hasn't moved because right now it's your recommendation and nobody else's — so if it goes well it's a team success and if it goes badly it's your name on it. That's an unfair distribution and I don't think it's about the case at all.

If that's roughly right, I'd rather fix that than keep sending you evidence you don't need.

Two things I could do. One, run a criteria workshop with you, the IT Director, and Finance, where they set the weightings — then the recommendation comes out of a shared process rather than from you. Two, put together the documentation of what was considered and why, including the two alternatives and the case for doing nothing, so that if it ever is questioned there's a defensible record rather than a memory.

And if the honest answer is that this isn't the right year for you to spend political capital on it, that's a legitimate answer and I'd rather you told me."
Sponsor: "…The workshop. And yes — I backed something two years ago that didn't land, and I'm not doing that alone again."

Why it succeeds: The seller named a structural asymmetry rather than a personal deficiency, and offered the buyer an easy way to correct them if wrong. Both proposed actions reduce real risk — shared criteria distribute the decision, documentation makes it defensible — rather than adding invented risk. And offering the exit made it safe to disclose the actual history, which was the whole obstacle and had been invisible for three months.

8. Practice: the organizational-risk hypothesis map

Exercise goal: map the personal and organizational stakes in a live deal as testable situational hypotheses, and reduce the risks you find.

Applies to your real situation
  1. List every stakeholder in a live deal and what you have observed them do — actions and dates, not impressions.
  2. For each, write one situational hypothesis about what this decision changes for them: scope, budget, workload, exposure, a prior commitment. Describe the situation, never the person.
  3. Write the one question that would test each hypothesis. It must be respectful, direct, and answerable in a sentence.
  4. Identify who carries asymmetric personal risk — usually the sponsor. State exactly what they are exposed to.
  5. Design two risk reductions for that person: something that distributes the decision, and something that documents it.
  6. Check yourself against the guardrails. Have you generalized to any group? Described a person rather than a situation? Written a hypothesis with no test? Anything that fails, rewrite or delete.
  7. Ask the questions and record which hypotheses were wrong. Expect at least one.
  8. Write the sentence you will not say — the manufactured-threat version you were tempted by. Naming it makes it easier to notice next time.

Evaluation rubric: Stakeholders listed with observed actions and dates (15%); every hypothesis situational rather than dispositional (25%); a respectful one-sentence test for each (20%); asymmetric personal risk identified with two genuine reductions (20%); guardrail check performed with at least one rewrite or deletion (10%); wrong hypotheses recorded honestly (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 4 and 5 only. Identifying who carries the asymmetric risk in your largest deal, and designing one way to distribute it and one way to document it, is most of the practical value.

Advanced variation: Audit your team's language in deal reviews for dispositional descriptions — "he's territorial," "she's a blocker," "they're just risk-averse." Each one is an untested character judgment standing where a testable situational hypothesis should be. Rewriting them as situations, with a question attached, usually produces several actions nobody had thought of.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. What is wrong with explaining a buyer's resistance as "coalition defence"?

It changes nothing you would do and cannot be disconfirmed. The situational version — "his budget or headcount case for next year may depend on owning this function" — produces the same suspicion but is checkable in a single question, and it is frequently wrong in an informative way. An explanation that alters no action and admits no test is decoration, and it is expensive in a deal review because it produces the feeling of understanding without any of the substance.

2. What is the difference between surfacing and exploiting personal risk?

Whether the risk already exists. Surfacing means naming a real asymmetry — the sponsor carries attributable blame if this fails — and then reducing it through shared criteria, a documented decision record, or a broader buying group. Exploiting means manufacturing a career threat that does not exist: "imagine explaining to your board why you didn't do this." The first makes a genuine obstacle addressable; the second adds pressure to someone already exposed, fails both the compression and disclosure tests, and is remembered long after the deal.

3. Why is "describe the situation, not the person" the load-bearing guardrail?

Because situations are checkable and changeable while dispositions are neither. "He's territorial" is a character judgment that ends inquiry and suggests no action; "this decision moves a function his team currently owns" is a hypothesis you can test with one question and address with a scope change. The dispositional version is also where this lens slides into stereotyping — once you are describing what kind of person someone is, generalizing to what kind of people behave that way is a very short step.

4. Your sponsor has been enthusiastic for months and will not escalate. What is the likely structural cause?

Asymmetric personal exposure: it is currently their recommendation and nobody else's, so a success is shared and a failure is attributably theirs. That is a structural problem, not a failure of nerve, and more evidence will not solve it because the case was never the obstacle. The two reductions that work are distributing the decision — a criteria workshop where other stakeholders set the weightings, so the recommendation emerges from a shared process — and documenting it, so there is a defensible record of what was considered rather than a memory.

5. Why does this lens carry more downside risk than the others in Level 6?

Because its failure mode is discriminatory rather than merely ineffective. Evolutionary framings in commercial settings slide readily into claims about how men, women, older buyers, or particular cultures behave — claims that are unsupported, harmful, and in hiring or customer-treatment decisions frequently unlawful. NLP wastes your time; group-level evolutionary storytelling can produce decisions that are both wrong and illegal. Hence the first guardrail is absolute rather than advisory.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

The residue of this lens is one sentence long: organizational decisions have a personal risk column that never appears in the business case, and a seller who ignores it will lose deals with excellent economics and no explanation. Everything else — the ancestral narratives, the dispositional labels, the group generalizations — should be discarded.

  • Keep the observation that decisions have social and personal stakes. Discard the evolutionary explanation.
  • Test every explanation: what would you do differently if it were false?
  • Describe situations, never persons. Never generalize to a group.
  • Ask. Almost everything this lens speculates about is available in one respectful question.
  • Identify who carries asymmetric exposure — usually the sponsor.
  • Reduce risk by distributing the decision and documenting it.
  • Surface personal risk; never manufacture or exploit it.

Immediate action: Take your most stalled deal with strong economics and ask who carries the asymmetric risk if it fails. Then design one way to distribute that decision — a criteria workshop, a second named advocate — and propose it this week. Deals that stall despite good numbers are usually stalling on this.

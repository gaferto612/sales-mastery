30Evolutionary Psychology

Evolutionary claims, used carefully.

Evolutionary perspectives can generate hypotheses about status, affiliation, and risk, but they do not explain every purchase. Learn to separate established decision research from speculative ancestral stories.

1. Academic foundation and limits

Evolutionary psychology asks whether recurring selection pressures contributed to psychological tendencies. Some accounts propose many specialized mental modules; that “massive modularity” claim remains debated. Modern organizational behavior is also shaped by culture, institutions, incentives, expertise, and immediate context.

Use an evolutionary explanation as a hypothesis generator, never as proof of what a particular buyer thinks. A useful hypothesis must produce an observable prediction and compete with simpler alternatives.

Three tests

Is the claim supported beyond a compelling ancestral story? Does it generalize across cultures and situations? What present-day evidence could prove it wrong?

2. Status, role, and accountability

Status can matter, but “status” may mean formal authority, expert reputation, access, workload, or protection from blame. Do not assume that a champion wants promotion or that a technical critic is defending territory.

  • Ask what the stakeholder is accountable for.
  • Identify which outcomes improve or damage their credibility.
  • Separate personal consequences from organizational consequences.
  • Test whether an objection remains after its technical evidence is addressed.

3. Loss aversion without fear selling

Research often finds that losses and gains are valued asymmetrically, but a 2024 meta-analysis also documents substantial variation. “Losses count twice” is not a universal constant, and it does not prove an evolutionary story.

Present the cost of action, cost of inaction, probability, time horizon, and uncertainty using the same quality of evidence. Fear-based claims about jobs, audits, or competitive collapse require particularly strong substantiation.

Balanced decision frame

“Here is the expected upside, the implementation downside, and the downside of waiting. These ranges depend on three assumptions. Which assumption should we test first?”

4. Group boundaries and trust

People sometimes favor groups they identify with, but buyers are not biologically compelled to exploit outsiders. Trust is better built through competence, reliability, honest disclosure, and aligned incentives than through a manufactured shared enemy.

Learn the customer's language to understand their work, not to impersonate an insider. Invite technical critics into governance and testing because their expertise improves the decision—not to neutralize “saboteurs.”

5. Composite Practice Scenarios

Composite Scenario 1: The technical critic

A Director of IT challenges an AI platform. The seller avoids assuming status threat, records the security requirements, and asks the director to co-design pass/fail criteria. The criticism may reveal a real product gap.

Composite Scenario 2: The cautious CFO

A CFO rejects an efficiency case because migration risk is larger than the modeled gain. The seller presents a range, stages the investment, and defines stop criteria instead of escalating fear.

Composite Scenario 3: Build versus buy

An engineering team prefers an internal build. The seller compares total cost, opportunity cost, control, security, maintenance, and reversibility. Respecting internal expertise produces a more credible evaluation.

6. Tactical application: competing hypotheses

When a stakeholder resists, write at least three explanations before responding: the evidence is weak; another priority dominates; or personal/organizational incentives conflict. Ask a question that distinguishes them.

Example

“Is the concern primarily that the compliance estimate is wrong, that the work has another owner, or that solving it now would displace a higher-priority project?”

7. Troubleshooting pitfalls

  • Just-so stories: A plausible ancestral narrative is not empirical evidence.
  • Universalizing: Culture, organization, role, and situation can change behavior.
  • Moral licensing: Describing conduct as evolved does not make manipulation ethical.
  • Mind reading: Verify motives; do not assign hidden primal algorithms to an individual.

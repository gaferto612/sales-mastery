03Digital Conversion

Fix the constrained stage before you buy more traffic.

Almost every underperforming online business is buying traffic to compensate for a leak it has not diagnosed. This module teaches funnel arithmetic, constraint identification, honest experiment design, and the unit economics that decide whether a channel is a business or a subsidy.

1. The traffic reflex and why it is expensive

When online revenue is below plan, the default response in most organizations is to buy more traffic. It is the most available lever, it produces visible activity within hours, and it requires no argument with anyone about the product. It is also, in the majority of cases, the most expensive way to buy the missing revenue.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A funnel is a product of rates, so improving the constrained stage multiplies through every stage below it, while adding volume at the top multiplies through the same leak. If your checkout converts at 40% against an achievable 55%, every additional visitor you buy is purchased at a 27% discount to its potential value. Baymard Institute — whose estimates come from sixteen years of large-scale checkout usability testing across more than 300 top-grossing sites — puts the average recoverable gain from checkout redesign alone at roughly 35%. There is no traffic channel that delivers a 35% cost reduction on demand.

The reason organizations reach for traffic anyway is organizational, not analytical: traffic is somebody's job, checkout is somebody else's, and no one owns the funnel as a single object. The first contribution of a good commercial thinker is usually not a clever growth tactic. It is a shared diagram with real numbers on it that forces the conversation to happen.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Build a funnel model with explicit denominators and identify the constrained stage by expected value, not by intuition.

Distinguish leaks caused by traffic quality from leaks caused by experience friction, using segmented evidence.

Diagnose a checkout or signup flow against the documented abandonment causes.

Compare paid, owned, and earned channels on contribution margin and payback rather than on volume.

Design an experiment with a pre-registered primary metric, a sample-size floor, and a stopping rule.

3. Funnel arithmetic and constraint identification

A funnel is not a picture. It is a chain of conditional rates, and the discipline that makes it useful is naming the denominator at every stage. Most disputes about funnel performance are actually disputes about denominators that nobody wrote down.

Table 1: A funnel with explicit denominators and improvement leverage

To find the constraint, do not look for the lowest rate. Look for the largest gap between the current rate and a defensible achievable rate for your context, weighted by the traffic passing through it. A 61% checkout completion against an achievable 75% on 40,000 checkouts is worth vastly more than lifting a 2.1% click-through to 2.4%.

The one calculation that reorders most roadmaps

For each stage compute Expected value = (achievable rate − current rate) × volume through the stage × downstream conversion × margin per conversion. Then divide by the estimated effort. Ranking by this ratio routinely inverts a roadmap that was ordered by which team shouted loudest — and it gives you a defensible answer when someone asks why checkout is being prioritized over a homepage redesign.

4. Where conversion actually leaks: the evidence

You do not have to guess where digital funnels fail. The failure modes are well documented and remarkably stable across studies.

Documented cart abandonment averages around 70% across studies — roughly 80% on mobile and 66% on desktop. That mobile–desktop gap is the single most reliable early diagnostic available: if your gap is much wider than that, the problem is almost certainly form burden and input friction on small screens rather than intent.

Boundary condition on every benchmark in this section

These are class V/R aggregates. They differ by category — documented abandonment runs from roughly 53% in pet supplies to 82% in beauty — and by traffic mix. A site running heavy discovery-driven social traffic will abandon more than one running branded search, and neither is "broken." Benchmarks tell you which hypothesis to test first. Your own segmented data tells you whether it was right.

5. Channel economics: paid, owned, and earned

Channels are usually compared on volume and cost per click, which are the two least decision-relevant numbers available. Compare them on contribution after variable cost and on payback period, because those determine whether a channel funds itself or is quietly subsidised by another.

Table 3: Channel comparison on the terms that matter

Retention as a channelCost of service and success motion.The most strongly compounding of all.Under-measured because it appears as absent churn rather than as visible revenue.Every point of repeat rate raises the price you can afford to pay for a new customer.

The decision rule follows directly: a channel is viable when contribution margin per customer exceeds fully-loaded acquisition cost within a payback period your cash position can survive. Not when ROAS looks good — ROAS uses revenue, not margin, and revenue-based targets have destroyed more consumer brands than any competitor. For subscription businesses the equivalent instruments are CAC payback and net revenue retention; 2025 SaaS medians put CAC payback near 16 months with top quartile at 6 or better, though those medians come from survivor-biased vendor surveys and are useless as targets for a business with different gross margins.

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A direct-to-consumer ergonomic furniture brand has 120,000 monthly sessions, a 1.2% overall conversion rate, and a paid CPC that rose 35% year on year. The growth team's proposal is to expand into two new paid channels. The analyst runs a constraint diagnosis first.

Decision: The cart-to-checkout constraint is worth roughly 2.7× the paid expansion, costs almost nothing, and improves the return on every future media pound. Sequence: fix the shipping disclosure, then reduce form fields, then build the post-purchase sequence, then expand paid channels against the repaired funnel.

What made the diagnosis credible: not the size of the numbers, but the evidence column. Two of the five targets were marked weak or moderate and were explicitly deprioritized despite attractive contribution figures. A funnel model where every stage is confidently achievable is a wish list.

The Brayford thread · Cadence's own funnel · the year before Brayford

Why Cadence had a self-serve tier at all

Brayford arrived through outbound, but Cadence's single-site tier is what made the outbound affordable, and the connection is worth tracing.

Cadence's self-serve funnel converted visitors to trials at 3.1% and trials to paid at 22%. The constrained stage was neither. It was trial-to-first-value: 34% of trials connected a real staff roster in the first session, and those converted at 51%; the other 66% converted at 6%.

The expected-value calculation put a guided first-session connection step above every other candidate on the roadmap, including a pricing-page redesign with a larger headline number and much weaker evidence. Raising first-session connection from 34% to 52% was worth more than doubling paid traffic, at a fraction of the cost.

The relevance to Brayford is indirect and real: self-serve gross margin funded an outbound motion that could afford to spend three weeks researching one eighteen-site operator. Funnel work is not a separate discipline from enterprise selling — it is what pays for it.

7. Weak versus improved experiment design

Composite Case StudyScenario: The team wants to test surfacing shipping cost earlier.

Weak execution (a test that cannot produce knowledge)

Growth lead: "Let's ship a new cart page with the shipping estimator, a trust-badge row, a countdown timer, and a redesigned button, and see if conversion goes up. We'll check on Monday and roll it out if it's winning."

Why it fails: Four changes in one variant means a positive result identifies nothing — you cannot tell which change caused it, or whether one change is winning while another loses. "Check on Monday and roll out if winning" is optional stopping, which inflates false positives dramatically; with peeking, a coin-flip test wins about a third of the time. No primary metric was pre-registered, so any of six numbers can be declared the winner after the fact. And the countdown timer is a manufactured scarcity claim that fails the Module 02 compression test regardless of its effect on conversion.

Improved execution (pre-registered, powered, one variable)

Analyst: "Primary metric: cart-to-checkout-started rate. Single change: shipping cost and estimated arrival date shown on the cart page. Nothing else moves. Current rate 44%; the minimum effect worth acting on is +4 points, which needs roughly 2,400 carts per arm — about 11 days at current volume, so we run 14 days to cover a full weekly cycle and we do not look at significance before day 14. Secondary metrics logged but not decision-eligible: AOV, refund rate, support contacts about shipping. Guardrail: if AOV drops more than 6%, we stop early and investigate, because moving shipping forward may suppress threshold-chasing. Segmented readout by device, because the mobile gap is the reason we believe this hypothesis at all."

Why it succeeds: One variable, a pre-registered primary metric, a stated minimum effect, a sample-size floor derived from that effect, a fixed horizon that removes optional stopping, a guardrail metric with a pre-committed action, and a segmentation plan tied to the original hypothesis. The result will be interpretable whichever way it goes — including a null result, which this design can actually detect rather than explain away.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced operators find the constrained stage and then fix it at the wrong altitude. Having correctly identified that trial-to-activation is the bottleneck, they optimise the activation flow — better empty states, a progress bar, a nudge email — and move the number three points.

What they skip is the question of who is in the trial. A large share of the 66% who never connected a roster were single-site operators outside the ICP, for whom the product was structurally wrong. No amount of onboarding design converts them; it merely converts a targeting problem into an engineering backlog.

The tell is that the constraint keeps returning. A stage that has been optimised three times and still constrains is usually not an experience problem — it is receiving the wrong population, and the leak is upstream of everything being measured.

Before optimising a stage, segment it. If the stage performs acceptably for the ICP and catastrophically for everyone else, the intervention is in acquisition, not in the funnel step where the loss is visible.

The mechanism: A funnel measures where users stop, not why they arrived. Optimising the stage where loss appears treats a selection problem as a design problem — and because the fix produces a small genuine improvement, it confirms the diagnosis and the real cause stays invisible for another cycle.

8. Practice: one-page funnel diagnosis

Exercise goal: produce a diagnosis that identifies the constrained stage with evidence and ranks interventions by expected value per unit of effort.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Draw your funnel with every stage, and write the denominator beside each rate. If you cannot state a denominator, that is your first finding.
  2. Segment at least one stage by device and by new-versus-returning. Report where the two segments diverge most.
  3. For each stage, propose a defensible achievable rate and grade the evidence for that target: strong, moderate, or weak. Be willing to write "weak."
  4. Compute expected annual contribution for each stage and divide by an effort estimate in person-weeks.
  5. Name the constraint. State the single intervention you would run first and the one you are deliberately not running.
  6. Design the experiment: primary metric, minimum effect worth acting on, sample-size floor, fixed horizon, guardrail, and stopping rule.

Completed exemplar (B2B SaaS free-trial funnel)

ElementEntry

Experiment designPrimary: first-session data-source connection rate. Single change: guided connection step with a sample dataset fallback. Minimum effect: +8 points. Floor: 1,900 trials per arm ≈ 18 days; fixed 21-day horizon, no interim looks. Guardrail: trial→paid must not fall (a sample dataset could produce hollow activation). Stopping rule: if connection rises but trial→paid falls, the metric is being gamed — revert and re-diagnose.

Evaluation rubric: Denominator discipline (20%); quality of segmentation and willingness to report divergence (20%); honesty of evidence grading, including at least one "weak" (20%); expected-value ranking arithmetic (20%); experiment design with pre-registered metric, floor, and stopping rule (20%).

Beginner variation: Diagnose a public checkout flow you use as a customer. Document the form-field count, when total cost first appears, and where you would leak. You do not need internal data to practise the reasoning.

Advanced variation: Add a cohort LTV model and re-rank the interventions on lifetime contribution rather than first-order revenue. Retention improvements usually move several places up the list, which is the point.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Traffic is strong but checkout completion is weak. What should you test first — more traffic, checkout friction, or more social posts?

Checkout friction. Adding traffic multiplies revenue through the existing leak, so you pay full price for a discounted visitor; fixing the constrained stage multiplies every visitor you already have and every visitor you buy later. Baymard's checkout usability work puts the average recoverable gain from checkout redesign alone near 35% — larger than most channel expansions, at a fraction of the cost, with no ongoing media spend attached.

2. Your mobile cart abandonment is 31 points worse than desktop. What is the most likely cause?

Form and input friction, not intent. The documented mobile–desktop abandonment gap is roughly 80% versus 66% — about 14 points. A 31-point gap is roughly double the expected difference, which points at something specific to the small-screen experience: too many form fields, no autofill or digital-wallet support, keyboard type mismatches on numeric inputs, layout shift at the payment step, or a validation error that is rendered off-screen. Start by completing a purchase on a real mid-range device on a slow connection, not in a desktop emulator.

3. Why is ROAS a poor decision metric for channel viability?

Because it uses revenue rather than contribution margin, so it is silent on whether the revenue is profitable. A 4× ROAS on a 22%-margin product is a loss; a 1.8× ROAS on an 80%-margin subscription with 90% annual retention can be excellent. ROAS also ignores payback period, so it cannot tell you whether the business survives the cash gap between spending and being paid. Judge a channel on contribution after variable cost, and on payback against your cash position.

4. A test shows +6% conversion after four days. Should you roll it out?

Not on that evidence. Four days is unlikely to reach the pre-registered sample floor, does not cover a full weekly cycle (weekday and weekend traffic differ in composition, not just volume), and stopping when the number looks good is optional stopping — which inflates the false-positive rate to roughly a third for a fixed-horizon design that is peeked at repeatedly. Run to the pre-committed horizon. If you genuinely need to look early, use a sequential design that budgets for interim analyses in advance.

5. Unexpected extra costs drive ~48% of stated abandonment. Why is this a trust problem rather than a price problem?

Because the buyer had already accepted a price. They evaluated a number, decided it was acceptable, invested effort, and were then shown a different number at the moment of commitment. The emotional content is not "too expensive" but "I was misled" — which is why discounting rarely fixes it while transparency does. It also explains why the fix is cheap: showing shipping and tax on the product or cart page costs almost nothing and removes the violation entirely, whereas subsidising shipping costs margin on every order forever.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Online selling rewards diagnosis over enthusiasm. The organizations that compound are not the ones with the cleverest campaigns; they are the ones that know, with evidence, which stage is constraining them this quarter — and are willing to leave attractive-looking work undone until that stage is fixed.

  • State the denominator at every stage. Most funnel disputes are undeclared-denominator disputes.
  • Find the constraint by expected value against a defensible achievable rate, not by lowest number.
  • Grade the evidence for each target and be willing to write "weak" — that column is what makes the model credible.
  • Unexpected costs, forced accounts, and form burden dominate documented abandonment. Check those before theorizing.
  • Judge channels on contribution margin and payback, never on ROAS or volume.
  • Pre-register the primary metric, the sample floor, the horizon, the guardrail, and the stopping rule — before you look.

Immediate action: Complete a purchase or signup on your own product using a mid-range mobile device on a throttled connection. Count the form fields, note the exact moment total cost first appears, and time the whole flow. Nearly everyone who does this finds their constraint in under ten minutes.

32Positioning

Segment by buying reality, not by industry code.

Two companies in the same sector with the same headcount can have entirely different problems, buying processes, and cost to serve. This module builds segments that predict behavior, an ICP with an anti-ICP, and positioning against the alternatives buyers actually consider — including doing nothing.

1. Why most segmentation predicts nothing

Ask a revenue team for their segments and you usually get a list of industries crossed with headcount bands. That is a filing system, not a segmentation, and the test is simple: does knowing which segment an account is in tell you anything about how they will buy, what they will pay, or whether they will stay? For most industry-by-size schemes the answer is no.

The consequence is not merely academic. A segmentation that does not predict produces a message that must hedge, a sales motion that must be improvised per deal, a pricing model that fits nobody, and a set of references that do not transfer. Every downstream activity gets harder, and nobody traces the difficulty back to its cause.

A useful segment has five properties: it is internally similar enough to support one offer and one motion; externally different enough that the choice matters; reachable through some identifiable channel; measurable, so you can count it and score against it; and economically attractive after cost to serve. Industry and headcount usually satisfy only "measurable."

The variables that actually predict are about buying reality: the trigger that makes the problem urgent, the workflow the product touches, the maturity of the function, the approval process, and the cost to serve. Two hospitals of identical size, one with an in-house integration team and one without, belong in different segments — and knowing that predicts implementation cost, cycle length, and churn.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Construct segments from buying-behavior variables and test whether they predict anything.

Build an ICP validated against retention and margin, not against logo count.

Write an anti-ICP specific enough to disqualify a real account this week.

Position against the full alternative set, including internal build and doing nothing.

Produce a positioning dossier with claims, proof, and the segments each claim is true for.

The prediction test

For each proposed segment, ask: if I know an account is in this segment, what can I predict that I could not predict otherwise? Cycle length, win rate, deal size, implementation duration, 12-month retention. If the answer is "nothing measurable," the segment is a label. Run this test against your existing segments before designing new ones — most schemes fail it, and demonstrating that is what unlocks the work.

One practical warning: segments must be operable. A segment defined by a variable nobody can observe before the first conversation cannot route leads, target campaigns, or qualify accounts. Where a predictive variable is unobservable — function maturity, say — pair it with an observable proxy, and record the actual value once you learn it so the proxy can be checked and improved.

4. ICP, anti-ICP, and the retention test

An ideal customer profile is a description of the situation in which you can create and capture value repeatedly. Two words carry the weight. Situation, because an ICP is a set of circumstances rather than a type of company. And repeatedly, because a customer you win and lose is not a good customer.

Table 2: The four ICP dimensions and their evidence

DimensionQuestionEvidence to useCommon error
Problem severityIs the problem bad enough to fund a solution?Committed resources already spent on workarounds (Module 31, rung 1).Inferring severity from complaint volume. People complain about things they will never pay to fix.
CompatibilityCan we actually deliver here?Implementation duration and support load by cohort.Treating this as a delivery concern rather than a sales qualification criterion.
EconomicsIs this profitable after cost to serve?Contribution margin by segment, not revenue.Using revenue. The largest accounts are sometimes the least profitable.
DurabilityDo they stay and expand?Cohort GRR and NRR by segment (Module 11).Defining the ICP from won deals rather than retained ones. This is the central error.
Win rate does not define an ICP

Most ICPs are built from closed-won data, which measures who is easy to sell to, not who is good to serve. A segment with a 40% win rate and 61% twelve-month gross revenue retention is not your ICP; it is a treadmill. Build the profile from cohorts that retained and expanded, then check that you can still win them. Where those two populations differ, the difference is the most valuable finding available to a commercial team — and it is usually large.

The anti-ICP is the more useful half and the less commonly written. It must be specific enough to disqualify a real account today, not a set of pious generalities. Useful anti-ICP entries name: technical environments you cannot serve; operating models where implementation reliably fails; sizes below which the economics do not work; buying processes your motion cannot survive; jurisdictions you cannot legally serve; and use cases where the product has demonstrably underperformed.

Writing an anti-ICP has a second effect worth having: it makes disqualification a legitimate act with a documented basis, which is one of the highest-leverage changes a sales organization can make (Module 18).

5. Positioning against real alternatives

Positioning starts with a question most vendors never ask honestly: what would this buyer do if we did not exist? The answer is rarely "buy from our named competitor."

Table 3: The alternative set

AlternativeHow often it winsWhat it demands of your positioning
Do nothingMost often, in most markets. The majority of qualified pipeline in complex B2B ends here.You must cost the status quo as a running total, not describe your benefit (Modules 19, 21).
Internal buildFrequently in technical organizations.Compare total cost of ownership honestly, including maintenance and staff turnover — and concede where building genuinely wins.
A spreadsheet or manual processVery often at the low end.Respect it. It is free, flexible, and understood. Name the specific point at which it breaks.
An adjacent tool they already ownOften, and usually invisible to you.Find out what they already pay for. "We already have something that does part of this" is a positioning problem, not an objection.
A named competitorLess often than vendors assume.Differentiate on checkable capability for a named segment, not on adjectives.
An agency or service providerCommon where the buyer lacks capacity.Position on where software plus their people beats outsourcing, and where it does not.

Once the alternative set is honest, positioning becomes a set of claims, each with three attachments: the segment it is true for, the alternative it beats, and the proof. A claim without all three is marketing atmosphere and will fail the substitution test from Module 07.

Positioning is a choice to be worse at some things

A position that claims advantage everywhere is not a position. The useful artifact names, explicitly, the segments where you are not the right answer and what you would recommend instead. This costs a small number of poor-fit deals and buys the credibility that makes every other claim believable — the same mechanism as the disclosed limitation in Modules 07, 09 and 25.

6. Worked example: a re-segmentation that changed the business

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A compliance-software company segments by industry (financial services / healthcare / manufacturing) and size (SMB / mid-market / enterprise). Growth has stalled and nobody can explain why some deals go smoothly and others do not.

Table 4: Old segments versus rebuilt segments

DimensionOld scheme (industry × size)Rebuilt scheme
BasisIndustry code and headcount band.Regulator + evidence-collection ownership + operating model.
Prediction testWin rate varied 31–36% across all nine cells. Cycle length varied 68–79 days. The scheme predicted nothing.Win rate varied 12–58% across four segments. Cycle length 41–160 days. 12-month GRR 54–96%.
Segment A—Single-regulator, named compliance owner, single operating entity. Win 58%, cycle 41 days, GRR 96%.
Segment B—Single-regulator, compliance owned by finance as a side duty. Win 44%, cycle 71 days, GRR 88%.
Segment C—Multi-regulator, federated entities. Win 21%, cycle 160 days, GRR 71%. Deal sizes large but implementation cost 3.4× the median.
Segment D—Compliance outsourced to an external firm. Win 12%, GRR 54%. The buyer is not the user and never adopts.
The finding nobody expectedSegment A contained companies from all three industries and all three size bands. The predictive variable was who owned evidence collection, which had never been recorded anywhere.
ActionsSegment A became the ICP; ownership of evidence collection became a mandatory qualification question. Segment D was added to the anti-ICP and closed to new acquisition. Segment C was repriced with implementation charged separately, which made it profitable at a lower volume.
Result, two quartersNew-business win rate 34% → 49%. Median cycle 74 → 58 days. New logos fell 18%; net revenue grew 31% because the mix shifted toward accounts that retained.

The methodological point: the rebuilt segmentation was not more sophisticated — it used three variables instead of two. It was predictive, because the variables were chosen by testing which ones actually separated outcomes rather than by which ones were already in the database.

The Brayford thread · The segmentation that put Brayford in scope

Three variables that predicted, and two that did not

Cadence's original segments were care sector × size band. Tested against outcomes, they separated nothing: win rate varied by four points across six cells and twelve-month gross retention by six.

The rebuilt segmentation used three variables observable during discovery: whether a named individual owns workforce systems, single versus multi-entity estate, and whether the rostering system exposes an API. Win rate across the resulting segments ranged from 19% to 54%; gross retention from 58% to 94%.

Brayford sits across two of them, which is the interesting part. The fourteen original sites are the top segment — named owner in Tom, single estate, API on Rotaflow. The four Marlow sites are the bottom one: no API, separate entity, 58% retention historically.

That is why the deal was always going to be four sites plus a question, and why treating Brayford as one homogeneous account would have produced a proposal that was wrong for most of it. An account is not necessarily a segment.

7. Weak versus improved positioning statement

Composite Case StudyScenario: A buyer asks, "So what makes you different from the others?"

Weak execution (adjectives, no segment, no alternative)

Seller: "Great question. I'd say three things really set us apart. First, we're much more flexible than the legacy vendors — we can configure to pretty much any process. Second, our customer support is genuinely best in class; you get a named contact. And third, we're the most modern platform in the space, built cloud-native from day one, whereas most of our competitors are running on twenty-year-old architecture."

Why it fails: All three claims fail the substitution test — every competitor in the category says exactly this, including the ones being described as legacy. "Flexible" is an unfalsifiable adjective and, to a buyer who has run a configuration project, a warning. No segment is named, so the buyer cannot tell whether any of it applies to them. No alternative is engaged: the buyer may well be comparing against a spreadsheet or against doing nothing, and none of this addresses either. And no proof is attached to anything.

Improved execution (segment, alternative, checkable claim, and where you lose)

Seller: "Depends what you're actually comparing us to, and I'd rather answer the real question. Are you looking at other vendors, at building something internally, or at carrying on as you are? Those have completely different answers.
Buyer: "Honestly, mostly at carrying on. And one other vendor."
Seller: "Then the honest comparison is this.

Against carrying on: on your own figures, evidence collection is about 110 hours per audit cycle across two people. That is happening whether or not you do anything, and it grows when you add the fourth entity.

Against [the other vendor]: they are genuinely better than us on workflow configuration — if you need to model an unusual approval chain, they will do it better and I would say so to your team. Where we are different is that we pull evidence directly from source systems rather than requiring upload. For organizations like yours — one regulator, a named compliance owner, single entity — that has taken evidence-gathering from about 110 hours to about 30 in three comparable deployments. I can send the measurement method and introduce you to two of them, including one that took much longer than planned and why.

And if you were multi-regulator and federated, I would tell you we are a worse fit and that our implementations there take three times as long. You are not, which is why I think this is worth your time."

Why it succeeds: The seller established the actual alternative set before positioning against it, and found the real competitor was the status quo. Each claim names a segment, an alternative, and a proof with a measurement method. A genuine competitor advantage is conceded, unprompted. And the seller states the segment where they are the wrong answer — which is what makes the rest credible rather than promotional.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced teams rebuild segmentation on predictive variables and then apply it at the wrong unit. Segments are assigned to accounts, because that is how the CRM is shaped, and a large account containing genuinely different estates gets a single label and a single motion.

Brayford is the ordinary case rather than the exception. Any organisation formed by acquisition, operating across jurisdictions, or running federated business units will contain sub-populations that differ on exactly the variables your segmentation says are decisive. Labelling the whole thing "top segment" because the headquarters qualifies produces a proposal priced and scoped for the best part of the estate and delivered into the worst.

The symptom appears in delivery rather than in sales: implementations that run long in a specific subset of sites, retention that is fine at group level and poor at particular locations, and expansion that stalls without an articulable reason.

Segment at the unit where the predictive variables actually vary — which is sometimes the account, and in multi-site or multi-entity buyers is very often the site.

The mechanism: Segmentation is applied at the granularity the CRM records, not the granularity at which the variables differ. When an account is internally heterogeneous on the deciding variables, an account-level label averages them — and the average describes no part of the estate, while looking rigorous.

8. Practice: the positioning dossier

Exercise goal: produce segments that predict, an ICP validated on retention, an operable anti-ICP, and positioning claims that each carry a segment, an alternative, and proof.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Run the prediction test on your current segments. For each, pull win rate, median cycle length, deal size, and 12-month GRR. If they do not separate, say so — that is the result that justifies the rest of the work.
  2. Propose three to five new segments using buying-reality variables from Table 1. Each must be operable — observable, or paired with an observable proxy.
  3. Re-run the prediction test on the new segments using the same four measures.
  4. Build the ICP from retained cohorts, not from won deals. Note explicitly where the "easy to win" and "good to serve" populations differ.
  5. Write the anti-ICP with at least five specific, checkable exclusions. Test it: name a real account in your pipeline it would disqualify today.
  6. List the full alternative set for your ICP, including do-nothing, internal build, and adjacent tools they already own. Estimate how often each wins.
  7. Write three positioning claims, each with the segment it holds for, the alternative it beats, and the proof with its measurement method.
  8. Write where you lose — the segment or use case where a named alternative is genuinely better, and what you would recommend.
  9. Name the trigger events that make your ICP buy now rather than eventually, and how you will detect each.

Evaluation rubric: Prediction test run on old and new segments with real numbers (25%); segments built from buying-reality variables and operable (20%); ICP derived from retained cohorts with divergence noted (20%); anti-ICP specific enough to disqualify a named pipeline account (15%); positioning claims with segment, alternative, and proof (15%); where-we-lose stated explicitly (5%).

Beginner variation: Run step 1 only — pull win rate and 12-month retention for your existing segments. If they do not separate, you have the finding that makes the rest of this work fundable.

Advanced variation: Model the revenue effect of closing your worst segment to new acquisition: lost bookings against recovered capacity, reduced support load, and improved blended retention. In most companies the arithmetic favours closing it, and the reason it stays open is that nobody has ever put the two numbers side by side.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Two groups report the same problem, but one self-serves and the other requires a six-month security process. Same segment?

No. Segments must predict behavior, and buying process is one of the strongest predictors of cycle length, cost of sale, and whether your motion works at all. These two groups need different pricing, different sales motions, different proof, and different economics — combining them produces a blended average that describes neither. The identical problem is a red herring; what matters commercially is how the purchase actually happens and what it costs you to serve.

2. A segment has your highest win rate and your worst retention. Is it your ICP?

No. Win rate measures who is easy to sell to; an ICP describes where you can create and capture value repeatedly. A segment with 40% win rate and 61% twelve-month GRR is a treadmill — you are refilling a leaking bucket and paying full acquisition cost each time. Build the ICP from cohorts that retained and expanded, then check you can still win them. Where the easy-to-win and good-to-serve populations differ, that gap is usually the most valuable finding a commercial team can produce.

3. What is the most common winner in a competitive evaluation?

Doing nothing. In most complex B2B markets the majority of qualified pipeline ends in no decision rather than in a competitor's contract, which means positioning built entirely around vendor differentiation addresses the minority case. The implication is concrete: cost the status quo as a running monthly total rather than describing your benefit, and make the first step small and reversible so the decision is not a large irreversible bet (Modules 09, 19, 21).

4. Why write an anti-ICP, and what makes one useful?

Because an ICP without exclusions is a wish. A useful anti-ICP is specific enough to disqualify a named account in your pipeline today — "no API on the source system," "workflow owned by an outsourced provider," "incumbent framework with 18+ months remaining" — not a set of generalities about poor fit. It also does something structural: it makes disqualification a documented, legitimate act rather than something that looks like giving up, which is one of the highest-leverage changes available to a sales organization.

5. Buyers praise a differentiator but rarely use it after purchase. What should you do?

Downgrade the claim until behavior or outcome evidence supports it. Praise is rung-5 evidence (Module 31) and tells you the feature sounds appealing, not that it creates value. Do not remove the feature — it may work passively, or the usage measure may be wrong — but stop leading with it and find an outcome measure that would settle the question. Leading with a differentiator that does not produce realized value is how a company builds a positioning that wins deals and loses renewals.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Segmentation is a prediction exercise and positioning is a choice to be worse at some things. Both fail in the same way — by being built from what is convenient to record rather than from what actually separates outcomes.

  • Test every segment: what does membership let you predict that you could not otherwise?
  • Segment on trigger, workaround, operating model, technical environment, and buying process — not industry and headcount.
  • Build the ICP from retained cohorts. Won-deal data describes who is easy to sell to.
  • Write an anti-ICP specific enough to disqualify a real account this week.
  • Establish the alternative set before positioning. Doing nothing usually wins most often.
  • Every claim needs a segment, an alternative, and a proof with a method.
  • State where you lose. It is what makes the rest believable.

Immediate action: Pull win rate and twelve-month gross revenue retention for your existing segments. If the numbers do not separate, your segmentation is a filing system — and that single table is what will fund the work to replace it.

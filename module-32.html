1. Segment for action

A usable segment is similar enough to support a shared offer and motion, different enough to justify a choice, reachable, measurable, and economically attractive. Firmographics alone rarely explain purchase behavior. Combine context, trigger, workflow, maturity, constraints, value potential, and buying process.

2. Build an evidence-based ICP

An ICP is a situation where you can create and capture value repeatedly. Score fit separately from intent. Fit includes problem severity, compatibility, economics, implementation readiness, and acceptable risk. Intent is a current signal, not a permanent identity. Define an anti-ICP: customers with poor economics, harmful use, impossible requirements, or low probability of value.

3. Start with competitive alternatives

Ask what customers would do if you did not exist: another vendor, internal build, spreadsheet, agency, delay, or tolerate the problem. List capabilities that are genuinely different, translate them into outcomes for the selected segment, and support each with proof.

4. Choose a market frame

The category sets expectations about competitors, features, price, and risk. Use an existing category when it helps buyers understand and budget. Create a subcategory only when the old frame hides your strongest value and you can teach the new frame without confusion.

5. Turn positioning into a message system

Create a hierarchy: audience and context; problem or opportunity; differentiated value; evidence; risk; next decision. Keep one source of truth, then adapt by stakeholder and channel. Test comprehension, relevance, credibility, price realization, activation, retention, and loss reasons—not applause.

6. Artifact: positioning dossier

Deliver a segment choice, ICP/anti-ICP, alternative map, differentiated value table, proof inventory, category decision, and message hierarchy. Include evidence and open assumptions.

7. Field guide: make a market choice

Segmentation, ideal customer profile, and positioning solve different decisions. Segmentation divides a market into groups that require meaningfully different offers or motions. An ICP describes the account situation where both customer value and seller economics are likely to be strong. Positioning establishes why that group should choose this approach over the alternatives it actually considers. A persona describes a person; it cannot replace account fit or buying context.

Build segments from buying reality

Begin with evidence from Module 31. Look for differences in trigger, workflow, consequence, current alternative, implementation readiness, buying group, risk, and willingness to change. “Companies with 50–500 employees” is reachable, but it may contain several incompatible buying situations. A useful segment predicts a different message, qualification rule, proof requirement, channel, product package, or service model.

Evaluate each candidate segment on problem importance, frequency, ability to realize value, reachability, competitive pressure, sales-cycle burden, implementation cost, retention potential, strategic fit, and evidence confidence. Keep uncertainty visible. A precise score built from guesses is still a guess.

Separate fit, intent, and access

  • Fit: Can this customer realize enough value, and can you serve them responsibly and profitably?
  • Intent: Is there current evidence of a change process, such as a trigger, active investigation, committed resources, or deadline?
  • Access: Can you reach the people and evidence needed for a fair evaluation?

A high-fit account without current intent may belong in long-term development, not an active forecast. A high-intent account with poor fit may close and then fail. The anti-ICP should include harmful use, impossible requirements, unsupported jurisdictions, unacceptable economics, and situations where another solution is better.

Position against the real alternative

Ask recent buyers what they would have done without you. The answer may be a spreadsheet, an internal hire, an agency, an incumbent feature, postponement, or tolerating the problem. April Dunford’s positioning sequence is a practical method: identify competitive alternatives, isolate genuinely differentiated capabilities, translate them into value for customers who care, choose the best-fit segment, and select a market frame that makes the value understandable. It is practitioner guidance that requires local validation, not proof that one sequence wins everywhere.

Positioning test

For [specific customer in a specific circumstance] who would otherwise [real alternative], our [category or approach] helps [verifiable outcome] because [differentiated capability and proof]. Unlike [alternative], it [important trade-off].

A category gives buyers expectations about budget, risk, features, and competitors. An existing category reduces explanation cost but can hide a meaningful difference. A new category may create distinction but forces the seller to teach and defend the frame. Choose deliberately.

Worked comparison

Composite Case Study

Composite educational scenario: A workflow vendor claims it serves “all professional services.” In practice, buyers naturally cluster into two distinct groups. Small agencies want a quick template and self-service setup; regulated consultancies need approval trails, data controls, and legal review. One message and package weakens both. The team chooses regulated consultancies because the differentiated audit trail matters there, but records longer sales cycles and higher implementation cost as trade-offs.

Weak message: “An innovative AI platform that transforms productivity.” It names no customer, alternative, or proof.
Improved message: “For regulated consulting teams replacing email approvals, the platform creates a traceable review record without moving client files into a new repository. A controlled pilot verifies completion time and audit evidence before rollout.” The improved version can still fail if integration, security, or economics do not support the claim.

Common failure modes

  • Defining the ICP as whoever has budget.
  • Using firmographics when workflow and trigger explain the purchase.
  • Calling every product in the category a competitor while ignoring “do nothing.”
  • Claiming differentiation that buyers cannot verify or do not value.
  • Changing the homepage headline without aligning product, sales, implementation, and proof.
  • Using win rate alone and ignoring retention, margin, time-to-value, and selection bias.

8. Practice: positioning dossier

Objective: choose a defensible segment and create a position that can be tested in real sales work.

Composite Case Study

Scenario: Use the accounting-workflow evidence from Module 31. Candidate segments include solo accountants, ten-to-fifty-person firms, and outsourced finance providers. Each uses different workflows and has different buying authority.

  1. Create three segment hypotheses using trigger, workflow, alternative, consequence, and buying process.
  2. Score them using documented evidence and confidence, not intuition alone.
  3. Define an ICP and anti-ICP for the selected segment.
  4. List the three real alternatives and the customer’s reason for choosing each.
  5. Build a capability → customer value → proof table.
  6. Write the positioning test statement and two stakeholder adaptations.
  7. Define a validation plan using qualified progression, implementation success, retention, and loss evidence.

Expected output: segment scorecard, ICP/anti-ICP, alternatives map, value-and-proof table, message hierarchy, and validation plan.

Evaluation criteria: evidence quality 25%; segment actionability 20%; alternative realism 20%; differentiation and proof 20%; stated trade-offs and test plan 15%.

Advanced variation: show how the position changes for retail, founder-led, and enterprise motions without changing the underlying truth.

Beginner variation: compare only two segments and use a simple high/medium/low evidence rating. Partial strong response: “Regulated consultancies show stronger need for traceable approvals, but current evidence comes from four interviews. We will not call this an ICP until two pilots confirm implementation fit and finance validates willingness to pay.”

Self-review: Would a different segment require a different offer or motion? Did you separate fit from intent? Is each differentiated value claim supported? What result would disconfirm your choice?

9. Applied knowledge check

1. A prospect shows strong web intent but requires an unsupported deployment model. What is strongest?

Treat intent and fit separately. Clarify whether the requirement is mandatory and disqualify if it is. More engagement does not repair serviceability; a supported alternative or a changed requirement could alter the decision.

2. Two segments report the same problem, but one requires a six-month security process and the other self-serves. Keep one segment?

Model them separately because buying process, cost to serve, proof, and motion differ. Combining them hides trade-offs; choosing only the faster segment may ignore greater value. Retention, margin, implementation, and reachable demand could change the priority.

3. Buyers like a differentiator but never use it after purchase. What should change?

Downgrade the claim until behavior or outcome evidence supports it. Interview praise is weak evidence of realized value. Usage may be the wrong measure if the capability works passively, so define the intended behavior and outcome first.

4. One segment has high win rate but poor retention. Is it the ICP?

Not without investigating selection, implementation, and realized value. Win rate measures acquisition, not durable fit. Stronger cohort retention, margin, time-to-value, and causal evidence could support or reject the segment.

Immediate action: Ask one recent customer what they would have done without you, then compare that answer with the alternatives in your current positioning.

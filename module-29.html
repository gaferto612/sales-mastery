The valuable part is orientation, not speed.

The OODA loop is usually taught as "go faster than your competitor." Boyd's actual emphasis was on orientation — the model through which you interpret evidence — and in complex sales, acting faster than you understand is the characteristic failure, not the cure.

1. What the loop is and how it is misused

John Boyd's OODA loop — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act — comes from military strategy and is class F: a conceptual model, not an empirical finding, and one developed for adversarial contexts rather than for collaborative commercial ones. Applying it to selling requires care, because the analogy breaks in an important place.

The popular version reduces it to a slogan: get inside your competitor's decision cycle, act faster than they can respond. That reading is both a misrepresentation of Boyd and actively harmful advice for complex sales. Boyd's own emphasis was overwhelmingly on orientation — the accumulated models, experience, and cultural assumptions through which raw observation becomes meaning. He regarded orientation as the step that determines everything else, and a fast loop running on a wrong orientation as worse than a slow one.

The analogy also breaks in the obvious place: your buyer is not an adversary. Speed against a competitor is occasionally relevant; speed against the buyer's own understanding is always counterproductive. A seller who "gets inside the buyer's decision cycle" is describing manipulation, and it produces exactly the pressure that makes indecisive buyers do nothing (Module 09).

The version worth keeping is narrow: selling is a process of forming and updating a model of a situation you can only observe partially, and most sales failures are orientation failures rather than speed failures. You were not too slow. You were confidently working from a picture that had stopped being true.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Explain why orientation, not speed, is the decisive step, and why the adversarial framing fails in selling.

Identify the assumptions in your current picture of a deal and mark which are evidenced.

Shorten the interval between an observation and an updated model.

Distinguish situations where speed helps from those where it destroys.

Produce a deal-learning loop with explicit assumptions and disconfirming tests.

3. Orientation: the step that matters

Orientation is the model through which observations acquire meaning. Two sellers observing the same event — "the champion has gone quiet for two weeks" — will produce completely different actions depending on their orientation, and neither will notice they are interpreting rather than perceiving.

Table 1: The same observation, four orientations

"Deals stall because sellers don't follow up enough."Insufficient contact.Increase follow-up frequency.Adds pressure to someone who may be blocked, not forgetful. Can convert a delay into a permanent silence.
"Buyers go quiet when they've chosen someone else."Lost.Deprioritize, reduce effort.Abandons a deal that may be stalled internally for a reason you could help with.
"Silence usually means an internal blocker my contact can't discuss."Something happened inside the organization.Ask directly, offer an exit, seek a second thread.Usually the most productive, but can be wrong — sometimes people are simply on holiday.

The orientation audit

For any deal, write your current picture in five sentences, then mark each as observed (someone said or did it, with a date), inferred (you reasoned to it), or assumed (you would struggle to say where it came from). Most sellers find that their confident picture is two observations and three assumptions — and that the assumptions are the load-bearing ones.

Orientation is also where previous success does damage. A seller whose last three wins came from a particular pattern will interpret new situations through it, and the fit will be poor exactly when the market or segment has shifted. Boyd's term for this was the danger of a closed orientation — a model that filters out the evidence that would correct it.

4. Loop time in a sales cycle

The useful metric is not cycle length but the interval between an observation and an updated model. A nine-month enterprise cycle with a two-day loop time is well run; a three-month cycle with a six-week loop time is a seller acting on a stale picture.

Table 2: Loop time by activity

Within a conversationSecondsCheck understanding aloud: "Did I get that right?" A misread corrected in the same minute costs nothing; one carried into a proposal costs weeks.
Between meetingsDays to weeksSend a written summary within 24 hours and ask for correction. Buyers correct written errors they would never volunteer verbally.

The cheapest loop-time improvement available to any seller is the written summary. A short note after every substantive conversation — "here's what I understood, correct anything I got wrong" — converts a private model into a shared, correctable one. Buyers routinely correct written errors they would not have raised in conversation, because correcting a document is low-friction and correcting a person mid-sentence is not.

5. When speed helps and when it destroys

Speed is not uniformly good or bad. It helps in some places and does real damage in others, and the distinction is not subtle once stated.

Table 3: Where to be fast and where to be slow

Be fast at…WhyBe slow at…Why
Responding — questions, documents, promised materials.Responsiveness is the cheapest reliability signal in the trust equation, and it is entirely within your control.Proposing a solution.Proposing before diagnosis is the most common process failure in selling, and it is very hard to undo.
Surfacing bad news.A defect disclosed early is a negotiation; disclosed late it is a crisis.Interpreting silence.Silence is underdetermined. Acting on the first interpretation that occurs to you is how deals are abandoned or over-pressured.
Disqualifying.Returned capacity compounds. A deal correctly dropped in week three is worth more than one dropped in week twenty.Pushing for a decision.Pressure raises the stakes of choosing wrongly, which produces delay in exactly the population you are trying to move (Module 09).

Escalating a blocker you cannot resolve.Time spent stuck is not learning.Reacting to a competitor's move.Competitive panic produces discounting and feature-matching, which are the two worst available responses.
The failure the popular version causes

"Get inside their decision cycle" produces sellers who compress the buyer's evaluation, push for decisions before readiness, and treat the buyer's deliberation as an obstacle. In a market where the majority of losses are no-decisions driven by fear of choosing wrongly, that is precisely the wrong intervention — you are adding speed to the one variable that responds badly to it. Fast orientation, patient action is the workable inversion.

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A £280k deal where the seller's picture has been stable for two months and the deal has not moved. An orientation audit is run.

The process change: a fortnightly ten-minute orientation audit on every deal over median value — five sentences, each marked observed / inferred / assumed, with a cheap test written for anything assumed and load-bearing. Across the following quarter it caught two dead deals early and three wrong economic-buyer assumptions.

The Brayford thread · Week 16 · five sentences, three of them assumptions

The orientation audit that unstalled it

Alex's picture of Brayford at week 16, written from memory before opening the CRM:

(1) They must act before the September Rotaflow renewal. (2) Tom is driving it internally. (3) Elaine will approve it once Tom takes it up. (4) We're ahead of Stafford. (5) The delay is Brayford's procurement being slow.

Marked: (1) observed — contract date, verified. (2) assumed — from Tom's responsiveness. (3) assumed — from one sentence in week 6. (4) assumed — from tone. (5) assumed — the most comfortable available explanation, and procurement had not yet been engaged at all.

Four of five were assumptions, and the two load-bearing ones — (2) and (5) — were both wrong. The cheap tests were one question each: "is it fair for me to assume you'd take this to Elaine?" and "I've assumed the delay is process rather than anything else — is that right?"

The picture had last been updated in week 6. Ten weeks of increasingly confident action on a two-month-old model — which is the failure this lens exists to catch.

7. Weak versus improved response to new evidence

Composite Case StudyScenario: The buyer mentions in passing that "we've asked a couple of other people to come in and present."

Weak execution (fast action on an unexamined interpretation)

Seller's internal read: "We're losing. I need to move faster than them."

Seller: "Of course, due diligence is important! Look, let me be straight with you — I want to make this easy. I can get you a 15% discount if we can wrap this up before the end of the month, and I'll throw in the premium support tier. I can also get our CTO on a call this week if that helps. What would it take to get this over the line now?"

Why it fails: A single ambiguous observation was converted instantly into a conclusion and an action, with no attempt to establish what was actually happening. Nobody asked who the others are, why they were added, or whether this is a procurement requirement rather than a preference shift. The discount is unconditional and arrives before any negotiation, which prices the product and signals weakness. And the acceleration reads as panic — a buyer who has just added vendors will notice that the incumbent favourite suddenly became desperate, which is itself evidence about the vendor.

Improved execution (update orientation before acting)

Seller: "That's useful to know, and I'd rather understand it than react to it. Can I ask three things? Who are they — or at least what kind of thing are they? Was adding them your call, or is it a procurement requirement above a certain value? And has something changed in what you're trying to solve, or is this about being able to show a comparison?"
Buyer: "Procurement, mostly. Anything over £250k needs three quotes. Though one of them does the reporting piece better than you, and my finance colleague is quite keen on that."
Seller: "Then two separate things are happening and they need different answers. The three-quote rule I can help with — I'd rather you had a genuine comparison than a token one, so tell me if you want me to suggest who'd give you a real alternative.

The reporting point is the real one, and I've been honest with you that it's our weakest area. Can I meet your finance colleague? Not to talk her out of it — if reporting is the deciding criterion, she should weight it and we might genuinely be the wrong choice. But I'd like to know how much of the gap matters in practice versus in a feature comparison, and that's a conversation I'd rather have now than lose to.

And I'm not going to discount to respond to this. That would just tell you the price was never real."

Why it succeeds: Three questions before any action, which separated a procedural requirement from a genuine competitive threat — two things requiring completely different responses. The seller helped with the procedural one, which is cheap and builds standing. The real threat was engaged directly, including conceding that the competitor may be right. And declining to discount preserved both margin and credibility at the moment when panic-discounting is most tempting.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced sellers run orientation audits and then run them only when a deal is visibly stuck — which is precisely when the audit is least valuable, because the picture has already been wrong for weeks.

The audit is cheap and diagnostic, so it gets deployed as a remedy. A deal moving smoothly is not audited, because there is no symptom prompting it; and a deal moving smoothly on a stale model is exactly the case where the correction is worth most, since the error has not yet compounded into a lost quarter.

At Brayford the two wrong beliefs were both available for testing in week 8. Nothing about the deal at week 8 suggested a problem — Tom was responsive, the case was progressing, and the model was confidently wrong in a way that produced no visible symptom for eight more weeks.

Run the audit on a cadence rather than on a trigger: ten minutes, fortnightly, on every deal above your median value, whether or not anything looks wrong. Deals that look fine are where stale orientation lives.

The mechanism: Diagnostics deployed on symptoms cannot detect errors that are not yet symptomatic. Because a wrong model produces confident, coherent action until it collides with reality, the interval during which correction is cheapest is exactly the interval in which nothing prompts a review — so symptom-triggered auditing systematically arrives after the cost is sunk.

8. Practice: the deal-learning loop

Exercise goal: audit your orientation on a live deal, identify the load-bearing assumptions, and design cheap tests for each.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Write your current picture of a live deal in five sentences. Do it from memory first, before opening the CRM — what you actually believe is more informative than what is recorded.
  2. Mark each sentence observed (with source and date), inferred, or assumed.
  3. Identify the load-bearing beliefs — the ones where being wrong would change your actions. Usually two of the five.
  4. Design a cheap test for each load-bearing assumption. Cheap means one question or one email, not a meeting.
  5. Measure your loop time: when did you last update this picture, and what caused the update?
  6. Install the written summary habit — a short note after every substantive conversation asking for correction. Write the template now.
  7. List what you are being fast at and slow at on this deal, against Table 3. Most sellers find they have it inverted.
  8. Set the audit cadence: fortnightly for deals over median value, ten minutes each.
  9. Run the tests and record which beliefs turned out to be wrong.

Evaluation rubric: Picture written from memory before consulting records (15%); each belief honestly marked with source and date (25%); load-bearing beliefs correctly identified (20%); tests genuinely cheap — one question or one email (20%); loop time measured (10%); fast/slow audit against Table 3 (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 1 and 2 on one deal. Most sellers are surprised to find their confident picture is two observations and three assumptions, and that they could not say where the assumptions came from.

Advanced variation: Run the orientation audit across the whole team's top deals and look at which category of belief is most often assumed. It is nearly always the economic buyer and the reason for delay — which is a systemic finding that a stage dictionary (Module 39) can enforce far more reliably than reminding people to be careful.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why is "get inside your competitor's decision cycle" bad advice for complex sales?

Because it misreads Boyd and it misreads the situation. Boyd's emphasis was on orientation, not speed, and he regarded a fast loop running on a wrong model as worse than a slow one. More importantly, the buyer is not an adversary: compressing their evaluation, pushing for decisions before readiness, and treating deliberation as an obstacle adds pressure to the exact variable that responds badly to it. In a market where most losses are no-decisions driven by fear of choosing wrongly, speed applied to the buyer produces delay.

2. What is orientation and why does it decide the outcome?

It is the model through which observations acquire meaning — accumulated experience, prior patterns, and assumptions that operate below notice. Two sellers observing "the champion has gone quiet" will act completely differently depending on it: one increases follow-up, one deprioritizes, one asks directly, one designs a test. None of them is perceiving; all are interpreting. It decides the outcome because every subsequent step inherits it, and a wrong orientation held confidently produces fast, decisive, wrong action.

3. What is the cheapest way to shorten loop time?

A written summary after every substantive conversation, asking for correction: "here's what I understood; tell me what I got wrong." It works because buyers correct written errors they would never raise verbally — interrupting someone mid-sentence is socially costly, replying to a bullet is not. It converts a private model into a shared, correctable one, and it takes four minutes. Most sellers' models decay for weeks because nothing ever forces them into contact with the buyer's version.

4. Where should a seller be fast, and where slow?

Fast at responding, surfacing bad news, disqualifying, correcting your own model, and escalating blockers you cannot resolve. Slow at proposing a solution, interpreting silence, pushing for a decision, changing a working process after one bad outcome, and reacting to a competitor's move. Most sellers have this inverted — fast at proposing and pushing, slow at disclosing and disqualifying — and the inversion accounts for a large share of avoidable losses.

5. A buyer mentions they have added other vendors. What is the correct first move?

Ask three questions before acting: who are they, whose decision was it to add them, and has something changed in the requirement. The answers frequently separate a procedural requirement — a three-quote threshold — from a genuine competitive shift, and those need opposite responses. Discounting in reaction is the worst available move: it is unconditional, it arrives before any negotiation, it signals that the price was never real, and it tells a buyer who has just added alternatives that their favoured vendor has become anxious.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

The loop is worth keeping for one idea: your picture of a deal is a model, it decays, and you will act on it with increasing confidence unless something forces it into contact with reality. Speed is a distraction; orientation is the whole game.

  • Boyd's emphasis was orientation, not speed. The popular version inverts him.
  • The buyer is not an adversary. Speed applied to their evaluation produces delay, not decisions.
  • Audit your picture: five sentences, each marked observed, inferred, or assumed.
  • Two of five will be load-bearing assumptions. Design a one-question test for each.
  • Loop time — observation to updated model — matters more than cycle length.
  • Written summaries after conversations are the cheapest loop-time improvement available.
  • Be fast at responding, disclosing, and disqualifying. Be slow at proposing, interpreting silence, and pushing.

Immediate action: Write your picture of your largest deal in five sentences from memory, then mark each observed, inferred, or assumed. Take the two load-bearing assumptions and write the single question that would test each. Ask both this week.

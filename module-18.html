18Leading a Team

Your largest lever is role clarity, not motivation.

Role ambiguity carries a meta-analytic β of −.25 against sales performance — comparable in magnitude to the strongest positive drivers, and fixable in an afternoon. This module separates coaching from inspection, builds an evidence-based forecast, and designs an operating cadence that does not depend on the manager's memory.

1. The manager's actual job

Most first-time sales managers are promoted for selling and then continue to sell — through their team, one deal at a time, in the deal review. It feels productive and it produces a team that cannot operate without them.

The evidence points somewhere less glamorous. In Verbeke, Dietz & Verwaal's meta-analysis, the single strongest negative driver of sales performance is role ambiguity at β = −.25 — larger in magnitude than cognitive aptitude or work engagement as positive drivers. Role ambiguity is uncertainty about what is expected, how performance is judged, and what good looks like. It is entirely within a manager's control, costs nothing to fix, and is almost never worked on because it does not feel like managing.

The other two top drivers — selling-related knowledge (β = .28) and adaptiveness (β = .27) — are also management outputs, because both are built by structured practice with feedback rather than by experience alone. A manager who runs deal reviews and skips call coaching is working on neither.

So the job resolves into three things: remove ambiguity, build knowledge and adaptiveness through deliberate practice, and produce a forecast that reflects reality. Everything else — pipeline reviews, one-to-ones, team meetings — is a delivery mechanism for one of those three, or it is theatre.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Measure role ambiguity on your own team and reduce it with a written expectations artifact.

Separate coaching from inspection in time, format, and purpose, and explain why mixing them destroys both.

Run a deal review that tests buyer evidence rather than seller confidence.

Produce a forecast with calibration data rather than commitment language.

Design an operating cadence with defined artifacts, owners, and frequencies.

3. Role ambiguity: the largest fixable variable

Role ambiguity is invisible to the manager, because the manager knows what they mean. It is highly visible in the variance of the team's answers.

The five-minute diagnostic

Ask every member of your team, independently and in writing, without discussion: (1) What are you expected to achieve this quarter, in numbers? (2) How will your performance be judged beyond the number? (3) What does an excellent discovery call look like here? (4) When should you disqualify a deal? (5) What is the one thing you should be spending most of your time on?

Then compare the answers. The variance is your β = −.25 problem, made visible in fifteen minutes. Most managers are shocked by question 4.

Table 1: The four dimensions of role ambiguity and their artifacts

DimensionThe question a seller cannot answerArtifact that resolves itFailure if left ambiguous
Outcome expectations"What am I actually accountable for?"A written quarterly expectation: the number, the leading activities that support it, and the non-negotiables.Sellers optimize whatever was mentioned most recently in a meeting.
Quality standards"What does good look like?"Scored exemplars: a recorded discovery call, a real business case, a mutual action plan — each annotated with why it is good.Every seller invents their own standard, and coaching becomes a matter of taste.
Decision rights"What can I decide alone?"A written threshold table: discount authority, terms deviation, disqualification, escalation.Sellers either escalate everything (slow) or nothing (risk), and both are read as judgment failures.
Priority"When everything is urgent, what wins?"A stated ranking, refreshed quarterly, with what to drop.The team defaults to whatever is loudest, which is usually the largest deal rather than the best use of capacity.

The artifact that matters most and is written least often is the disqualification standard. Sellers will not disqualify if they suspect it will be read as giving up, so pipeline fills with deals nobody believes in and the forecast becomes fiction. Writing down when a deal should be dropped — and visibly rewarding returned capacity — is one of the highest-leverage things a manager can do, and it costs one page.

4. Coaching versus inspection

These are different activities with opposite psychological requirements, and running them in the same meeting destroys both. Inspection requires the seller to defend; coaching requires them to expose weakness. A seller who has just been asked to justify their forecast will not then say "I don't know how to ask for access to the CFO."

Table 2: The two activities, separated

PurposeEstablish what is true about the pipeline.Improve a specific, observable behavior.
Question type"What buyer evidence supports this stage?""Let's listen to minute 14. What were you deciding there?"

Manager's postureSceptical. The default assumption is that the stage is overstated.Curious. The default assumption is that the seller had a reason.

What kills itAccepting seller narrative in place of buyer evidence.Listing ten faults from a whole call. One behavior, or nothing changes.

The highest-return coaching format is single-moment call coaching, and it takes twenty minutes: pick one two-minute segment of a recorded call; ask the seller to self-assess it first; name one specific alternative behavior; model it; have them rehearse it immediately; agree where they will use it next. Reviewing a whole sixty-minute call and enumerating errors produces agreement and no change, because nothing was practised.

The trap of coaching only the strugglers

Coaching capacity usually flows to the bottom of the team, where the deficit is most visible. The return is often higher in the middle: a seller at 85% of quota moved to 105% adds more than a seller at 40% moved to 55%, and is far more likely to stay. Allocate coaching deliberately rather than by squeaky wheel, and track where it actually went — most managers are surprised by the answer.

5. The operating cadence

A cadence is a set of recurring meetings, each with a defined artifact, owner, and decision. Without artifacts, meetings become status updates that consume the week and change nothing.

Two rules keep a cadence alive. Every ritual has an artifact, so preparation is visible and the meeting starts from evidence rather than recollection. And coaching is never sacrificed to pipeline pressure — the week you cancel coaching to chase the quarter is the week you tell the team which of the two actually matters, and they will believe you for a long time afterwards.

7. Weak versus improved deal review

Composite Case StudyScenario: Weekly pipeline review. A seller has a £300k deal in Commit for the third consecutive week.

Weak execution (interrogating confidence, then selling the deal yourself)

Manager: "Where are we on Northgate? Still confident?"
Seller: "Yeah, really good. Had a great call with Dan on Thursday, he's fully bought in. They love the product."
Manager: "Great. Have you pushed for a date? You need to create some urgency. Tell them our Q3 pricing expires — actually, let me jump on a call with Dan, I'll get it moving."
Seller: "Sure, that'd help."

Why it fails: "Still confident?" tests the seller's emotional state, which is the least reliable available signal. "Great call" and "fully bought in" are seller narrative with no buyer evidence behind them. The manager then supplies a manufactured deadline and takes the deal over — which closes it, perhaps, and teaches the seller nothing except that escalation is how deals move. The Commit category has also just been reaffirmed on no evidence for the third week running, which is how a forecast becomes fiction.

Improved execution (test buyer evidence, then coach the gap)

Manager: "Northgate's been in Commit three weeks. Let's test it — not your confidence, the evidence. Who is the economic buyer and when did you last speak to them directly?"
Seller: "It's the COO. I haven't spoken to her — Dan's taking it to her."
Manager: "What's the approval process, step by step, with dates?"
Seller: "Dan said it just needs her sign-off."
Manager: "Has anything in writing come back from their side — a security questionnaire, a legal review, a procurement contact?"
Seller: "Not yet."
Manager: "Then it isn't Commit and I'm moving it to Best Case — that's not a criticism, it's a category definition. Commit requires a named economic buyer we've spoken to and a dated process. We have neither.

Now the useful part. This is the fourth deal this quarter where the champion is taking it upward and you haven't met the buyer. That's a pattern and it's worth twenty minutes on Thursday. Bring me the recording of your last call with Dan and we'll work on the access conversation specifically — I don't think it's a confidence problem, I think you don't have the sentence."
Seller: "Honestly, I don't. I always feel like I'm going over his head."
Manager: "That's exactly what we'll rehearse."

Why it succeeds: Tested buyer evidence rather than seller feeling. Applied the category definition impersonally, so the downgrade is a rule rather than a judgment. Identified a pattern across deals rather than fixing one deal. Deferred the coaching to a separate protected session, keeping inspection and coaching apart. And surfaced the actual blocker — the seller lacks a sentence, not confidence — which is coachable in a way that "be more assertive" is not.

8. Practice: the manager operating cadence

Exercise goal: measure role ambiguity on your team, produce the artifacts that remove it, and design a cadence with defined outputs.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Run the five-question ambiguity diagnostic in writing, independently, with no discussion. Record the variance verbatim — do not summarize it, because the exact words are the evidence.
  2. Write the expectations artifact: the number, the leading activities, the non-negotiables, for the quarter.
  3. Produce three quality exemplars. Real artifacts from your own team — a discovery call, a business case, a mutual action plan — annotated with why each is good.
  4. Write the decision-rights table: discount authority, terms deviation, disqualification, escalation thresholds.
  5. Write the disqualification standard and decide how you will visibly reward returned capacity.
  6. Separate coaching from inspection in the calendar — different days, different formats, explicitly named.
  7. Design one single-moment coaching session in full: which call, which two minutes, the self-assessment question, the alternative behavior, and the rehearsal.
  8. Audit your coaching allocation from the last month's calendar. Where did it actually go?
  9. Set up forecast calibration: record a probability per deal, score it quarterly against outcomes.

Evaluation rubric: Ambiguity diagnostic run in writing with verbatim variance recorded (25%); expectations artifact with leading indicators and non-negotiables (15%); three real annotated exemplars (15%); decision-rights table including disqualification authority (15%); coaching and inspection separated in the calendar (15%); one coaching session designed to the two-minute segment (15%).

Beginner variation: Run question 4 of the diagnostic only — "when should you disqualify a deal?" The variance in the answers, and the number of people who believe they are not permitted to, is usually enough to justify the whole exercise.

Advanced variation: Run the diagnostic across three teams and compare variance by manager. Role ambiguity is a manager-level variable, and the differences between managers on the same team structure, selling the same product, are usually large and rarely discussed.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why is role ambiguity described as the largest fixable variable in sales management?

Because it carries a meta-analytic β of −.25 against sales performance — comparable in magnitude to the strongest positive drivers — and unlike cognitive aptitude or market conditions it is entirely within a manager's control. It can be materially reduced in an afternoon by writing down four things: outcome expectations, quality standards with exemplars, decision rights, and priority. It goes unaddressed because it is invisible to the manager, who knows what they mean, and because fixing it does not feel like managing.

2. Why should coaching and pipeline inspection never happen in the same meeting?

Because they require opposite psychological states. Inspection asks the seller to defend their assessment; coaching asks them to expose a weakness. A seller who has just justified a forecast will not then admit they do not know how to ask for access to a CFO — so the coaching becomes advice-giving, which changes nothing. Separate them by day and by format, and protect the coaching session, because cancelling it under quarter-end pressure tells the team precisely which one you consider real.

3. A seller says a deal is going "really well" and the champion is "fully bought in." What should the manager ask?

For buyer evidence rather than seller assessment: who is the economic buyer and when did you speak to them directly; what are the approval steps and their dates; what has come back from their side in writing — a security questionnaire, a legal review, a procurement contact. Enthusiasm and rapport are seller-side signals and are uncorrelated with outcome. If the evidence is absent, apply the category definition impersonally and move the deal, so the downgrade is a rule rather than a judgment on the individual.

4. Why does writing a disqualification standard usually improve attainment?

Because sellers do not disqualify when they suspect it will be read as giving up, so pipeline fills with deals nobody believes in, capacity is spread across them, and the forecast becomes fiction. A written standard makes dropping a deal a correct action rather than an admission — especially when disqualified deals are reported weekly as good work and the recovered capacity is named. Pipeline typically falls, sometimes sharply, and attainment rises, because the same effort is now concentrated on deals that can close.

5. Where should coaching capacity be allocated, and where does it usually go?

It usually goes to the bottom of the team, where the deficit is loudest — commonly two-thirds or more of available coaching time. The higher return is often in the middle: moving a seller from 85% to 105% adds more absolute revenue than moving one from 40% to 55%, and the middle performer is more likely to stay. Audit your own calendar for where coaching time actually went last month, then allocate deliberately rather than by whoever is most visibly struggling.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Sales management is mostly the unglamorous work of making expectations explicit, practising specific behaviors, and refusing to accept narrative in place of evidence. None of it feels like the job people are promoted for, and all of it outperforms the job they were promoted for.

  • Role ambiguity (β = −.25) is your largest fixable variable. Measure the variance in your team's answers.
  • Write the four artifacts: expectations, quality exemplars, decision rights, priorities.
  • The disqualification standard is the most under-written and highest-leverage of the four.
  • Separate coaching from inspection in time and format, and never cancel coaching for pipeline pressure.
  • Coach one two-minute segment with a rehearsal, not a whole call with a list of faults.
  • Test buyer evidence, not seller confidence. Apply category definitions impersonally.
  • Allocate coaching deliberately; audit where it actually went.

Immediate action: Ask every member of your team, in writing and independently, "when should you disqualify a deal?" Do not discuss it first. The variance in the answers — and the number who believe they are not allowed to — will tell you more about your team's performance ceiling than this quarter's pipeline report.

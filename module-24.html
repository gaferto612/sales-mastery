24Procurement

Procurement is not the enemy. It is a function with a mandate.

Sellers who treat procurement as an obstacle to be routed around get worse terms, longer cycles, and occasionally disqualified. This module explains what procurement is measured on, what they can and cannot do, and how to trade with them rather than against them.

1. What procurement is actually for

Most sellers describe procurement as the department that arrives at the end to take money away. That description is inaccurate in a way that costs deals.

Procurement exists to manage three things the business unit is not equipped to manage: commercial risk (liability, termination, data protection, business continuity), compliance (competition rules, anti-bribery, modern slavery, sanctions, and — in the public sector — statutory process), and value across the portfolio (avoiding six overlapping contracts for the same capability, consolidating spend, maintaining supplier competition).

Price is one instrument among those, and often not the one they are most rewarded for. A procurement professional who negotiates 12% off a contract that later fails a security audit has not had a good year. This matters practically: a seller who arrives with only a discount to offer is negotiating on the one dimension where procurement is strongest and least interested in your differentiation.

Two structural facts follow. First, procurement's leverage comes almost entirely from substitutability — the more interchangeable you appear, the more they can extract. Everything you did in discovery and business-case work is what makes you less substitutable, which is why deals with weak value cases get squeezed hardest. Second, procurement is usually brought in after the business decision, which means their mandate is to validate and extract, not to select. Understanding which of those two roles they are playing determines your entire approach.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Identify what a specific procurement function is measured on and adapt your approach accordingly.

Recognize the standard procurement tactics and respond without conceding or becoming adversarial.

Build a trade matrix of non-price variables scored by cost to you and value to them.

Prepare the paper process in advance so it does not become the timeline.

Maintain the business relationship through the commercial phase without going around procurement.

3. The mandate: what they are measured on

Procurement functions differ enormously, and the first question of any engagement is which kind you are dealing with.

Table 1: Four procurement postures and how to work with each

PostureMeasured onHow to recognise itWhat works
Savings-drivenReported savings against a benchmark or previous price.Opens with a target discount; asks for benchmarks; introduces late.Give them a reportable win that is not pure margin — term commitment, payment timing, scope structure, a documented rate card. They need a number to report.
Risk-drivenContract risk, supplier failure, audit findings, regulatory exposure.Leads with the MSA, security questionnaire, DPA, business-continuity questions.Arrive with the evidence pack complete. Speed and completeness here buy more goodwill than price movement.
Category-drivenPortfolio rationalization, supplier count, spend consolidation.Asks what else you do, what you overlap with, who else in the organization uses you.Show the consolidation case. Being one of three suppliers replaced by one is a stronger position than being the cheapest.
Process-driven (regulated / public sector)Statutory compliance and defensibility of the award.Formal tender documents, quiet periods, written-questions-only rules.Follow the process exactly. Contacting evaluators outside official channels can disqualify you outright. See Module 42.
The question that identifies the posture

Ask directly, early, and without irony: "What does a good outcome on this look like for you, beyond price?" Most sellers never ask, and most procurement professionals answer honestly — because it is the first time anyone has treated their objectives as legitimate. The answer tells you which of the four postures you are in and therefore which currency you should be trading in.

One further distinction is worth establishing early: are they selecting or validating? If the business unit has already chosen and procurement is validating the commercial terms, your job is to make the validation easy and defensible. If procurement is genuinely running a selection, the criteria and weighting are the whole game and you need them in writing. Getting this wrong — treating a validation as a selection, or vice versa — wastes weeks.

5. Building the trade matrix

The single most useful preparation for a procurement engagement is a matrix of everything you can trade besides price, scored on cost to you and value to them. Your trades come from the low-cost, high-value quadrant, and there are always more of them than sellers expect.

Table 3: Trade variables and where each sits

Contract termLow (if churn risk is low)High — budget certainty, fewer renewals to processThe most reliable trade in the matrix. Pair with a capped uplift.

Rate card for future purchasesLowHigh — extends their savings claim beyond this dealGives a savings-driven buyer something reportable at almost no margin cost.

Consolidation of existing spendLowVery high for category-driven functionsReplacing three suppliers with one is a headline result for them.
Reference / case studyVery low — you want itLow–mediumBetter traded with marketing than with procurement, but still free.
Service levelsMediumHigh for risk-driven functionsOnly commit to SLAs your delivery organization has agreed to.
Liability capHighHighNever trade for price. Trade only against matching commitment, with legal approval.
Termination for convenienceHighHighConsider a notice period and a wind-down fee rather than a flat refusal.
The paper process is a timeline, not a formality

Legal review, security questionnaires, data-processing agreements, insurance certificates, supplier onboarding portals, and three-quote thresholds routinely add four to eight weeks after the commercial decision. Sellers who discover this in the final month lose the quarter to administration. Ask early what the post-decision steps are and how long the buyer's last comparable purchase took, then run them in parallel rather than in sequence. This is frequently worth more elapsed time than any negotiation tactic.

7. Weak versus improved procurement conversation

Composite Case StudyScenario: First formal call with procurement, who open with a benchmark claim and a deadline.

Weak execution (defend, concede, then escalate around them)

Procurement: "I'll be straight with you — we have comparable quotes 30% below this, and I've got a mandate to close a 15% gap."
Seller: "I understand, but I really think you're comparing apples and oranges here. Our platform does a lot more than the alternatives. Look — let me see what I can do. I could probably get you 10% if that helps move things along?"
Procurement: "That's a start. Get me the revised quote and I'll take it to the team."
[Seller then emails the business sponsor: "Procurement are being difficult — can you help push this through?"]

Why it fails: "Apples and oranges" disputes the comparison without asking what is in it, so nothing is learned and the objection is not resolved. An unconditional 10% arrives before any trade, teaching procurement that pressure produces movement and that the list price was soft. And going around procurement to the sponsor is the move that most reliably converts a professional counterpart into an adversary — they will hear about it, and they control the paper process you now depend on.

Improved execution (investigate, ask their objective, trade conditionally)

Procurement: "We have comparable quotes 30% below this, and I've got a mandate to close a 15% gap."
Seller: "That's a big gap, and I'd genuinely like to see it — which products, and at what scope and term? If it's a like-for-like comparison then I've got a real problem to solve rather than a comparison problem, and I'd rather know.

Before that though, can I ask something I probably should have asked earlier: what does a good outcome on this look like for you, beyond the price? I ask because if all I've got is discount, I'm negotiating on the one dimension where you're strongest and I'm least useful."
Procurement: "Honestly? I need a number I can report. But what I actually care about is not being back here in twelve months negotiating an uplift, and we've got two overlapping contracts in this category I'd love to kill."
Seller: "That's genuinely useful, and it changes what I'd offer. Three things. Seven percent is available on a three-year term rather than annual — that's a real cost difference for us, not a gesture. A CPI-capped uplift for the full term, so you're not back here. And if the two overlapping contracts are what I think they are, I can scope replacing both, which gives you a consolidation result on top of the savings number.

What I can't do is fifteen percent flat. That's below where we can deliver this properly, and I'd rather tell you now than have it fail in month four."

Why it succeeds: The benchmark was tested rather than disputed or accepted. The objective question was asked and answered honestly, revealing that the reportable number was only one of three things they wanted — and the other two cost the seller almost nothing. Every concession carried a condition and a rationale. And the refusal was framed around the buyer's outcome rather than the seller's margin, which is a reason a procurement professional can accept and repeat internally.

8. Practice: the procurement trade matrix

Exercise goal: prepare a procurement engagement so thoroughly that the conversation becomes execution rather than improvisation.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Identify the posture. For a live deal, determine whether procurement is savings-, risk-, category-, or process-driven, and state the evidence. If you do not know, that is your first action.
  2. Selecting or validating? Establish which role they are playing and how you know.
  3. Build the trade matrix: at least eight non-price variables, each scored cost-to-you and value-to-them. Identify the low-cost / high-value quadrant.
  4. Map the paper process: every post-decision step, its owner, and an elapsed estimate taken from the buyer's last comparable purchase.
  5. Identify what you can send now — security pack, insurance, MSA, DPA — to move steps off the critical path.
  6. Pick your two contract battles and write what you will concede gracefully. Never put a liability cap in the "trade for price" column.
  7. Write your responses to the four standard tactics: unsubstantiated benchmark, final squeeze, deliberate delay, and standard-terms-are-non-negotiable.
  8. Set your reservation value in writing, approved in advance (Module 08).
  9. Write the objective question you will ask, and predict their answer. Then ask it and compare.

Beginner variation: Build the trade matrix only. Most sellers discover they have been treating a multi-variable negotiation as though price were the only currency, and simply listing eight alternatives changes their behavior permanently.

Advanced variation: Interview a procurement professional — ideally at a customer, or someone in your own company's procurement function — about what they are measured on and what sellers get wrong. Thirty minutes with an actual procurement professional will teach you more than this module can, and they are almost always willing.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Procurement claims to have comparable quotes 30% below yours. What is your first move?

Ask what is in them: which products, what scope, what term, what support tier. Gaps that size are usually scope or structure differences rather than genuine price gaps, and testing the comparison costs nothing. Very often the benchmark is never produced, which is itself informative. If it turns out to be genuinely like-for-like, you now have a real problem to solve and you know it — which is far better than having conceded 30% to a comparison that was never valid.

2. Why is going around procurement to the business sponsor a serious error?

Because procurement controls the paper process you depend on, and they will hear about it. It converts a professional counterpart into an adversary at precisely the moment you need them to move quickly through legal, security, and onboarding. It also signals that you regard their mandate as illegitimate, which is both inaccurate — they manage real commercial and compliance risk — and self-defeating, since the things they care about beyond price are frequently cheap for you to provide.

3. What single question most improves a procurement engagement?

"What does a good outcome on this look like for you, beyond price?" Most sellers never ask, and the answer routinely reveals objectives that cost you very little: a capped uplift so they are not renegotiating in twelve months, a rate card that extends their savings claim, consolidation of overlapping contracts, or simply a number they can report. Trading in those currencies protects margin while giving procurement a better internal result than the flat discount they were mandated to seek.

4. Why should you never trade a liability cap for price?

Because liability is an asymmetric, potentially existential exposure, while price is bounded and recoverable. Unlimited liability on a £400k contract can produce a loss orders of magnitude larger than the deal, and it is the kind of term that is invisible until the day it is not. It also requires legal judgment rather than commercial judgment, which is why it should never be conceded under quarter-end pressure by someone without authority. Trade it only against matching commitment — term, volume, scope — and only with legal approval.

5. Why does the paper process deserve as much preparation as the negotiation?

Because it routinely adds four to eight weeks after the commercial decision, and those weeks are pure elapsed time with no negotiating value. Legal review, security questionnaires, DPAs, insurance certificates, supplier onboarding portals, and three-quote thresholds can all run in parallel with the commercial conversation if you send the materials early — or sequentially at the end if you do not. Asking how long the buyer's last comparable purchase took, and sending the security and legal pack unprompted before you are asked, is frequently worth more elapsed time than any tactic in this module.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Procurement rewards preparation and professional respect, and punishes improvisation and evasion. The seller who arrives knowing what the function is measured on, holding eight tradeable variables, and having already cleared the paper process will outperform one who arrives with a discount and a deadline.

  • Procurement manages commercial risk, compliance, and portfolio value. Price is one instrument among those.
  • Identify the posture — savings, risk, category, or process — and trade in that currency.
  • Establish whether they are selecting or validating. The approaches are different.
  • Ask what a good outcome looks like beyond price. Two of the three answers usually cost you nothing.
  • Test benchmarks, never concede to them. They frequently do not exist.
  • Pick two contract battles and concede the rest gracefully. Never trade a liability cap for price.
  • Send the security and legal pack before you are asked. The paper process is the timeline.
  • Never go around procurement to the sponsor.

Immediate action: For your largest open deal, find out who the procurement contact will be and send them the security pack, insurance certificates, and standard MSA now, with a note explaining you would rather none of it sat on the critical path later. This costs an hour and routinely removes several weeks.

26Performance & Sustainability

Judge the process. The outcome is partly not yours.

Selling combines high outcome variance with visible public scoring, which is a reliable formula for burnout and for learning the wrong lessons. This module builds process-based self-assessment, honest loss review, and routines that survive a bad quarter.

1. Why selling is psychologically unusual

Three features combine in selling that rarely combine elsewhere, and together they explain most of what people call the "mental game."

High outcome variance with a long feedback lag. A well-run enterprise deal can be lost to a budget freeze, a reorganization, or an acquisition — none of which you influenced, and several of which you could not have known about. A badly-run deal can close because the incumbent had an outage in week nine. Outcomes are noisy signals of process quality, and the noise arrives months after the work.

Public, continuous scoring. Attainment is visible on a leaderboard, discussed in meetings, and reset every quarter. Very few professions publish a running score of each individual's contribution.

Repeated rejection as a normal input. A functioning outbound motion has a low reply rate by design. Treating each non-response as a personal verdict is arithmetically absurd and psychologically automatic.

The combination produces two characteristic failures. The first is burnout. The second, less discussed and more expensive, is learning the wrong lesson: a seller who wins a badly-run deal concludes their process works, and a seller who loses a well-run deal concludes it does not. Over a career, outcome-based self-assessment in a high-variance environment teaches you approximately nothing — and sometimes teaches you the reverse of the truth.

The response is not positive thinking. It is changing what you evaluate. Evaluate the process, which you control and which is observable immediately; treat outcomes as a lagging, noisy indicator to be reviewed in aggregate rather than case by case.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Separate process quality from outcome and score yourself on the former.

Run a loss review that identifies a controllable cause without either self-blame or excuse.

Recognize the difference between a slump caused by process decay and one caused by variance.

Design routines that hold under pressure, when they are most needed and most likely to be dropped.

Produce a personal performance operating system with leading indicators and a review cadence.

3. Separating process from outcome

The 2×2 that makes this concrete is borrowed from decision analysis in poker and investing, where the same problem — good decisions with bad outcomes — is well understood.

Table 1: Process versus outcome

Good processDeserved win. Extract the pattern and repeat it. This is the only cell you should try to reproduce.Bad luck. Budget froze, sponsor left, company was acquired. Change nothing. The most commonly mishandled cell — sellers "fix" a process that was working.
Bad processDangerous win. Single-threaded, no economic buyer, closed anyway. Reinforces a habit that will fail at scale. Review this as if you had lost.Deserved loss. The only cell that should change your behavior. Also the easiest to learn from, if you are honest.
The two cells everyone gets wrong

Good process / bad outcome gets over-corrected: a seller loses a well-run deal to an acquisition and changes their discovery approach, degrading something that worked. Bad process / good outcome gets ignored entirely, because nobody reviews a win — and so a single-threaded, unqualified deal that closed by luck becomes the template. Reviewing your wins with the same scepticism as your losses is the single most useful habit in this module.

4. The loss review that produces learning

Most loss reviews produce one of two useless outputs: an excuse ("they went with the cheaper option") or a flagellation ("I should have pushed harder"). Neither identifies a controllable cause, and neither changes behavior.

Table 3: The five questions of a working loss review

QuestionWhat it surfacesDiscipline required
1. What did the buyer actually say?The stated reason, verbatim, not your interpretation of it.Ask them. Rep-reported loss reasons default to price at a rate that does not survive independent interviews (Module 31).
2. When was it actually lost?The decision point, which is almost always months before the notification.Work backwards through the timeline. The moment is usually a missing conversation, not a final objection.
3. What did I know then, and what could I have known?Separates unavoidable uncertainty from avoidable ignorance.Being honest that the information was available and you did not ask.
4. What is the controllable cause?One thing, in your control, that you would do differently.Resisting both "nothing, bad luck" and "everything, I'm bad at this."
5. Is this a pattern?Whether this is variance or a systematic gap.Requires looking at the last ten losses together. One loss teaches almost nothing.
One loss is noise; ten losses are data

The single most common error in loss review is over-fitting to the most recent, most painful case. In a high-variance environment a single loss carries very little signal — you cannot distinguish bad luck from bad process from one observation. Review losses in batches of ten, looking for the repeated element. That is where the pattern lives, and it is usually one specific thing: no economic buyer contact, no confirmed problem statement, no disqualification discipline.

5. Routines that survive a bad quarter

Routines fail exactly when they are most needed. Under pressure, the activities that produce future pipeline are the first to be cut in favour of chasing present deals — which guarantees the next quarter is worse, which increases the pressure. This is the structural mechanism of most sales slumps, and it is a design problem rather than a discipline problem.

Table 4: Designing routines for the bad weeks

PrincipleWhyImplementation
Protect the leading activity firstPipeline creation has the longest lag and is therefore always the rational thing to cut in the short term — and always the wrong thing.A fixed block that is never moved. If it moves once under pressure, it will move every time.
Use implementation intentionsSpecifying when, where, and how substantially raises follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, d ≈ 0.65).Not "do more prospecting" but "Tuesday and Thursday, 08:30–10:00, at my desk, from the scored list, phone first."
Score the process weeklyOutcomes arrive too late to steer by; process scores arrive immediately.Six criteria from Table 2, scored Friday, on paper, in five minutes.
Separate the review from the emotionReviewing a loss on the day you lose it produces attribution, not analysis.Log the facts immediately; run the five questions at a fixed weekly time.
Make recovery a rule, not a decisionJudgment about rest is worst when rest is most needed.Pre-committed boundaries: a stop time, a day off, a limit on evening email. Written down when calm.
Keep a "what I control" list visibleAnxiety attaches to outcomes; action attaches to inputs.A physical card: conversations initiated, problems diagnosed, stakeholders contacted, artifacts produced, deals disqualified.

One further point on the slump, because it is worth diagnosing rather than enduring. A variance slump and a process slump look identical from inside and require opposite responses. If your process scores have held and your leading activity volume has held, you are in variance — the correct action is to change nothing and wait, which is psychologically very difficult and almost always right. If your process scores have declined, you are in a process slump, and more effort at a decayed process makes it worse. The weekly process score is what tells you which one you are in.

The mechanism worth noticing: Seller A's collapse was not caused by the bad quarter. That trade always looks rational in the moment and is almost always wrong, which is why the block has to be protected by rule rather than by judgment.

7. Weak versus improved self-assessment

Composite Case StudyScenario: A seller has just lost a large deal they expected to win, and is talking it through with a peer.

Weak execution (attribution instead of analysis)

Seller: "I don't know what happened. I did everything right — great relationship with Dan, he loved it, the demo went really well. They just went with the cheaper option in the end. Honestly I think procurement had it in for us from the start. Maybe I should have pushed harder at the end, or dropped the price sooner. I don't know. I'm starting to think I'm just not good at the big ones."

Why it fails: Three incompatible explanations in one paragraph — bad luck, a hostile procurement function, and personal inadequacy — none of which is checked against evidence. "They went with the cheaper option" is the default rep-reported loss reason and is frequently not the real one. "Great relationship with Dan" is the seller's assessment of a coach. And the conclusion, "I'm not good at the big ones," is a global trait attribution drawn from a single observation, which is the reasoning error most likely to end a career.

Improved execution (process score first, then the controllable cause)

Seller: "It stings, so I'm going to do the process score before I decide what it means.

Qualified against ICP: yes, fit and trigger both scored. Diagnosed before proposing: yes — I've got the problem statement they confirmed in writing. Disclosed a limitation: yes, the reporting gap, in the second meeting. Asked for the honest no: yes, twice. Economic buyer contacted directly: no. That's the one. Dan said he was taking it to the COO and I accepted that for four months.

So when was it actually lost? Probably in February, when I accepted 'Dan's taking it up' instead of testing whether he could. Not in the final week.

Could I have known? Yes — he'd deflected the introduction twice by then and I read that as him being protective rather than him having no route.

Controllable cause: I don't test the champion's standing. And I want to check whether that's a pattern, so I'm pulling the last ten losses tomorrow rather than deciding from this one.

I'll also ask them what actually happened. 'Cheaper option' is what people say."

Why it succeeds: The process score came first, before any interpretation, and it isolated one failed criterion out of six rather than producing a global verdict. The timeline question moved the loss from the final week to February, which is where the controllable cause lives. The seller distinguished what they knew from what was available. And crucially they refused to draw a conclusion from one case, deferring to a ten-loss review — which is the difference between analysis and rumination.

8. Practice: your performance operating system

Exercise goal: build a personal system that evaluates what you control, learns from batches rather than incidents, and holds under pressure.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Write your process criteria — six, specific to your motion, each with observable evidence. Use Table 2 as a starting point, not a template.
  2. Score your last ten closed deals (won and lost) against them, and place each in the Table 1 grid.
  3. Examine the dangerous wins. Which deals closed on a bad process? What habit are they reinforcing?
  4. Run the five loss questions on the batch of losses, not on the most recent one. Identify the repeated element.
  5. Name your leading activity — the one with the longest lag between doing it and being paid for it. That is the one you will be tempted to cut.
  6. Write it as an implementation intention: when, where, how, and with what materials. Fixed block, never moved.
  7. Write your recovery rules while calm: stop time, days off, evening boundaries. These are decisions made in advance precisely because you will make them badly under load.
  8. Make the "what I control" card — five inputs, physical, visible.
  9. Set the weekly review: five minutes, fixed time, process scores only. Decide now what would tell you the difference between a variance slump and a process slump.

Evaluation rubric: Six process criteria with observable evidence (20%); ten deals scored and placed in the grid, including wins (20%); dangerous wins examined honestly (15%); pattern identified from the batch rather than the latest loss (20%); leading activity named and protected by an implementation intention (15%); slump test defined in advance (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 1 and 5 only. Writing six process criteria and identifying the activity you cut first under pressure is most of the value, and it takes half an hour.

Advanced variation: Run this as a team exercise. Score the team's last thirty closed deals into the grid and look at the bad process / good outcome cell together. That cell is where a team's bad habits are manufactured and celebrated, and nobody looks at it because everything in it is a win.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why is outcome-based self-assessment unreliable in sales?

Because outcome variance is high and the feedback lag is long. A well-run deal can be lost to a budget freeze or an acquisition you could not have known about; a badly-run one can close because the incumbent had an outage. So an outcome is a noisy signal of process quality, arriving months late — and judging yourself by it teaches you roughly nothing, or occasionally the reverse of the truth. Score the process, which you control and which is observable within days, and treat outcomes as a lagging indicator reviewed in aggregate.

2. Which cell of the process/outcome grid is most dangerous, and why?

Bad process / good outcome — the deal that closed despite being single-threaded, unqualified, or undiagnosed. It is dangerous because nobody reviews a win, so the habit is reinforced rather than examined, and it will fail as soon as conditions get harder or deal sizes get larger. Reviewing your wins with the same scepticism as your losses is the single most useful habit in this module, and almost nobody does it.

3. Why review losses in batches of ten rather than individually?

Because one loss carries very little signal in a high-variance environment — you cannot distinguish bad luck from bad process from a single observation, and the most recent loss is also the most emotionally loaded, which makes over-fitting to it almost inevitable. A batch of ten reveals which criterion fails repeatedly. That repeated element is the real, controllable finding, and it is usually something specific: no economic-buyer contact, no confirmed problem statement, no disqualification discipline.

4. How do you tell a variance slump from a process slump, and why does it matter?

By your weekly process scores and leading-activity volume. If both have held, you are in variance and the correct action is to change nothing and continue — which is psychologically very hard and almost always right. If a process score has declined, you are in process decay, and the fix is that one specific thing rather than more effort. It matters because the two require opposite responses: adding effort to a variance slump usually means cutting the long-lag activity to fund short-lag chasing, which manufactures the next slump.

5. Why must the leading activity be protected by a rule rather than by discipline?

Because under pressure it is always rational in the short term to cut it. Pipeline creation has the longest lag between doing the work and being paid for it, so when this quarter is at risk, the obviously sensible move is to redirect that time to chasing open deals — and that move guarantees the next quarter is worse, which increases the pressure further. A fixed block that never moves removes the decision from a moment when your judgment is systematically distorted. If it moves once, it will move every time.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

The inner game is not about attitude. It is about changing the unit of evaluation from something noisy and delayed to something observable and controlled — and then designing routines that hold on the weeks when you would otherwise abandon them.

  • Outcomes are noisy and lagged. Score the process; review outcomes in aggregate.
  • Good process with a bad outcome means change nothing. Bad process with a good outcome means review it as a loss.
  • Run loss reviews on batches of ten. One loss is noise.
  • Find the controllable cause and the moment it was actually lost, which is rarely the final week.
  • Ask the buyer what happened. "They went cheaper" is what people say, not usually what happened.
  • Protect the long-lag activity by rule, not by judgment.
  • Write your recovery boundaries in a good week, to be obeyed in a bad one.

Immediate action: Write your six process criteria today and score your last ten closed deals against them — wins included. The pattern in the losses will be one thing, and the wins in the bad-process cell will tell you which habit is quietly being reinforced.

02Decision Psychology

Influence is compressed evidence, or it is a lie.

The persuasion principles taught in most sales courses replicate unevenly, and several have been overstated for decades. This module separates what survives scrutiny from what does not, and rebuilds influence as the honest compression of real evidence about a real situation.

1. Why "sales psychology" needs an audit

Sales psychology is the part of the profession most contaminated by confident nonsense. Two decades of replication work in psychology have not been kind to the field's borrowed material: several famous priming effects failed to reproduce, ego-depletion shrank dramatically under scrutiny, and effect sizes taught as universal turned out to be small, moderated, and sometimes reversible. Meanwhile sales training kept teaching the 1984 version of the story with growing certainty.

The professionally useful response is neither credulity nor cynicism. It is grading. Some findings are robust and large — conformity effects are among the most reliable results in social psychology. Some are real but modest and heavily moderated, like scarcity. Some never had support at all, like the idea that buyers reveal a preferred sensory modality through their word choice.

Underneath the grading sits a single ethical principle that makes the whole topic tractable: influence is legitimate when it compresses evidence the buyer would endorse if they had time to gather it themselves, and illegitimate when it substitutes for evidence that does not exist. Telling a buyer that three comparable organizations reached a specific result is compression — the information is true, relevant, and checkable. Inventing the three organizations is fraud. Implying capacity constraints you do not have is fraud with better manners. The technique is identical; the evidence behind it is not.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Grade each influence principle by its replication evidence and state the boundary condition under which it weakens or reverses.

Identify status quo bias, omission bias, loss framing, and choice overload in a real buying situation.

Apply the compression test to distinguish legitimate influence from manipulation in your own material.

Use the trust equation to diagnose why a specific relationship is not progressing.

Produce an ethical influence audit of your team's live sales assets, with rewrite recommendations.

Cialdini's principles are the most widely taught model in commercial persuasion. They are worth knowing precisely — and worth grading, because they do not all carry the same weight.

These effects are small and probabilistic in aggregate, while the reputational damage from one detected fabrication is large and permanent. A manufactured scarcity claim might move an r = 0.28 effect a little; being caught costs you the account, the reference, and — in a connected industry — the segment. The expected-value arithmetic favours honesty even before ethics enters the room.

4. Decision biases that actually change buying

Ignore the long lists of a hundred cognitive biases. Four do most of the work in real commercial decisions, and three of the four push in the same direction: toward doing nothing.

Table 2: The four biases that decide most deals

BiasMechanismHow it shows up in a dealCounter-move that respects autonomy
Status quo bias (Samuelson & Zeckhauser)Disproportionate preference for the current state, independent of its merits."We'll revisit next year." The buyer agrees with everything and changes nothing.Quantify the cost of the current state as a running total, not the benefit of change. Make inaction visible rather than change attractive.
Omission biasErrors of action feel worse than errors of inaction, even at equal cost.The buyer would rather under-perform quietly than be the person who chose the failed vendor.Shrink the size of the reversible bet: pilot scope, phased rollout, exit terms, success criteria agreed in writing.
Loss framing (Tversky & Kahneman)Losses loom larger than equivalent gains in evaluation."Save £200k" lands differently from "avoid losing £200k you are already losing."Frame honestly around what is already occurring. Manufacturing fear about a loss that is not happening is manipulation.
Choice overloadMore options raise the cost of deciding and can suppress decision entirely.Six packages, twelve add-ons, and an open-ended pilot scope produce a stalled evaluation, not a considered one.Make a recommendation. "Based on what you described, I'd take option B and not C — here is why, and here is when I'd be wrong."

The commercial significance of this cluster is easy to underrate. Dixon & McKenna's analysis of roughly 2.5 million recorded sales conversations found that in about 56% of deals lost to "no decision," the buyer wanted to change but could not carry the decision through. This is a vendor dataset (class V) and the percentage should be held loosely — but the mechanism is independently supported, and it inverts the standard seller reflex.

Why adding urgency to an indecisive buyer makes things worse

If a buyer is stalled because they are unconvinced, more evidence helps. If they are stalled because they are afraid of choosing wrongly, more evidence and more urgency raise the stakes of the decision — and omission bias pushes them further toward doing nothing. The evidence-consistent moves are the opposite of pressure: narrow the options, make an explicit recommendation you will stand behind, and remove downside through pilot scope, staged commitment, and honest disclosure of what your product does badly. Modules 09 and 10 build this into a working method.

5. The trust equation and the arithmetic of self-orientation

Maister, Green & Galford's trust equation is a practitioner framework (class F), not a measured law — but it is unusually good at diagnosing why a specific relationship is stuck, because it separates four things that sellers usually blur together.

Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-orientation

Table 3: Diagnosing a stalled relationship with the trust equation

TermWhat it meansSymptom when it is the weak termRepair
CredibilityDo they believe what you say about the subject?Your claims get independently verified before they are acted on; technical people are brought in to check you.Demonstrate domain knowledge, cite checkable sources, and state the limits of your knowledge before you are caught at them.
ReliabilityDo you do what you said, when you said?They chase you for things you promised; deadlines slip by a day and nobody mentions it.Make fewer promises and keep all of them. Reliability is the cheapest trust term to fix and the most commonly neglected.
IntimacyIs it safe to tell you something inconvenient?You learn about the competitor, the budget cut, or the internal opposition from someone else, late.React well to bad news — visibly. The first time a buyer tells you something awkward and you handle it without flinching, this term jumps.
Self-orientation
(the denominator)		Whose interest is visibly driving your behavior?Your follow-ups cluster at quarter-end; you argue when they raise a risk; you never recommend against yourself.The only term that divides. Halving it does more than doubling any other. The strongest single move is to recommend against your own interest once, credibly.

Because self-orientation is the denominator, the arithmetic is unforgiving: a seller with excellent credibility, perfect reliability, and obvious self-interest scores worse than a modest seller who is visibly working the buyer's problem. This is the mathematical form of a thing every experienced buyer knows instinctively.

6. Worked example: the same tactic, ethical and unethical

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A B2B software company runs an end-of-quarter push. Two account executives use the same four influence principles on comparable accounts. One passes the compression test; one does not. Twelve months later the difference is visible in the numbers.

Table 4: Identical tactics, opposite evidence bases, opposite outcomes

Social proof"Everyone in your industry is moving to this." No names, no numbers, no comparability."Three organizations with your ERP and headcount ran this. Two hit the result, one didn't — their data quality wasn't ready. I can introduce you to all three."AE 1: three deals closed, two churned at renewal citing "not what we were told." AE 2: two deals closed, both expanded, both became references.
Scarcity"This pricing expires Friday." It did not; the same offer ran the next quarter."Our implementation team has two Q1 slots; after that you're into April. Pricing is unchanged either way."AE 1: two buyers discovered the offer repeated and disengaged mid-cycle. AE 2: one buyer accelerated genuinely, for a real reason.
Authority"We're the market leader in this space." Based on a paid analyst placement."We're strong on the reconciliation workflow and weak on multi-entity consolidation. If consolidation is your priority, look at the other two."AE 1: credibility collapsed when procurement pulled independent market data. CommitmentA ladder of small agreements engineered so that declining felt inconsistent: "You did say efficiency was a priority…"A written mutual action plan with named owners and dates, which either party could revise, and an explicit "either of us can stop here."AE 1: two champions privately felt cornered and stopped responding. Takeaway: The tactics were the same. The evidence behind them was not. Manipulation is not a different set of techniques from influence — it is the same techniques with the evidence removed, and its costs are simply deferred past the commission date. Any compensation system that pays out before the deferred costs land will keep producing AE 1s; Module 41 addresses that design flaw directly.

The Brayford thread · Week 6 · the reference question

Compression, and what it cost

Priya asks the question every buyer asks: "who else like us is doing this?"

The compressed, legitimate answer: three operators with comparable site counts and dual-system estates cut same-day agency bookings by 19% to 31% over six months. The 31% case had an unusually large bank pool and is not the expectation. And a fourth operator got nothing at all — their bank-staff records lived across three systems and never became usable, which is the first thing worth checking at Brayford given the Marlow acquisition.

Every element of that is checkable, which is what makes it influence rather than assertion. The negative case is offered before it is asked for.

The manufactured version was available and would have worked better in the room: "most groups your size have already moved on this." It would also have been unverifiable, and Brayford's procurement function pulls independent market data at week 20. The compression test is not a moral preference here — it is a prediction about what happens fourteen weeks later.

7. Weak versus improved use of social proof

Composite Case StudyScenario: A buyer at a regional healthcare provider asks, "Who else like us is using this?"

Weak execution (unfalsifiable social proof)

Seller: "Loads of providers like you — we work with some of the biggest names in healthcare. Most of the market is moving this way now. I can't name them because of confidentiality, but trust me, you'd recognize them."

Why it fails: Every element is unverifiable by design. "Some of the biggest names" hides comparability — a 40,000-bed national system tells a regional provider nothing. "Most of the market is moving this way" is a bandwagon assertion with no denominator. And confidentiality invoked as a blanket shield is the oldest tell in the profession; a sophisticated buyer hears "there are no comparable references." Worse, the claim invites a check the seller will fail.

Improved execution (comparability, method, and the negative case)

Seller: "Two are genuinely comparable to you — same bed count band, same EHR, both multi-site. One went live 14 months ago and cut medication-reconciliation time by 31%; that was measured against their own pre-implementation baseline over eight weeks, and I can send you their methodology. The second is at month four and hasn't hit their target yet — their integration team was pulled onto an EHR upgrade. I'd introduce you to both, including the one that's behind, because their problem is the one most likely to be yours. There's a third I'd normally mention, but they're a specialty clinic and I don't think their numbers transfer."

Why it succeeds: Named comparability criteria, a stated measurement method, an offered introduction, a volunteered negative case, and an explicitly excluded non-comparable reference. Every element is checkable, which is exactly what makes it persuasive. The seller has also pre-empted the buyer's real question — what goes wrong? — rather than waiting to be caught out by it.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

The characteristic failure is not fabrication. It is comparability drift: a seller assembles a genuinely true reference set, then uses it for buyers it does not fit, because rebuilding the set for each segment is work and the existing one is available.

Every claim stays technically accurate. The three named operators really did achieve those results. But cited to a single-site operator, or to a group whose bank pool is a quarter the size, the same true statement now implies a result that will not occur — and the seller cannot see the drift, because nothing they said was false.

This is the mechanism by which honest people build dishonest reference decks. The check is not "is this true?" but "is this true for the person I am saying it to?" — and the moment you notice you are reaching for a reference because it is impressive rather than because it is comparable, the drift has already happened.

The mechanism: Social proof depends on perceived similarity, not on accuracy. A true claim about a non-comparable referent is not a weaker version of the effect — it can invert it, because the buyer's inference is not "this works" but "this vendor does not understand my situation," which damages every subsequent claim.

8. Practice: the ethical influence audit

Exercise goal: audit the influence claims your organization actually makes, classify each against the compression test, and produce rewrites for the ones that fail.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Collect ten live influence claims from real assets: website, email sequences, deck, pricing page, call recordings. Take them verbatim.
  2. For each, name the principle it invokes and the evidence class of the claim behind it.
  3. Apply the compression test: would the buyer endorse this claim if they had gathered the underlying evidence themselves? Mark PASS, WEAK, or FAIL.
  4. For every FAIL, write the rewrite that would make it pass — or delete it if no true version exists.
  5. For every WEAK, identify the specific evidence that would upgrade it, and who could produce it.
  6. Run the reversal test on your three strongest claims: could you say this to the buyer's face, with the buyer's own analyst in the room, and be comfortable? If not, it fails regardless of what the compression test said.

Completed audit exemplar (five of ten rows)

Claim as publishedPrincipleVerdictRewrite or action
"Join 5,000+ companies who trust us."Social proofWEAK — true but non-comparable; includes free-tier sign-ups."1,240 paying teams; 310 in financial services, where our median deployment is 6 weeks." Segment-specific and checkable.
"Offer ends Friday" (recurring weekly).ScarcityFAIL — the constraint does not exist.Delete. Replace with the real constraint if one exists: onboarding capacity, cohort dates, or an approved price change with a date.
"The #1 platform for mid-market retail."AuthorityFAIL — ranking is from a paid placement in a vendor-funded report."Rated highest for ease of implementation in [named report, 2025], which is vendor-funded — here's the methodology and the two categories where we ranked third."

Evaluation rubric: Accurate principle identification (15%); correct evidence classification (20%); rigorous and honest application of the compression test (25%); quality and truthfulness of rewrites (25%); willingness to mark your own organization's material as FAIL (15%). That last criterion is not decorative — an audit that finds nothing wrong is an audit that was not performed.

Beginner variation: Audit five claims from a company you do not work for. It is far easier to see the failures from outside, and the skill transfers.

Advanced variation: Extend the audit into the compensation and pricing system. Which incentives make FAIL-class claims rational for the individual seller? A recurring "offer ends Friday" is usually not a copywriting decision — it is a symptom of a quarterly bonus structure. Recommend the structural fix, not just the copy fix.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Which Cialdini principle has the strongest replication evidence, and what is its main boundary condition in selling?

Social proof. Bond's meta-analysis of 125 Asch-type conformity studies found a weighted mean effect around d = 0.89, and a 2023 replication reproduced conformity rates close to the original. The boundary condition that matters commercially is comparability: the effect depends on the referent group being seen as genuinely similar. Citing a global bank to a 40-person clinic does not deliver a weak version of the effect — it can deliver none at all, or a negative one, because the buyer concludes you do not understand their situation.

2. A buyer has agreed with your business case for three months and still has not decided. Should you add more ROI evidence or more urgency?

Probably neither. Agreement plus inaction over three months points at indecision rather than unconvinced-ness — the pattern Dixon & McKenna associate with roughly 56% of no-decision losses. More evidence addresses a problem they do not have; more urgency raises the perceived stakes of choosing wrongly, which omission bias converts into further delay. The evidence-consistent moves are to reduce the option set, make an explicit recommendation you will stand behind, and shrink the size of the bet — a smaller first phase, agreed exit criteria, a documented rollback.

3. Your product genuinely has two implementation slots left this quarter. Is saying so manipulation?

No. It passes the compression test: the constraint is real, and a buyer who gathered the information themselves would reach the same conclusion. Two conditions keep it legitimate. The constraint must remain true — if a slot frees up, tell them. And it must be stated neutrally, as information rather than as pressure: "there are two Q1 slots; after that you're into April, and pricing is unchanged either way." The moment you attach a fabricated consequence to the real constraint, you are back across the line.

4. Why does self-orientation sit in the denominator of the trust equation rather than as a fourth negative term?

Because its effect is multiplicative, not additive. High self-orientation does not subtract a fixed amount of trust — it discounts everything else you have built. A seller with strong credibility, perfect reliability, and visible self-interest is trusted less than a mediocre seller who is obviously working the buyer's problem, because the buyer reinterprets every prior good act in light of the motive. The practical consequence is that reducing self-orientation is the highest-leverage move available, and the fastest way to do it is to recommend against your own interest once, credibly, where it costs you something.

5. A colleague argues that since scarcity has a measurable effect (r ≈ 0.28), fake deadlines are justified by results. Where does the argument break?

It breaks on expected value before it breaks on ethics. An r ≈ 0.28 effect is small-to-moderate and heavily moderated; the gain from a fake deadline is a marginal shift in probability on some deals. The cost of detection is the account, the reference, the segment reputation, and — under consumer-protection regimes such as the FTC's dark-patterns enforcement — potential legal exposure. Buyers now actively test scarcity claims, so detection probability is not low. The argument also ignores reactance: a detected fake deadline does not return you to neutral, it moves you below where you started.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Sales psychology becomes usable the moment you stop asking "does this technique work?" and start asking "what evidence stands behind this claim, and would the buyer endorse it if they had gathered that evidence themselves?" That question grades your material faster than any framework.

  • Grade influence principles by replication evidence: social proof is strong, scarcity is real but modest, unity is least tested.
  • Influence is compressed evidence. Remove the evidence and the same technique becomes manipulation.
  • Three of the four biases that decide deals push toward inaction — so pressure is usually the wrong instrument.
  • For an indecisive buyer, narrow the options, recommend explicitly, and shrink the bet.
  • Self-orientation divides. Reducing it beats improving anything else in the trust equation.
  • An influence audit that finds nothing wrong was not performed.

Immediate action: Take the single strongest social-proof claim your team makes and add three things to it today — the comparability criteria, the measurement method, and one honest negative case. Then watch what the next buyer asks about; it will be a better question than the one they were asking before.

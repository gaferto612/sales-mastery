02Psychology of Selling

Why buyers actually say yes.

People buy with emotion and justify with logic. Every neuroscience study confirms it — the limbic system fires before the prefrontal cortex when a buying decision is made. This module is the engine room.

People buy with emotion, justify with logic

This isn't a marketing cliché — it's a measurable neurological reality. The limbic system fires before the prefrontal cortex in buying decisions. Logic comes in afterward to justify what emotion already chose.

This doesn't mean logic is irrelevant. It means emotion opens the door and logic walks through it. A sale needs both.

Sell to the heart, then arm the head.

Cialdini's 7 Principles of Influence

Robert Cialdini spent decades studying what makes people say yes. His seven principles are the periodic table of persuasion. Click each to explore.

The Seven Principles
Tap to explore
i.
Reciprocity

Humans feel obligated to return favors. Give first — value, information, samples, attention — and the buyer is psychologically inclined to give back.

Application: Free trials, valuable content, genuine help before the pitch.

⚠ Manipulative reciprocity (forced gifts to create guilt) breaks trust fast.
ii.
Commitment & Consistency

Once people commit, especially publicly or in writing, they act consistently with that commitment.

Application: Get small yeses first. Ask the buyer to articulate their own goals — they'll then resist contradicting themselves.

iii.
Social Proof

We look to others' behavior to decide our own, especially under uncertainty.

Application: Testimonials, reviews, case studies, "10,000 customers," celebrity endorsements.

⚠ Vague proof now hurts credibility. "Sarah, dentist in Aalborg, 6-month customer" > "John D., satisfied customer".
iv.
Authority

People defer to credible experts.

Application: Credentials, media features, expertise demonstrated through teaching, awards. Authority must be shown, not claimed — "as featured in" with real logos > "industry-leading expert."

v.
Liking

We say yes to people we like. Liking is driven by: similarity, compliments, cooperation, attractiveness.

Application: Find genuine common ground. Mirror language. Actually care about the person — fake liking is detected.

vi.
Scarcity

Things in short supply feel more valuable. Loss aversion is roughly twice as motivating as gain.

Application: Limited inventory, time-bound offers, exclusive access.

⚠ Fake scarcity destroys trust. Don't pretend a permanent product is "running out."
vii.
Unity

The strongest version of social proof: people don't just like or follow groups they belong to — they identify with them. "We" beats "they."

Application: Build a community/identity around the product. "For founders," "for runners," "for parents like us."

The big cognitive biases that drive buying

Beyond Cialdini, these biases reliably affect decisions. Recognizing them in yourself first makes them easier to address in buyers.

Loss Aversion

Losing €100 hurts roughly twice as much as gaining €100 feels good. Frame offers as preventing loss, not just adding gain.

Anchoring

The first number heard anchors all later ones. Show €5,000 first; €2,000 then feels reasonable.

Endowment Effect

Once people feel ownership, they overvalue. Test drives and free trials create pre-ownership.

Status Quo Bias

People prefer not changing. Your competition isn't always another product — it's often "do nothing."

Decoy Effect

Adding a slightly worse option makes the target option look better. Classic Economist subscription test.

Paradox of Choice

Too many options paralyze. 3 options beats 30. Tier complex offerings.

Peak-End Rule

People remember the peak emotional moment and the ending more than the middle. End every interaction strong.

Trust: the master variable

Without trust, no technique works. With trust, you barely need techniques. The Trust Equation (Maister, Green, Galford) breaks it down into measurable parts.

Reading the signals

How do you know someone's getting close to "yes"? And what tells you they're pulling away? Watch for these.

→ Buying signals
  • Switch from "if I bought this" to "when I have this"
  • Asking about delivery, setup, onboarding
  • Asking about pricing details a second time
  • "What's the next step?"
  • Asking about guarantees or returns
  • Leaning forward, picking up the product
  • Showing the offer to someone else nearby
  • Online: repeat visits to pricing page, time on testimonials
← Resistance signals
  • Crossed arms, leaning back, short answers
  • "Let me think about it"
  • Sudden price focus after agreeing on value
  • Bringing in objections they didn't have before
  • Going silent on email after engagement
  • Asking the same question twice (wasn't listening)
  • Checking phone/clock repeatedly
  • "Send me information" instead of meeting
Critical

When you see buying signals, stop pitching and start closing. Continuing to sell after the buyer is sold is the #1 way to lose deals.

The empathy edge

The single most underrated sales skill is tactical empathy — accurately reading and naming what the other person is feeling.

When you say to a buyer, "It sounds like you're worried this won't work for your team specifically," and you're right, three things happen:

  1. They feel understood
  2. Their guard drops
  3. They tell you what's actually going on

Most sellers project. Empathetic sellers receive. Practice this and your close rate jumps without changing anything else.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing pressure with persuasion
  • Using fake scarcity or fake social proof
  • Treating every buyer the same emotionally
  • Pitching before establishing trust
  • Continuing to talk past the buying signal
  • Ignoring the buyer's fear of looking stupid

Exercises

  1. Cialdini audit. Take an ad or sales page you remember. Identify which of the 7 principles it uses. Which could it add?
  2. Trust score. Score yourself on credibility, reliability, intimacy, self-orientation in a recent sale. What's your weakest link?
  3. Empathy drill. In your next conversation, name the emotion you sense in the other person out loud. Notice what happens.
  4. Bias spotting. Find 3 examples of anchoring, decoy effect, or loss aversion in product pricing this week.

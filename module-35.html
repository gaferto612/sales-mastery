35Account Strategy

Put scarce selling time where it can compound.

1. Practical introduction

Territory and account planning is the discipline of allocating scarce resources—time, attention, executive sponsorship, and technical capacity—to the opportunities most likely to yield durable value. It is used by account executives, key account managers, and founders when the number of potential targets exceeds their capacity to pursue them deeply.

When performed badly, account planning degenerates into completing templates for management. Sellers chase recognizable logos with low propensity to buy, while ignoring smaller, highly qualified accounts. A strong territory strategy treats revenue size as only one factor, weighing it against strategic fit, access, timing, and cost to pursue.

2. Observable learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Calculate realistic selling capacity.
  • Prioritize accounts using fit, potential, propensity, and access.
  • Map whitespace across an existing or target customer.
  • Formulate falsifiable account hypotheses.
  • Evaluate the opportunity cost of pursuing a low-propensity account.
  • Review and exit accounts that consume capacity without yielding evidence.

3. The logic of prioritization

Resource allocation requires distinguishing account potential (how much they could theoretically spend) from propensity to buy (how likely they are to change). A Fortune 500 manufacturer may have enormous potential, but if they just signed a five-year contract with a competitor, their propensity is near zero. Pursuing them now carries a massive opportunity cost.

Effective account segmentation requires observing trigger events—leadership changes, acquisitions, regulatory shifts, or new product launches—that disrupt the status quo. In SaaS and mid-market sales, coverage models dictate how many accounts a seller can work simultaneously. In enterprise and professional services, a single key account may consume a seller's entire year. In all contexts, ethical planning means disqualifying early when strategic fit is poor, rather than forcing a mismatch.

Scoring systems help organize data, but they should support seller judgment, not replace it. If the score says an account is top-tier but the seller has no access and no hypothesis, the score is misleading. Conversely, a mid-sized account with an urgent trigger event and strong champion access often deserves more immediate focus than a stagnant enterprise giant.

4. Step-by-step territory planning

  1. Audit capacity. Calculate available selling hours. Subtract internal meetings, admin, and travel. Determine how many deep account cycles you can realistically sustain.
  2. Define the ideal customer profile (ICP). List the firmographic, technographic, and behavioral criteria that predict durable success.
  3. Segment and tier the territory. Group accounts by potential and propensity. Assign tiers (e.g., Tier 1 for deep account-based selling, Tier 2 for targeted campaigns, Tier 3 for monitoring).
  4. Identify trigger events. Scan Tier 1 and 2 accounts for recent changes that create urgency or re-open closed decisions.
  5. Map the whitespace. For existing customers, identify divisions or workflows not yet using the solution. For prospects, map known problems against your capabilities.
  6. Formulate account hypotheses. Draft a specific, testable claim about why the account needs to change now.
  7. Set objectives and action plans. Define the next verifiable buyer commitment and the resources required to secure it.
  8. Establish review and exit criteria. Define what evidence would prove the hypothesis wrong, and when you will demote the account to free up capacity.

Complete account plan: TechParts Ltd.

  • Account context: Mid-sized electronics manufacturer expanding their primary facility to handle a new automotive contract.
  • Strategic hypothesis: The facility expansion will strain their current manual QA process, causing delays that jeopardize the new contract. Our automated QA routing can prevent this bottleneck.
  • Customer priorities: On-time delivery for the automotive client; maintaining QA standards without tripling headcount.
  • Current relationship: VP of Operations (Sarah) is a former champion from a previous company.
  • Whitespace: Currently using our basic sensor package; the routing software is whitespace.
  • Competitive situation: In-house spreadsheets and one low-cost local vendor.
  • Risks: IT may block cloud deployment; Sarah may lack budget authority for software.
  • Objectives: 1. Validate the QA bottleneck hypothesis. 2. Identify the economic buyer.
  • Planned actions: Present the diagnostic to Sarah (Oct 12). Request workflow mapping session with IT and QA leads (Oct 20).
  • Evidence gaps: We do not know the cost of a delayed shipment or IT's security requirements.
  • Review date: Oct 25.
  • Exit criteria: If Sarah cannot secure a meeting with IT and the economic buyer by Oct 25, demote to Tier 2 and pause active pursuit.

6. Weak versus improved account plan

Weak plan:
Goal: Sell €500K to MegaCorp.
Strategy: Send weekly emails to the CEO. Drop by their office. Offer a 20% discount. Leverage our industry-leading AI features.
Why it fails: This is a wish list, not a plan. It lacks a verifiable hypothesis, ignores customer context, relies on generic outreach without existing access, and contains no exit criteria. It will consume capacity indefinitely.

Improved plan:
Goal: Determine if MegaCorp’s recent acquisition of SmallCo creates a supply-chain integration problem we can solve.
Strategy: Interview our existing contacts at SmallCo to map their current integration pains. Use those insights to draft a targeted hypothesis for MegaCorp’s VP of Supply Chain. Request a 15-minute verification call.
Why it succeeds: It is specific, evidence-based, leverages existing access (SmallCo), and sets a clear, testable next step. If the hypothesis is wrong, the seller learns quickly and moves on.

7. Common mistakes and warning signs

  • Whale hunting without a harpoon: Spending 80% of capacity on the largest account in the territory despite having no access and no trigger event. Fix: Enforce strict exit criteria for inactive large accounts.
  • The static template: Writing an account plan in January and never updating it. Fix: Treat the plan as a living document; update it after every material customer interaction.
  • Confusing activity with progress: Logging 50 unanswered emails as "working the account." Fix: Measure progress by verified buyer commitments, not seller activity.
  • Ignoring the whitespace: Treating an existing customer as "closed" while competitors sell into adjacent departments. Fix: Map the organization chart and actively search for unserved workflows.

8. Practice: allocate scarce capacity

Objective: Rank accounts and justify resource allocation using evidence, not just revenue size.

Composite Case Study

Scenario: You sell cybersecurity compliance software. You can only actively work three accounts this month. You have five targets:

  1. Bank A: €2M potential. Happy with current vendor. No known access.
  2. Fintech B: €300K potential. Just failed a compliance audit (public news). You know the CTO.
  3. Credit Union C: €150K potential. Asked for a demo yesterday. High fit.
  4. Insurer D: €800K potential. Competitor contract expires in 90 days. You have a mid-level contact.
  5. Crypto E: €1.5M potential. Rapid growth, but their tech stack is incompatible with your software.

Instructions: 1. Rank the top three accounts to pursue actively. 2. Explain why the other two were deprioritized. 3. Build a short account plan for your #1 target, including a hypothesis, risk, and exit criteria.

Evaluation rubric: Prioritization logic and tradeoff reasoning (40%); Evidence-based hypothesis (30%); Realistic risk assessment (15%); Clear exit criteria (15%).

Strong partial exemplar

"Crypto E is deprioritized despite its €1.5M size because the incompatible tech stack means strategic fit is low; pursuing them would require massive custom engineering. Bank A is deprioritized because, without a trigger event or access, the cost to pursue is too high."

9. Applied knowledge check

Test your judgment on territory prioritization, capacity limits, and account hypotheses. Look for the answers that protect seller time and demand verifiable evidence.

10. Practical conclusion

A territory strategy is an exercise in sacrifice. By deliberately choosing which accounts not to pursue, you concentrate your resources on the opportunities where you have the highest probability of creating mutual value. Account plans are not administrative chores; they are hypotheses waiting to be tested by customer reality.

  • Score fit and propensity, not just potential.
  • Use trigger events to time your approach.
  • Build falsifiable account hypotheses.
  • Set strict exit criteria to recover wasted capacity.

Immediate action: Review your current top three active accounts. For each, write down the specific trigger event that makes them likely to buy now. If you cannot name one, reconsider their priority.

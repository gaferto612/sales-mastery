04Offline Selling

Selling face to face.

Online is asynchronous — you publish, they consume, they decide. Offline is synchronous — every second is happening in real time, and your tone, posture, breathing, and pace carry information that no text ever can.

Why offline is different

The implications of synchronous selling:

  • Higher bandwidth — body language, energy, micro-expressions all carry information
  • Higher pressure on both sides — you can't edit, neither can they
  • Trust forms faster or fails faster — people decide within 7 seconds, often unconsciously
  • The salesperson IS the medium — your presentation matters more than your slides
If you can sell in person, you can sell anywhere. If you've only ever sold online, you'll struggle in person until you train it.

The first 30 seconds

The opening sets everything. The buyer decides whether to trust you before you finish your second sentence.

0–3s

Smile genuinely

Not a customer-service smile — a "I'm pleased to see you" smile. The micro-difference is everything.

3–6s

Open posture

Arms uncrossed, hands visible, body squared toward them. Project relaxed confidence.

6–10s

Confident pace

Walk into the encounter, don't drift. Make eye contact for 2–3 seconds, then break naturally.

10–30s

Open with service, not pitch

"How can I help today?" beats "Hi, I'm Mike from [company] and we're offering..."

Common mistake

Leading with a pitch. Pros lead with a question or observation: "It looks like you're shopping for someone specific — gift?" works far better than "These are our best sellers."

Body language — read and use

You're being read whether you mean to or not. Conscious use of body language is half the offline game.

Retail selling

The customer walked in. That's a strong signal — they have some level of interest. Your job is to convert it without scaring them off.

Acknowledge, don't ambush

"Welcome — let me know if you'd like a hand." Give them space. Customers hate being attacked at the door.

Open with observation

After they've browsed a minute: "I see you're looking at the [X] — are you replacing one or new to it?" Opens conversation without pressure.

Guide, don't sell

Ask what they're trying to accomplish. Recommend based on that, including options that don't make you the most money. Trust earned here closes today and earns referrals.

Retail pro tips

  • Stand at a 90-degree angle to the customer, not facing them directly. Feels less confrontational.
  • Hand them the product. Endowment effect — once it's in their hands, ownership starts.
  • Ask "How will you be using it?" — tells you everything about which model to suggest.
  • When they say "I'm just looking" — fine. Give them a path back: "Take your time, I'll be right over there."

Door-to-door & cold field sales

Brutal but trainable. Used in solar, security, B2B services, fundraising. The difference between top reps and average reps isn't knock count — it's what happens at the door in the first 10 seconds.

Opening framework

  1. Pattern interrupt. They expect a sales pitch. Don't give one. "Hi, I'm Sara — I'm not selling anything door-to-door, but I was hoping you could help me with something quick."
  2. Reason for being there. Specific, true. "We just installed a system three doors down for the Petersens."
  3. Permission. "Have you got 60 seconds, or is this a bad time?"
  4. Qualify fast. Are they the right person? Any interest?
  5. Set next step. Usually not the sale — a demo, quote, or callback.
D2D rules

Smile before they open the door (they see you through the peephole). Step back from the door (less threatening). Don't take the first "no" as final — but also don't pester. One graceful reframe, then move on. Track everything — your conversion ratios are your career.

Events, trade shows, conferences

Different game: people came expecting to be sold to, but with their guard up against the boring booths.

  • Stand at the edge of your booth, not behind a table. Tables are barriers.
  • Open with a question, not your product. "What brought you to the show this year?"
  • Don't rush into a pitch. Ask permission, understand the visit, and adapt the amount of help to the customer.
  • Capture details for follow-up. Most deals close in the two weeks after the show, not at it.
  • Free swag should require a micro-commitment (email, business card) to filter in qualified leads.

Voice & tone

Your voice is half your in-person presence.

⤓
Pace · slower than feels natural. Nervous sellers speed up.
⤵
End sentences with downward tone. Rising tone sounds uncertain.
∅
Pauses. After a question, shut up. Silence pulls answers out.
∼
Match the environment. Loud showroom = louder; quiet office = softer.

Exercises

  1. Mirror practice. Watch a video with sound off. Read each person's body language. Then with sound on — check.
  2. Pause drill. Ask a question, count to 5 before saying anything. Notice what fills the silence.
  3. Open-handed greeting. For one week, greet every interaction with palms visible. Notice shifts.
  4. Retail observation. Visit 3 stores as a customer. Rate the salespeople on greeting, listening, recommendation, close attempt. Steal the good moves.

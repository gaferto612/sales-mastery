33Discovery

Diagnose before you prescribe.

Conduct a mutual investigation of the current state, desired state, causes, consequences, constraints, and decision—not an interrogation designed to force pain.

1. Contract the conversation

Agree on purpose, time, roles, and possible outcomes. Give the buyer permission to say there is no fit. State what you need to understand and ask what they need from the conversation. This makes qualification reciprocal.

2. Build a causal problem map

Move from symptoms to mechanisms. Map workflow, trigger, frequency, variation, people, systems, failure modes, downstream effects, and prior attempts. Separate facts, estimates, and interpretations. A diagnosis explains why a result occurs; it does not merely rename the symptom.

3. Use questions as a sequence

SPIN is a useful practitioner framework: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff. Use it flexibly. Research basic situation facts before the call. Ask implication questions only to clarify real consequences, never to manufacture fear. Let the buyer define value in their language.

4. Listen, summarize, and test

Attend, clarify, paraphrase, reflect uncertainty, and verify. Summarize the current state, impact, desired state, constraints, and unanswered questions. Ask, “What did I get wrong?” A good summary can be corrected; a performance cannot.

5. Qualify the change

Test whether the problem is material, the desired result coherent, the customer able to change, your solution able to help, and the buying effort justified. Explore competing priorities, resources, authority, timing, and implementation. Disqualify respectfully when evidence is missing.

6. Artifact: discovery dossier

Create an agenda, question map, causal workflow, qualification record, and shared diagnosis memo. Score accuracy, listening, relevance, and buyer autonomy.

7. Conversation lab: diagnosis under uncertainty

Discovery is not a fixed list of questions. It is a joint attempt to determine whether a meaningful change should happen and whether the seller can responsibly help. The output is a testable shared diagnosis, not a completed CRM form. In transactional retail, this may take two minutes. In enterprise selling, it may require separate conversations with users, finance, security, procurement, and executives.

Prepare without pre-deciding

Before the conversation, record what is known, assumed, and unknown. Research the account, role, likely workflow, relevant alternatives, and public triggers. Write two or three hypotheses, including one that would make the opportunity unsuitable. Prepare questions by decision area rather than memorizing a script: current process, trigger, consequences, desired result, prior attempts, stakeholders, constraints, decision, implementation, and evidence.

Contract the conversation

Opening

“We have 35 minutes. I’d like to understand how this process works today, what prompted the review, and what a worthwhile change would require. I can then explain where we may or may not fit. If there is no useful next step, we can say that directly. What would make this conversation useful for you?”

The contract reduces ambiguity without pretending the seller has no commercial interest. If the buyer asks for price immediately, answer the question as far as possible, explain the variables honestly, and request permission to learn enough to avoid a misleading estimate.

Weak and improved discovery

Buyer: “We are mostly researching.”
Weak seller: “Great. How many users do you have? What is your budget? Are you the decision-maker?”
Buyer: “Just send information.”

The seller treats curiosity as an opportunity, asks seller-centered qualification questions, and earns little context.

Improved seller: “That makes sense. What started the research, if anything?”
Buyer: “Our operations lead asked us to compare tools, but there is no approved project.”
Seller: “What is happening in the current process that made a comparison worth someone’s time?”
Buyer: “Month-end errors. We do not know whether software or process discipline is the answer.”
Seller: “Then a product evaluation may be premature. Would it help to map where the errors enter and what you have already tried?”

The improved seller does not manufacture urgency. The conversation may end with diagnosis, a referral, a later review, or no sale.

Use SPIN as a map, not an interrogation

SPIN—situation, problem, implication, need-payoff—came from a large practitioner research program in complex selling. Its useful lesson is sequencing: understand context, investigate difficulty, clarify real consequences, and let the buyer articulate value. It is not a requirement to ask every prepared question. Too many situation questions waste time; exaggerated implication questions can become fear-based pressure; need-payoff questions can sound artificial when the buyer has not accepted the diagnosis.

Listen in observable behaviors

  • Allow the buyer to complete the thought; do not use silence merely as a tactic.
  • Follow important words rather than rushing to the next scripted question.
  • Distinguish fact, estimate, interpretation, and emotion in your notes.
  • Summarize mechanism and uncertainty: “Errors appear after three manual transfers, but we have not heard from compliance or measured frequency.”
  • Ask for correction: “What is inaccurate or missing?”
  • Record commitments and open questions, not private psychological diagnoses.

Research synthesizing salesperson-listening studies associates listening with adaptive and relational outcomes, but listening is not passive agreement. A customer-centered seller can challenge an assumption, disclose poor fit, or recommend a different investigation.

Diagnose the change, not only the pain

A credible opportunity needs more than dissatisfaction. Test the mechanism, material consequence, ownership, desired state, alternatives, priority, ability to implement, acceptable risk, and decision process. In enterprise settings, one contact cannot reliably speak for the whole buying group. In retail, avoid turning a simple purchase into an interview. In founder-led sales, separate genuine repeated demand from requests for custom development.

Common failure modes

  • Asking questions whose only purpose is to lead toward the product.
  • Hearing a symptom and immediately prescribing a feature.
  • Using talk-time ratios without reviewing question quality and understanding.
  • Accepting the first quantified estimate without owner, period, source, or confidence.
  • Calling a friendly contact a champion before they act internally.
  • Ending with a demo despite unresolved fit or no meaningful next decision.

8. Practice: discovery dossier and call review

Objective: conduct and evaluate a discovery conversation that produces an accurate, mutual diagnosis.

Composite Case Study

Composite educational scenario: A 120-person logistics company is evaluating workflow software after repeated invoice disputes. The operations manager agreed to talk but says the company is “just researching.” Finance believes the cause is poor data entry; operations blames customer changes; IT expects an integration problem.

  1. Write known facts, assumptions, unknowns, and a no-fit hypothesis.
  2. Create a 35-minute mutual agenda and a question map.
  3. Role-play the buyer and seller; the buyer must withhold at least one important fact until asked well.
  4. Produce a causal map showing workflow, failure points, consequences, stakeholders, and contrary explanations.
  5. Write a 150-word shared-diagnosis email that invites correction.
  6. Review the recording and mark each interruption, premature recommendation, unsupported inference, useful follow-up, summary, and buyer correction.

Expected output: preparation sheet, recording or transcript, causal map, qualification record, and shared-diagnosis email.

Evaluation criteria: preparation and relevance 15%; question sequencing 20%; listening evidence 25%; diagnostic accuracy and uncertainty 20%; autonomy and ethical qualification 10%; next-step quality 10%.

Advanced variation: conduct separate finance and IT conversations, then reconcile conflicting diagnoses without pretending consensus exists.

Strong partial answer

“We heard three competing explanations for invoice disputes. Operations described late customer changes; finance identified inconsistent entry; IT has not verified the integration assumption. Before evaluating software, the team will sample twenty disputed invoices and classify the failure point. If transfer errors are not material, our workflow product may not be appropriate.”

Beginner variation: use a ten-minute role-play focused only on contracting, one causal follow-up, and an accurate summary. Self-review: Which statement was fact, estimate, interpretation, or emotion? Where did you recommend too early? What did the buyer correct? Which missing stakeholder or evidence could reverse the diagnosis?

9. Applied knowledge check

1. A buyer describes a serious problem but has not measured it or assigned an owner. What is strongest?

Propose a small joint diagnostic action and keep the opportunity unqualified until evidence improves. An immediate close overstates readiness; automatic disqualification may be premature. Refusal to investigate or a confirmed low consequence could change the next step.

2. A contact asks for a demo before discussing workflow. Must the seller refuse?

Answer the request respectfully, clarify what they need to evaluate, and scope a short demo around stated criteria. A rigid refusal creates friction; a full feature tour may create false fit. Existing requirements, role, and buying stage determine how much discovery is needed.

3. Finance blames data entry while operations blames customer changes. What belongs in the shared diagnosis?

Record both hypotheses, supporting evidence, disagreement, and a test. Choosing the senior view hides uncertainty; averaging them invents consensus. A representative error sample may reveal different causes by segment.

4. The seller’s product fits the symptom but cannot address the confirmed cause. What is strongest?

Disclose the limitation and recommend a better investigation, smaller scope, referral, or no sale. Reframing the cause to fit the product is unethical. New evidence that the product affects a material part of the mechanism could justify reconsideration.

Immediate action: Review one recent call, locate the first unsupported interpretation, and write the follow-up question that would have tested it.

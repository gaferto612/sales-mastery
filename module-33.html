33Discovery

Diagnose before you prescribe.

Discovery is a mutual investigation of a situation, not an interrogation designed to manufacture pain. This module builds the contracting move, the causal problem map, question sequencing, and the discipline of separating what you know from what you assume.

1. What discovery is, and the two ways it fails

Discovery is the joint construction of an accurate picture of the buyer's situation — accurate enough that either party could conclude no change is warranted. That last clause is what separates it from everything that impersonates it.

Failure one: the interrogation. The seller works through a list — budget, authority, need, timeline, current vendor, contract end date — extracting information that serves their CRM. The buyer experiences an audit conducted by someone who has not yet been useful, and answers minimally. This is qualification wearing discovery's clothes (Module 01), and buyers can tell.

Failure two: the pain manufacture. The seller asks escalating implication questions designed to make a minor annoyance feel catastrophic, because a training programme taught that the job is to "create urgency." Sophisticated buyers recognise the pattern within two questions, and it converts a consultative conversation into a defensive one.

Both failures share a root: the seller has decided the conclusion before the investigation. A genuine diagnosis can come out the other way — the problem is smaller than assumed, the cause is something you do not address, the timing is wrong. A seller who cannot reach those conclusions is not diagnosing.

The commercial argument for doing it properly is strong. Selling-related knowledge is the single strongest meta-analytic driver of sales performance (β = .28), and discovery is where that knowledge is acquired for a specific account. Conversation-intelligence datasets consistently associate winning discovery calls with roughly 11–14 questions, a talk-to-listen ratio near 46/54, and coverage of three to four problems rather than one — these are observational vendor figures (class V) and should be read as coaching ranges, not targets, but the direction is consistent with everything else in this module.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Contract a discovery conversation so that disqualification is a legitimate outcome for both parties.

Build a causal problem map that reaches a mechanism rather than renaming a symptom.

Sequence questions from situation through to consequence without manufacturing urgency.

Separate fact, estimate, and interpretation in your own notes and in the buyer's statements.

Produce a discovery dossier the buyer confirms in their own words.

3. Contracting the conversation

The first two minutes determine what kind of conversation follows. Contracting means stating the purpose, the time, the roles, and — crucially — the acceptable outcomes, including the one where you go away.

Table 1: The four elements of a contract

ElementWhat you sayWhat it does
Purpose"My aim is to understand how this actually works for you, well enough to tell you honestly whether we're relevant."Sets an investigative rather than persuasive frame, and commits you to a judgment.
Time and shape"Thirty minutes. I'll ask most of the questions for the first twenty, then tell you what I think."Removes ambiguity, which is one of the largest sources of avoidable pressure (Module 28).
Reciprocity"What do you need out of this conversation? I'd rather cover that than my list."Makes it mutual. Also frequently redirects the session usefully.
Permission to disqualify"If it turns out we're not a fit, I'd rather find that today than in six weeks — and I'll say so."The load-bearing element. A seller who cannot say no is not trusted when they say yes.
Why the permission clause changes the answers you get

A buyer who believes the seller must win the deal filters what they say — they withhold the budget problem, the internal opposition, the fact that they already prefer a competitor, because disclosing it invites pressure. A buyer who has heard you volunteer that no-fit is an acceptable outcome discloses more, earlier. The clause costs a sentence and materially changes the quality of your information, which is the actual product of the meeting.

4. The causal problem map

The core discipline is moving from symptom to mechanism. A symptom is what someone notices; a mechanism explains why it occurs. Most discovery stops one level too early and renames the symptom in vendor vocabulary — "so you have a data visibility problem" — which feels like progress and is not.

Table 2: The layers of a problem map

LayerQuestionExampleFailure at this layer
1. SymptomWhat do they notice?"Month-end close takes seven days."Accepting this as the problem and proposing against it.
2. WorkflowWhat actually happens, step by step, and who does it?Three people export from two systems, reconcile in a spreadsheet, chase exceptions by email.Not asking. Most sellers never learn the actual sequence.
3. Trigger and frequencyWhen does it occur, how often, and what varies?Every month; worse in quarter-end months; worst when the acquired entity's data is included.Treating an intermittent problem as constant, which inflates any subsequent model.
4. MechanismWhy does it happen?The two systems use different cost-centre codes, so ~8% of lines fail to match and each requires manual investigation.Stopping here is the goal; stopping before it is the most common failure in selling.
5. ConsequenceWhat does it cost, and who bears it?~46 person-hours per cycle; the Finance Director reports late to the board; two people work weekends in quarter-end months.Estimating the cost yourself rather than having the buyer supply it.
6. Prior attemptsWhat have they already tried, and why did it not work?Built a matching macro in 2023; it broke when the chart of accounts changed and nobody maintained it.Skipping this. Prior attempts predict both objections and implementation risk.
7. ConstraintsWhat cannot change?The acquired entity's system is contractually fixed for two more years.Designing a solution that violates a constraint nobody mentioned because nobody asked.

Two disciplines make the map trustworthy. Label every entry fact, estimate, or interpretation — "46 person-hours" is an estimate unless someone measured it, and treating it as a fact is how business cases become indefensible (Module 34). And preserve disagreement: if operations and finance give different causes for the same failure, both hypotheses belong in the map along with the disagreement and a proposed test. Averaging them invents a consensus that does not exist.

The test for whether you reached a mechanism

Can you state the problem in a sentence that contains a because, using the buyer's own vocabulary, and would they nod? "Close takes seven days because two systems use different cost-centre codes and 8% of lines need manual investigation" is a mechanism. "You have a reconciliation challenge" is a symptom in a suit. If your problem statement has no because, you have not finished.

5. Question sequencing and the listening discipline

SPIN — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff — remains the most useful sequencing framework available (class F, from a large practitioner research programme rather than a controlled trial). Three adaptations make it work in practice.

Table 3: SPIN, adapted

StagePurposeAdaptationFailure mode
SituationEstablish the factual context.Research most of it beforehand. Asking what a buyer's annual report already says spends your credibility on your own homework.Twenty minutes of questions the buyer resents answering.
ProblemSurface the difficulties they experience.Ask about a recent instance, not the general case (Module 31). "Walk me through the last close."Generic problem questions producing generic answers.
ImplicationExplore consequences the buyer may not have connected.The most powerful and most abused stage. Use it to clarify a real consequence, never to manufacture fear. Stop when they stop discovering.Escalating implication questions that a buyer recognises as a technique.
Need-payoffLet the buyer articulate the value of solving it.Ask openly, and accept a small answer. "If exceptions dropped to near zero, what would that change?" — and if they say "not much," believe them.Leading the witness toward the number you wanted.

The listening discipline matters at least as much as the questions. Three habits do most of the work:

  • Silence. A full second after every question. Most sellers wait under two seconds; the answer that matters often arrives after four (Modules 22, 28).
  • Summarize and test. Every few minutes: "Let me play that back — have I got it right?" Buyers correct a summary they would never interrupt to correct.
  • Ask what makes them say that. When someone offers a conclusion — "the system is the problem" — the reasoning behind it is usually more valuable than the conclusion.

One structural point on scope: winning discovery calls in large recorded-call datasets tend to explore three or four problems rather than one. A single-problem conversation produces a narrow business case that one budget objection can kill; ten produces a shapeless one. Three or four related problems sharing a mechanism is the shape that survives a committee.

6. Worked example: symptom to mechanism

Composite Case Study

Scenario: Two sellers work the same account. Both hear "month-end close takes too long." One proposes; one diagnoses.

Table 4: The same opening statement, two investigations

LayerSeller A (stopped at symptom)Seller B (reached mechanism)
Symptom"Close takes seven days." → proposed a close-acceleration module."Close takes seven days." → "Walk me through the last one, day by day."
WorkflowNot explored.Days 1–2 exports; days 3–5 reconciliation; days 6–7 review and sign-off. Days 3–5 are 70% of the elapsed time.
Trigger / variationNot explored.Five days in normal months, nine in quarter-end, twelve since the acquisition. Variation is the clue.
MechanismAssumed: "manual processes."The acquired entity uses different cost-centre codes; ~8% of lines fail automatic matching and each takes ~6 minutes to investigate manually.
ConsequenceEstimated by the seller at "several days of finance time."Buyer supplied: ~46 person-hours per cycle, board reporting late twice, two staff working weekends in quarter-end months, and one resignation cited it as a factor.
Prior attemptsNot asked.A matching macro built in 2023, broken by a chart-of-accounts change, unmaintained. Predicts the objection: "we tried automating this."
ConstraintNot asked.The acquired entity's system is contractually fixed for two more years. Seller A's proposed module required replacing it.
OutcomeProposal rejected in week three — it required a system change that was contractually impossible. Seller A never learned why.Scoped to code mapping and exception handling only, at a third of the price. Closed in six weeks; expanded twice within a year as the mechanism proved out.

The decisive difference: Seller B asked about variation. "Five days normally, twelve since the acquisition" is the sentence that leads directly to the mechanism, and it only appears if you ask what changes rather than what happens. Variation is the cheapest diagnostic instrument in discovery and the most consistently skipped.

The Brayford thread · Week 4 · the question that found the mechanism

"Is that consistent, or does it move?"

Priya's opening statement was the symptom: "agency spend is out of control since the acquisition." A seller who proposes against that sells a generic rostering system and loses to Stafford on reporting.

The layers, worked in order. Workflow: a home manager finds a gap at 06:40 and has a spreadsheet of bank availability updated weekly. Variation — the decisive question: "is that consistent, or does it move?" Priya: agency use was manageable before Marlow; it is now 34% higher, and worst at the four acquired sites. Mechanism: the acquired estate runs a separate system, so its bank pool is invisible to the group list — a manager at a Marlow site cannot see that a qualified carer at a Brayford site three miles away is free.

Prior attempt: a shared spreadsheet, abandoned after four months because it was never current. That single fact predicted the objection Cadence would face at week 14 and the reason Sam Whitfield was sceptical.

Constraint: the Marlow contract runs two more years. Everything about the eventual four-site scope was determined here, in week 4, by one question about variation.

7. Weak versus improved discovery

Composite Case StudyScenario: First discovery call with a finance director who has agreed to thirty minutes.

Weak execution (qualification disguised as discovery, then manufactured pain)

Seller: "Thanks for the time. So just to understand your setup — how many entities do you have? And what ERP are you on? How many people in the finance team? And who else is involved in decisions like this? Do you have budget allocated for this year?"
Buyer: "…Three entities. NetSuite. Eleven people. It'd be me and the CFO. Budget depends."
Seller: "Got it. And I imagine a seven-day close must be putting real pressure on the team? That must make it hard to give the board timely numbers. And if the board doesn't have timely numbers, that presumably affects how quickly you can respond to problems? So really this is costing you strategic agility, wouldn't you say?"
Buyer: "I suppose so. Look, could you just send me some information?"

Why it fails: The first five questions serve the seller's CRM and two of them — entity count and ERP — were answerable from public sources, which spends the buyer's goodwill on the seller's homework. The implication sequence is transparently escalating toward a predetermined conclusion, and "strategic agility" is a phrase the buyer did not use and does not think in. The request for information is not a stall; it is an accurate assessment of what this conversation earned.

Improved execution (contract, recent event, variation, mechanism)

Seller: "Before we start — thirty minutes, and my aim is to understand how close actually works here well enough to tell you honestly whether we're relevant. If we're not, I'd rather say so today. And I've read your annual report, so I won't ask you things I can look up. What would you want out of this half hour?"
Buyer: "Mainly whether this is worth a longer conversation."
Seller: "Fair. Can you take me through the last close, day by day? Where did the time actually go?"
Buyer: "Days one and two are exports. Three to five is reconciliation. Six and seven, review and sign-off."
Seller: "So three to five is most of it. Is that consistent, or does it move?"
Buyer: "It's got much worse. It was five days total before we acquired Brayford. Now it's twelve in a quarter-end month."
Seller: "What specifically changed?"
Buyer: "Their cost-centre codes don't map to ours. So a chunk of lines just don't match and someone has to work out what each one is."
Seller: "Roughly what proportion, and how long does one take?"
Buyer: "Maybe eight percent? Five, ten minutes each. It's about forty-something hours across the team."
Seller: "Let me play that back — close is seven days, and most of the growth since the acquisition is because roughly 8% of lines fail to match on cost-centre codes and each needs manual investigation, costing about 46 hours a cycle. Have I got that right?"
Buyer: "That's exactly it. Better than I'd have put it."
Seller: "Has anyone tried to fix it?"
Buyer: "We built a macro. It broke when we changed the chart of accounts and nobody owns it now."
Seller: "And is Brayford's system something that could change?"
Buyer: "No — contracted for another two years."

Why it succeeds: Contracting removed ambiguity and made disqualification legitimate. Research was done in advance. The recent-event question produced a sequence; the variation question produced the mechanism; the buyer supplied every number themselves, which means they will defend them. The played-back summary was confirmed in the buyer's own words — that sentence is the discovery artifact. And the last two questions surfaced a prior failed attempt (which predicts the objection) and a hard constraint (which would have made a larger proposal impossible).

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced sellers reach a mechanism and then stop at the first one, because it explains the symptom and it is one their product addresses. The relief of having found a causal story ends the investigation exactly when a second question would be cheapest.

At Brayford the first mechanism — invisible bank pool across two systems — was real and Cadence solves it. A second, discovered only at week 18, was that the Marlow sites' staff records had never been reconciled after the acquisition, so even a unified view would have shown names with unverified qualifications. That was Dev Osei's actual concern and it was available in week 4 to anyone who had asked "and what else makes 06:40 hard?"

The failure is not laziness; it is that a mechanism you can solve is a satisfying place to stop, and mechanisms you cannot solve tend to be the ones that kill deals in month four.

The correction is to keep asking after the answer arrives, particularly about the parts of the workflow your product does not touch.

The mechanism: Discovery terminates on solvability rather than on completeness. Once a cause is found that maps to the seller's product, the search cost of further questions feels unjustified — so the causes that would disqualify, delay, or reshape the deal are systematically the ones left undiscovered, and they surface later at maximum cost.

8. Practice: the discovery dossier

Exercise goal: produce a written diagnosis the buyer confirms in their own words, with facts, estimates, and interpretations distinguished.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Write your contracting sentences — purpose, shape, reciprocity, and permission to disqualify — verbatim, and use them.
  2. Research the situation layer before the call. List what you already know and will therefore not ask.
  3. Prepare eight to twelve questions anchored in recent events, sequenced across situation, problem, implication, and payoff. Include at least one variation question ("is that consistent, or does it move?").
  4. Run the call. Leave a full second after each question. Summarize and test at least twice.
  5. Build the problem map across all seven layers. Mark every entry fact, estimate, or interpretation.
  6. Write the mechanism sentence — one sentence, containing a because, in the buyer's vocabulary.
  7. Record prior attempts and constraints. These predict your objections and your implementation risk.
  8. Record disagreements between stakeholders, unresolved, with a proposed test.
  9. Send the dossier to the buyer within 24 hours and ask them to correct it. Their edits are the most valuable output.
  10. State the mutual qualification decision: proceed, narrow, test, or stop — including the case for stopping.

Evaluation rubric: Contracting including explicit permission to disqualify (15%); situation layer researched rather than asked (10%); problem map reaching a stated mechanism with a because (25%); fact/estimate/interpretation labelled throughout (15%); prior attempts and constraints captured (15%); disagreement preserved with a proposed test (10%); dossier sent and buyer corrections incorporated (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 5 and 6 on a call you have already had. Most sellers discover they cannot write the mechanism sentence, which locates the gap precisely.

Advanced variation: Record three discovery calls and score them against this rubric with a colleague, blind. Then compare against the observational benchmarks — question count, talk ratio, number of problems explored — treating them as coaching prompts rather than targets. The disagreement between your self-assessment and the blind score is the coaching finding.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. How do you know whether you reached a mechanism or just renamed a symptom?

Write the problem in one sentence containing a because, in the buyer's vocabulary, and check whether they would nod. "Close takes seven days because two systems use different cost-centre codes and 8% of lines need manual investigation" is a mechanism — it explains, and it points at a specific intervention. "You have a reconciliation challenge" restates the symptom in vendor language. If your statement has no because, the investigation is unfinished, and any proposal built on it is guessing.

2. What is the single most useful diagnostic question, and why?

"Is that consistent, or does it move?" Variation is where mechanisms hide. A problem that is worse in some months, at some sites, or since some event has a cause you can find by asking what differs — whereas a problem described as constant offers no leverage. In the worked example, "five days normally, twelve since the acquisition" leads directly to the mechanism, and it appears only because someone asked what changes rather than what happens.

3. Why does asking for permission to disqualify improve the information you get?

Because a buyer who believes you must win the deal filters what they tell you. They withhold the budget problem, the internal opposition, the fact that a competitor is preferred — because disclosing any of it invites pressure. Volunteering that no-fit is an acceptable outcome, and meaning it, removes that incentive. The clause costs one sentence and changes the quality of the meeting's actual product, which is information rather than agreement.

4. A buyer says a problem is serious but has never measured it or assigned an owner. What is the strongest next step?

Propose a bounded diagnostic and keep the opportunity unqualified. Stated seriousness without measurement or ownership is rung-3 or rung-4 evidence (Module 31) — real but unproven. A small, cheap investigation — sample 100 failed lines and classify them, measure the time for four weeks — tests seriousness without manufacturing urgency, and produces a number the buyer owns. Pushing for a decision while concern is high converts an uninvestigated problem into an unfounded business case; disqualifying immediately discards something that may be real.

5. Finance and operations give different causes for the same failure. What belongs in the dossier?

Both hypotheses, the fact that they disagree, and a proposed test. Averaging them invents a consensus that does not exist and produces a diagnosis neither party endorses; siding with the more senior stakeholder discards information and will be discovered later. Preserving the disagreement is also commercially useful — surfacing it neutrally and proposing a test is often the most valuable thing a seller does for a buying group, because it is a conversation they have been avoiding internally (Module 36).

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Discovery is where the strongest driver of sales performance — knowledge of the buyer's situation — is actually acquired. Done properly it produces a diagnosis either party can act on, including the diagnosis that no change is warranted.

  • Contract the conversation, and give explicit permission to disqualify.
  • Research the situation layer. Do not spend the buyer's goodwill on your own homework.
  • Ask about recent events, not the general case.
  • Ask about variation. It is the cheapest route to a mechanism.
  • Write the problem as one sentence containing a because, in their words.
  • Label every entry fact, estimate, or interpretation.
  • Capture prior attempts and constraints; they predict objections and implementation risk.
  • Preserve disagreement and propose a test. Never average it away.
  • Send the dossier and let the buyer correct it. The corrections are the point.

Immediate action: On your next discovery call, add one question — "is that consistent, or does it move?" — and send a written summary within 24 hours asking to be corrected. Those two habits change more about discovery quality than any question list.

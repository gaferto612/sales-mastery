The irrational buyer.

B2B buyers pride themselves on making logical, spreadsheet-driven decisions. Nobel Prize-winning research proves otherwise. Here is how to ethically use cognitive biases, anchoring, and choice architecture to nudge buyers toward a closed won deal.

The Anchoring Effect

Human beings are terrible at assessing absolute value. We only understand value in relative terms. Anchoring is the cognitive bias where an individual depends too heavily on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") when making decisions.

If you present a €50,000 proposal cold, it feels expensive. But if you first discuss how building the solution in-house would cost €250,000 in engineering time and delayed time-to-market, that €250k becomes the anchor. Suddenly, the €50,000 proposal is framed as a massive saving.

Tactical Application

Consider establishing an initial anchor value (such as total cost of the problem, competitor baseline, or premium package) when appropriate to set a reference point.

Loss Aversion (Prospect Theory)

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky proved that the psychological pain of losing something is approximately twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining something of equal value.

Most amateur salespeople sell the gain: "Our software will increase your revenue by 10%."

Elite salespeople sell the loss avoidance: "By continuing to use your legacy system, you are actively leaking 10% of your revenue to your competitors every single month."

Quantify the cost of inaction. Make the status quo feel incredibly expensive and painful to maintain.

Choice Architecture (Nudging)

When buyers are presented with too many options, they experience decision fatigue and will often default to doing nothing (the JOLT effect). As a seller, you are the Choice Architect.

  • The Rule of 3: Limit options to 3 or fewer where possible to reduce decision friction. The brain categorizes them as "Cheap", "Safe/Normal", and "Expensive/Luxury".
  • The Center-Stage Effect: Most buyers will choose the middle option. Use your "Cheap" tier to make the middle tier look feature-rich, and use the "Expensive" tier as an anchor to make the middle tier look reasonably priced.

The Contrast Principle

If you lift a 20kg weight, and then lift a 10kg weight, the 10kg weight feels incredibly light. If you lift a 2kg weight first, the 10kg weight feels heavy.

In sales, sequence matters. If you are selling a €10,000 software license and a €2,000 onboarding package, sell the €10,000 license first. Once they have mentally committed to spending €10,000, adding €2,000 feels trivial (like buying floor mats after buying a car). If you try to negotiate them together, or sell the cheap thing first, it creates unnecessary friction.

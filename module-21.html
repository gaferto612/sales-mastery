Some of it replicated. Know which.

Behavioral economics entered sales training in its most confident decade and stayed there while the field revised itself. This module separates the findings that survived replication from the ones that did not, and shows how to use the survivors to reduce friction rather than to exploit it.

1. Why this module is a filter, not a toolkit

Behavioral economics arrived in commercial training around 2010, at the height of the field's confidence, and much of that material is still taught unchanged. In the intervening years psychology went through a replication crisis that revised several of its most-cited results — some effects shrank substantially, some failed to reproduce, and some were shown to have been inflated by publication bias.

Sales training largely did not update. The result is a body of confident practice built partly on findings the originating field has since qualified or abandoned. A seller who deploys "the decoy effect" or "priming" as reliable levers is working from a 2010 snapshot.

This module is therefore organized as a filter. Its purpose is to tell you which ideas are robust enough to build on, which are real but smaller than advertised, and which should be dropped. And it takes a definite position on application: the honest use of this material is reducing friction in decisions the buyer already wants to make, not engineering decisions they do not.

That position is not merely ethical. Choice architecture that exploits rather than assists is now an enforcement area — the FTC's dark-patterns work and equivalent European action have moved a set of these techniques from clever to actionable. The same knowledge that lets you design a cleaner decision lets you recognise when your own organization has built a trap.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

State which behavioral findings survived replication and which did not, with the reasons.

Apply framing honestly — describing what is already true rather than manufacturing a comparison.

Diagnose status quo bias and choice overload in a stalled decision.

Distinguish friction reduction from choice manipulation, and identify the regulatory line.

Produce a decision-friction audit of a real buying process with prioritized fixes.

3. What survived replication

Table 1: Robust findings and their commercial application

Status quo biasRobust. Samuelson & Zeckhauser, widely replicated in field settings.Disproportionate preference for the current state, independent of its merits.Make the cost of the current state visible and cumulative. This is the single most useful item in the table.

The three that do most of the work

Status quo bias, omission bias, and default effects. All three describe the same underlying commercial reality from different angles: doing nothing is the strongest competitor in most markets, and it wins by not requiring a decision. Almost everything useful in this module follows from taking that seriously — which is why the counter-moves are making inaction visibly costly and making action small and reversible.

4. What did not, and what to do about it

Table 2: Findings that failed, shrank, or never generalized

Claim as taught in sales trainingWhat happenedWhat to do instead
Social/behavioral priming — exposing people to words or images reliably shifts subsequent behavior.Multiple high-profile failures to replicate. This is the area the replication crisis hit hardest.Drop it. Do not build "prime them with words about growth" into a script.
Ego depletion — willpower is a depletable resource, so schedule the ask for the morning.Large multi-lab replications found effects near zero.Drop the mechanism. Scheduling for the buyer's convenience is still good manners.
The decoy effect — add a deliberately inferior option to steer choice.Replicates inconsistently, and often disappears with realistic products and real money.Do not build a pricing tier whose only purpose is to be rejected. Sophisticated buyers detect it, and it undermines the pricing logic you do have.
Choice overload as a universal law — fewer options always sell more.Meta-analysis finds a near-zero average effect; real effects are conditional on complexity and expertise.Reduce options because the buyer cannot evaluate them, not because fewer is inherently better. Then recommend.
"Loss aversion is 2:1"The specific ratio is not a constant; a 2024 meta-analysis places it lower and shows heavy context dependence.Keep the direction, drop the number. Never present a 2:1 multiplier in a business case.
Power posing — postural changes produce hormonal and behavioral effects.The physiological claims failed to replicate; one original author publicly withdrew confidence in them.Preparation and rehearsal produce the confidence effect. Skip the mechanism.
How to hold this without becoming a nihilist

The correct response to the replication crisis is not "psychology is worthless." It is calibration: prefer meta-analyses to single striking studies, expect field effects to be smaller than lab effects, treat any effect quoted as a clean ratio with suspicion, and check whether a finding has been tested with real stakes. That posture is the same one Module 00 asks you to apply to sales claims generally — this module is simply the same filter pointed at a specific literature.

5. Friction audits: the honest application

The distinction that keeps this material usable: friction reduction removes obstacles from a decision the buyer wants to make; choice manipulation engineers a decision they would not otherwise make. Same body of knowledge, opposite direction.

Table 3: The same mechanism, both directions

MechanismFriction reduction (legitimate)Choice manipulation (dark pattern)
DefaultsA sensible starting configuration the customer would likely choose, clearly labelled and easily changed.Pre-ticked marketing consent; auto-added support tiers; auto-renewal with a buried opt-out.
Option reduction"Two of these five aren't worth your time, and here's why." A recommendation with reasons.Hiding the cheaper plan; making the exit option hard to find.
Framing"You're currently spending £14k a month on this" — a true statement in the frame most relevant to the decision.Presenting a best case as typical; "up to" claims; comparisons against a strawman.
ScarcityA real capacity or calendar constraint, stated neutrally.Countdown timers that reset; "only 2 left" that never depletes.
Loss framingQuantifying an ongoing, documented cost.Manufacturing fear about a loss that is not occurring.
EffortRemoving steps from the path the buyer chose.Adding steps to the path away from you — cancellation by phone only, 90-day notice.

The test in each row is the same one from Module 12: would you be comfortable if the buyer could see exactly what you were doing and why? A default you would explain proudly is friction reduction. A default you would rather they did not notice is a dark pattern, and increasingly a regulatory exposure.

6. Worked example: a decision-friction audit

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A B2B software company has a 34% no-decision rate on qualified late-stage deals. Rather than adding urgency, they audit the buyer's decision process for friction.

The instructive finding: the largest single win — sending the security pack in week one — involved no behavioral science at all. Friction audits routinely find that the most expensive obstacles are procedural rather than psychological, and a team that arrives looking only for cognitive biases will walk past them.

The Brayford thread · Weeks 14–20 · what the friction audit found

The largest win was not psychological

The six-week Brayford stall invited a behavioural reading, and part of it was: Priya faced an irreversible eighteen-site commitment with her name alone on it, which is omission bias in its textbook form. The fixes that worked — a criteria workshop that distributed the decision, a four-site scope that shrank the bet — came straight from that diagnosis.

But the friction audit run at week 20 found something larger and duller. Mapping the buyer's actual path with real dates showed that information governance had not started, and would take five weeks whenever it did. That single procedural gap was worth more elapsed time than every cognitive intervention combined.

The full audit, ranked by days: IG not started (five weeks); legal review sequential after commercial agreement rather than parallel (three weeks); no recommendation given among three packaging options (unmeasured, but the proposal had sat unanswered for a fortnight); and a proposal defaulting to full rollout rather than a phased site.

Two of four were procedural. A team that had gone looking only for biases would have found the interesting half and left the expensive half in place.

7. Weak versus improved use of framing

Composite Case StudyScenario: Presenting the economic case to a buyer who has been deliberating for two months.

Weak execution (manufactured loss and a fabricated multiplier)

Seller: "Let me put this in perspective. Behavioral economics tells us people feel losses twice as strongly as gains — so instead of thinking about what you'd gain, think about what you're losing every single day you don't act. That's £470 a day, gone. Over the two months you've been deliberating, that's £28,000 you'll never get back. And every day you wait, that number grows. Can you afford not to move?"

Why it fails: The 2:1 loss-aversion ratio is not a constant and should not be quoted as one — a 2024 meta-analysis places it lower and shows heavy context dependence. Announcing that you are applying behavioral economics to the buyer is a disclosure failure in itself: you have told them you are working on their cognition rather than their problem. "£28,000 you'll never get back" weaponizes a sunk cost to induce guilt about deliberating carefully, which is the behavior you would want from anyone spending real money. And "can you afford not to move" is pressure applied to a buyer who is quite possibly stalled by indecision — which, per Module 09, makes it worse.

Improved execution (true frame, buyer's numbers, smaller decision)

Seller: "Can I reframe this, and tell you why I'm reframing it? Most of our proposals lead with what you'd gain, and I think that's the wrong way round for a decision like this — it makes doing nothing look free, and it isn't.

On your own numbers — the 40 minutes per exception and the nine exceptions a day your ops lead gave me — the current process costs about £11,400 a month. That's happening whether or not you buy anything, and it's the number I'd want on the first page if I were you.

I'd also say the opposite honestly: that figure assumes every recovered minute is redeployed, and it won't be. Somewhere between half and all of it converts to something real, and which end depends on whether you'd avoid the seasonal temps.

And I don't think the answer is to move faster on a five-site rollout. If the thing that's hard is committing to something this size, then let's make it smaller — one site, eight weeks, measured against your own baseline, with a clean exit if it doesn't move. Then the decision on your desk is an eight-week test, not a five-site programme."
Buyer: "That I can approve myself."

Why it succeeds: The reframe is disclosed, with the reason — which is the opposite of hiding the technique. The frame is factually true and uses the buyer's own numbers. The seller immediately discounts their own figure by naming the capacity-to-cash assumption. No multiplier is invented. And rather than applying pressure, the seller shrinks the decision, which is the move that actually addresses the mechanism likely to be stalling it.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced practitioners learn which behavioural findings survived replication and then reach for the surviving ones first, because those are the ones they can now defend.

Status quo bias, omission bias, and defaults are robust, genuinely useful, and — critically — they are psychological explanations, which are more interesting than procedural ones and confer more expertise on the person offering them. A stalled deal explained by omission bias makes the seller sound sophisticated. A stalled deal explained by "we hadn't sent the security pack" does not.

So the diagnosis reaches for the mechanism that flatters the diagnostician, and the five-week governance gap sits undiscovered because nobody thought to draw the timeline.

The correction is ordering rather than content: map the buyer's actual path with real dates before any behavioural analysis. If the longest gap is procedural, fix it first — and if it is not, you now have a much stronger case that the remaining delay is genuinely about the decision rather than the process.

The mechanism: Explanatory sophistication is rewarded socially and procedural explanation is not, so diagnosis is biased toward the mechanism that best displays the diagnostician's expertise. Because behavioural explanations are unfalsifiable at the level of a single deal, they are never disconfirmed — and the procedural cause, which a timeline would expose in ten minutes, is never sought.

8. Practice: the decision-friction audit

Exercise goal: map every point of friction in a real buying process, classify each as procedural or cognitive, and produce prioritized fixes — plus find the dark pattern your own organization is running.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Map the buyer's actual decision path from first contact to signature, as they experience it — every step, form, approval, review, and wait. Use a recently closed deal and check the dates.
  2. For each step, record the elapsed time and who was waiting for whom.
  3. Classify each friction point as procedural (a step that could be removed or moved earlier) or cognitive (choice overload, status quo bias, omission bias, unclear default).
  4. Quantify the cost of the status quo for a live deal, using the buyer's own numbers, and mark the capacity-to-cash assumption explicitly.
  5. Count your options. How many packages, tiers, and configurations does a buyer choose between? Which would you remove, and what recommendation replaces them?
  6. Find the smallest reversible first step your product genuinely supports, with real success criteria and a real exit.
  7. Audit your own dark patterns against Table 3 — including contract terms, not just interfaces. Name at least one.
  8. Prioritize by expected value: elapsed days saved × deal volume, versus effort. Expect the top item to be procedural.

Evaluation rubric: Path mapped from the buyer's perspective with real dates (20%); friction correctly classified as procedural or cognitive (20%); status quo cost quantified in the buyer's numbers with the assumption flagged (20%); smallest reversible step defined with criteria and exit (15%); at least one of your own dark patterns identified, including in contracts (15%); prioritization by days saved versus effort (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 1 and 2 only, for one closed deal. Simply writing down where the elapsed days actually went usually finds a procedural fix worth more than any messaging change.

Advanced variation: Run the audit on your renewal and cancellation flows as well as your acquisition flow. Organizations that have carefully removed friction from buying frequently discover they have deliberately added it to leaving — which is the exact pattern regulators now pursue.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Which behavioral findings should a seller stop using, and why?

Social and behavioral priming (multiple high-profile replication failures), ego depletion (large multi-lab replications found effects near zero), power posing's physiological claims (withdrawn by one original author), the decoy effect as a reliable pricing lever (replicates inconsistently with real products and money), and any specific loss-aversion ratio such as 2:1 (a 2024 meta-analysis places it lower and shows heavy context dependence). The correct posture is calibration rather than nihilism: prefer meta-analyses, expect field effects to be smaller than lab effects, and distrust any effect quoted as a clean constant.

2. Is choice overload real?

Conditionally. The famous jam study did not replicate cleanly and meta-analysis finds an average effect near zero — but genuine effects appear under specific conditions: complex options, non-expert choosers, and unformed preferences. That combination describes most enterprise evaluations quite well. So the practical guidance survives while the universal law does not: reduce options because this particular buyer cannot evaluate them, and then make an explicit recommendation rather than simply offering fewer choices.

3. What distinguishes friction reduction from choice manipulation?

Direction. Friction reduction removes obstacles from a decision the buyer wants to make; manipulation engineers a decision they would not otherwise make. The same mechanisms serve both: a default can be a sensible, clearly-labelled starting configuration or a pre-ticked consent; option reduction can be a reasoned recommendation or a hidden cheaper plan. The test is Module 12's disclosure test — would you be comfortable if the buyer saw exactly what you were doing and why? A default you would explain proudly is legitimate; one you would rather they did not notice is a dark pattern.

4. Why does quantifying the status quo outperform projecting the benefit?

Because status quo bias means inaction is evaluated as free until something makes its cost visible. A projected benefit is a gain competing against a comfortable default that appears to cost nothing; a running monthly total of what the current process is already consuming changes what "doing nothing" feels like. The essential companion move is to shrink the first step — having made inaction expensive without making action small and reversible, you have only raised the stakes on a decision the buyer already feared getting wrong.

5. In a friction audit, why is the biggest win often not psychological?

Because procedural friction is usually larger and cheaper to fix. Security reviews starting at week nine, legal running sequentially after commercial agreement, questionnaires taking eleven days to answer — each of these routinely costs more elapsed time than any framing improvement, and each is fixable by sending something earlier. A team that goes looking only for cognitive biases will walk straight past them, which is why the audit should map the buyer's actual path with real dates before any behavioral analysis begins.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Behavioral economics is useful to a seller mainly as a filter and a friction lens. The robust findings all point at the same commercial truth — doing nothing is the strongest competitor, and it wins by not requiring a decision — and the honest response is to make inaction visible and action small.

  • Robust: status quo bias, omission bias, defaults, framing, loss aversion (direction, not ratio), anchoring (moderate in the field).
  • Drop: priming, ego depletion, power-posing mechanisms, the decoy effect as a reliable lever, and any quoted loss-aversion multiplier.
  • Choice overload is conditional — real where options are complex and preferences unformed, which is most enterprise buying.
  • Reduce options and recommend. Fewer choices without a recommendation just moves the burden.
  • Friction reduction assists a decision; manipulation engineers one. Same mechanisms, opposite direction, and one of them is now an enforcement area.
  • Quantify the status quo, then make the first step small and reversible.
  • Audit procedure before psychology. The biggest win is usually a document sent eight weeks earlier.

Immediate action: Take one recently closed deal, write down every step the buyer went through with the actual dates, and find the single longest gap. Then work out what you could have sent earlier to remove it. That is almost always worth more than any framing change you could make this quarter.

# Research standard

Every claim in this course carries an evidence class. The classes are defined once, here,
and used consistently in all forty-three modules:

| Class | Meaning | Permitted use | Failure mode when misused |
|-------|---------|---------------|---------------------------|
| **R** | Peer-reviewed study, meta-analysis, or systematic review | State as a finding, with effect size and boundary condition | Quoting the effect while dropping the moderators |
| **L** | Legislation, regulator guidance, or official standard | State as a requirement, with the jurisdiction named | Assuming one country's rule is global |
| **V** | Large practitioner dataset, not peer reviewed | Cite as *observed in one dataset*; use as a coaching prompt | Turning a correlation into a KPI, then gaming it |
| **F** | Framework — a structure for organizing thought | Use to make work systematic and inspectable | Treating framework completion as evidence a deal is real |
| **X** | A claim that failed replication or never had support | Teach explicitly as a thing not to believe, and explain why it feels true | Building a methodology on it and blaming the seller when it fails |

Two further categories appear in module text:

- **Illustrative scenario** — a composite example used for practice, tagged as such in the
  page. Its numbers are assumptions, not benchmarks.
- **Contested lens** — a framework whose mechanism is disputed or refuted. Level 6 modules
  are labelled this way in their headers. These are taught critically rather than omitted,
  because learners will meet them elsewhere presented as established fact.

`EVIDENCE.md` holds the quantified findings the modules rely on, each with its source, its
effect size, and the condition under which it stops being true.

Conversion rates, margins, retention rates, talk ratios and other operating metrics must be measured in the learner's context. A benchmark is useful only when its population, period, sample, channel and denominator are disclosed.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer systematic reviews and meta-analyses; primary peer-reviewed research; official legislation and regulator guidance; government procurement guidance; and company filings only for company-specific metric definitions. Label practitioner models as frameworks.

Avoid unsourced viral statistics, neurological explanations inferred from behavior, deterministic personality labels, fabricated case studies, and universal claims based on vendor datasets.

### Calibration rules applied throughout

Written after the replication crisis revised several findings that entered sales training in
their most confident decade:

1. Prefer meta-analyses to single striking studies.
2. Expect field effects to be smaller than lab effects, often substantially.
3. Distrust any effect quoted as a clean ratio — "losses hurt twice as much", "it takes eight touches", "93% of communication is non-verbal". Constants of that shape are almost always artifacts.
4. Ask whether the finding has been tested with real stakes and real money.
5. Where a number comes from a vendor dataset, say so in the same sentence.
6. Never let a class **V** correlation become a compensated KPI.

## Maintenance rule

Every substantial factual revision should record its source in `research-data.js`,
`EVIDENCE.md`, or this bibliography. Review legal and regulatory links at least annually
because requirements differ by jurisdiction and change over time.

When a module states a number, that number belongs in `EVIDENCE.md` with its class letter,
its source, and the condition under which it stops being true. If a finding cannot be given
a boundary condition, it is probably folklore and should be removed from the module rather
than softened.

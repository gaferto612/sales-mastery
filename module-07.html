07Messaging

Write the claim you can survive being checked on.

Commercial writing is not decoration applied to a product; it is the compression of a real argument into the smallest form that still survives scrutiny. This module covers message hierarchy, proof attachment, specificity, and the regulatory line that separates persuasion from a fine.

1. Why most sales copy says nothing

Take any B2B homepage and apply one test: could a direct competitor put their logo on this page without changing a word? If yes — and it usually is — the page contains no claim. It contains atmosphere.

This happens for a predictable reason. Specific claims can be wrong, and being wrong is visible. "Streamline your operations with our intelligent platform" cannot be wrong because it does not assert anything checkable. It is written to survive internal review, not to survive a buyer's scrutiny — and the two are in direct tension. Every round of stakeholder feedback tends to remove specificity, because specificity is where objections attach.

The professional discipline is the reverse: write the claim you could defend in front of the buyer's technical evaluator, then compress it. That produces copy that risks something, which is the only kind that persuades anyone who has read more than three vendor websites.

There is a research-backed reason to attend to the form as well as the content. Narrative material shows a consistent memory advantage over expository material in meta-analytic work — roughly twice as memorable in some estimates — while the evidence on whether statistical or narrative evidence is more persuasive is genuinely mixed and depends on the audience and the claim. The practical reading is not "tell stories instead of giving numbers." It is that a number the buyer cannot situate in a concrete case will not be remembered, and a story with no number attached will not be believed by anyone who has to justify the purchase.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Apply the substitution test to identify copy that contains no claim.

Build a message hierarchy with one primary claim and subordinate support, rather than five competing claims.

Attach the appropriate proof type to each claim and identify claims that cannot currently be supported.

Apply specificity transformations that raise credibility without overstating.

Recognize the regulatory boundaries — substantiation, testimonials, dark patterns — that convert a copy decision into a legal exposure.

3. Message hierarchy: one claim, then support

A page or email with five equally weighted claims communicates none of them. Hierarchy is not a design preference; it is how a reader who gives you eight seconds extracts anything at all.

Table 1: The message hierarchy

LevelJobLengthTest it must passCommon failure
1. Primary claimState the one change the reader gets, in their language.Under 12 words.The substitution test — a competitor could not truthfully use it.Category description instead of a claim: "The modern platform for X."
2. MechanismExplain how, concretely enough to be doubted.One or two sentences.A skeptical engineer could say "that wouldn't work because…"Naming a technology instead of a mechanism. "AI-powered" is not a mechanism.
3. ProofShow it has happened, with conditions.One comparable, with method.A named referent, a measurable, and the boundary under which it held.Logo walls, which prove only that a contract was signed.
4. Objection pre-emptionAddress the top two reasons this reader would say no.Two short blocks.The objections are the real ones from your call recordings, not convenient ones.Answering easy objections and leaving the hard one for the sales call.
5. ActionOne next step, sized to the reader's current commitment.One line.Proportionate — the ask matches the trust earned so far.Four competing calls to action, which is choice overload at the point of decision.

The primary claim is where nearly all the value sits, and it is the sentence most likely to be diluted by committee. Protect it with one rule: the claim must be one a competitor could not honestly make. If they could, you have written a category description and the reader has learned nothing about choosing you.

Awareness determines which level leads

Module 01's awareness spectrum decides the entry point. A problem-aware reader needs the mechanism first — they do not yet believe the problem is solvable. A product-aware reader comparing three vendors needs proof and differentiation first; restating the problem to them wastes the attention you have. The same five levels, reordered by where the reader already stands.

4. Proof attachment and the specificity test

Every claim carries an implicit demand: show me. Different claims require different proof, and using the wrong type is why so much "social proof" persuades nobody.

Table 2: Claim types and the proof each requires

Claim typeExampleProof that worksProof that does not
Capability ("it can do X")"Reconciles multi-currency ledgers nightly."A demo, a sandbox, documentation, an API reference.A testimonial. Customers are not qualified to verify capability.
Outcome ("you will get Y")"Cuts month-end close by four days."A named comparable with a measurement method and a stated baseline.An unnamed aggregate ("customers see up to 40%").
Comparative ("better than Z")"Faster to deploy than the incumbent."Documented side-by-side under stated conditions; independent benchmark.Assertion. Comparative claims also carry the highest legal exposure.
Trust ("we are safe to buy from")"Enterprise-ready."Certifications, audit reports, uptime history, named reference customers of similar scale.Adjectives. "Enterprise-grade" is a word, not a control.
Ease ("this will not hurt")"Live in two weeks."A published implementation plan, median time-to-value from real deployments, an exit path.The word "seamless," which now signals the opposite to experienced buyers.

Table 3: The specificity transformation

VagueSpecificWhy the second is stronger
"Save time on reporting.""Finance closes the month on day 3 instead of day 7."Names the actor, the unit, and the before/after. Checkable, and it maps to a calendar the reader recognises.
"Trusted by industry leaders.""Used by 14 building societies, including three of the largest five."A countable, comparable population instead of an unverifiable adjective.
"Up to 40% more efficient.""Between 18% and 34% in four deployments; the outlier at 40% had unusually poor baseline data quality.""Up to" quotes the best case as if typical. A range with the outlier explained is both more honest and more persuasive.
"Easy integration.""Two REST endpoints; median integration 11 days including customer-side testing. It does not support on-prem AS/400."The disclosed limitation makes the rest credible — the reader now believes you would have told them.

Notice the pattern in the last two rows. Voluntarily naming a limit is the cheapest credibility available. A page that admits one thing it does badly is read differently from one that admits nothing — the reader stops discounting everything by an unknown factor.

5. The regulatory line: substantiation and dark patterns

Class L. Copy decisions become legal exposure faster than most sellers realize, and the exposure sits with the company regardless of who wrote the line.

Table 4: Where copy crosses into regulatory risk

PracticeRequirementWhere the line falls
Performance claimsAdvertisers must hold adequate substantiation before the claim is disseminated. Objective claims imply a reasonable basis exists."Reduces costs 40%" needs evidence in hand at publication. Retrofitting a case study after a challenge is not substantiation.
"Up to" and typicalityRegulators treat a best-case result presented as representative as deceptive.If 40% is the ceiling and the median is 22%, publishing "up to 40%" without disclosing typical results is the classic violation.
Testimonials & endorsementsMust reflect honest opinion, disclose material connections, and not imply typicality that does not exist.Incentivised reviews without disclosure; employee reviews presented as customers; a testimonial about a result most customers do not achieve.
Comparative claimsMust be substantiated under the stated comparison conditions.The highest-risk category, because the injured party is a competitor with both motive and budget to challenge you.
Dark patternsThe FTC's Bringing Dark Patterns to Light and equivalent regimes target design that obscures or subverts choice.Countdown timers that reset; pre-ticked consent; hidden fees until checkout; "roach motel" cancellation; obscured unsubscribe.
Fake scarcity & urgencyIncreasingly enforced as a deceptive practice, not merely a tactic."Only 2 left" that never depletes; recurring "final day" offers. See Module 02 for the expected-value argument against them.
The rule that keeps you clear of all six

Before publishing any claim, write down the evidence that would satisfy a regulator or a hostile competitor's counsel, and note where it lives. If that sentence cannot be written, the claim is not ready — not because it is necessarily false, but because you cannot demonstrate it is true, and those are the same thing under enforcement.

6. Worked example: rewriting a landing page

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A compliance-automation company sells to mid-market financial services. Their landing page converts visitors to demo requests at 1.1%. The rewrite is done claim by claim, with each change tied to a specific defect.

Table 5: Section-by-section rewrite

SectionOriginalDefectRewrite
Headline"Compliance, simplified."Fails the substitution test — every competitor could use it. No claim, no actor, no change."Evidence your auditors accept, without three weeks of screenshots."
Subhead"Our AI-powered platform helps you stay ahead of regulatory change."Names a technology, not a mechanism. "Stay ahead" is unfalsifiable."We pull control evidence directly from your systems on a schedule, so the audit pack assembles itself instead of being rebuilt by hand each cycle."
ProofLogo wall of 24 companies.Proves contracts were signed; proves nothing about outcome or comparability."Three building societies of 400–900 staff cut evidence-gathering from ~110 hours per audit cycle to ~30. Measured against their own prior cycle; method and scope on request." Plus two named references.
Objection blockFAQ: "Is it secure?" / "Do you integrate?"Answers easy objections. The real ones — from call recordings — were "our auditor won't accept automated evidence" and "we tried this and the data was wrong."Two blocks addressing exactly those, including a named audit firm's written position and a description of the reconciliation step that catches source-data errors.
LimitationNone.Absence of any stated limit causes the reader to discount everything."We don't cover FCA reporting obligations — if that's your main burden, we're not the right tool and we'll say so on the first call."
Call to action"Book a demo" · "Start free trial" · "Contact sales" · "Download the guide"Four competing actions; choice overload at the decision point.One primary: "See it run against one of your own controls — 25 minutes." Secondary link to the technical documentation for evaluators who are not ready to talk.
Result after 9 weeks: visitor→demo 1.1% → 2.6%. More significantly, demo→opportunity rose from 34% to 58%, because the disclosed limitation and the real objection blocks disqualified poor-fit visitors before the call rather than during it.

Takeaway: the second number matters more than the first. Copy that is honest about scope converts fewer people and converts them better, and it moves disqualification from the seller's calendar to the reader's own judgment — which is where it is cheapest for everyone.

The Brayford thread · Week 1 · the message that opened it

One claim, and the substitution test

Cadence's website headline at the time was "Smarter workforce scheduling for care providers." Every competitor in the category could have used it unchanged. It contained no claim.

The message that actually reached Priya did not use it. Its working claim was: "your managers stop phoning the agency at 06:40 because they can't find out who's free." Stafford could not honestly say that — their product does not surface bank availability at the moment of the gap — so it passes the substitution test.

The proof attached was a range with the outlier explained (19–31% across three operators, low end expected here) and a negative case (one operator got nothing; their records spanned three systems). The ask was proportionate: worth a conversation, or is this handled.

The disclosed limitation came one message later rather than in the first, which is a defensible sequencing choice and worth noticing: Cadence disclosed the reporting gap at the demo, not in cold outreach, on the grounds that a limitation is only meaningful once the buyer knows what it limits.

7. Weak versus improved headline and body

Composite Case StudyScenario: An email to operations leaders at multi-site healthcare providers.

Weak execution (atmosphere, superlatives, unsupported comparison)

Subject: Transform your operations
"Healthcare is changing faster than ever. Leading providers are turning to next-generation workforce solutions to stay ahead. Our award-winning platform is the fastest and most reliable in the industry, trusted by top healthcare organizations nationwide. Up to 40% reduction in agency spend! Don't get left behind — book your free consultation today. Limited slots available this month."

Why it fails: "Transform your operations" survives the substitution test for every vendor in the category. "Fastest and most reliable" is a comparative claim with no substantiation — the highest-risk category and the easiest for a competitor to challenge. "Up to 40%" presents a ceiling as if typical, which is the classic substantiation violation. "Award-winning" is unverifiable. "Don't get left behind" is manufactured fear with no mechanism. "Limited slots" is fake scarcity. The message asserts six things and supports none.

Improved execution (one claim, mechanism, ranged proof, stated limit)

Subject: agency spend on unplanned absence cover
"Most of the agency spend I see at multi-site providers isn't planned cover — it's the last-minute fill after a same-day absence, booked by a ward manager under time pressure at whatever rate is available.

We surface the internal bank staff who are actually available for that specific shift and skill mix, at the moment the gap appears, rather than after the ward has already phoned an agency.

Across four providers of similar size, last-minute agency bookings fell between 19% and 31% in the first six months. The 31% case had an unusually large existing bank; if your bank is small, expect the lower end or less — that's the main thing that determines whether this works for you.

Worth a look at your own absence data before we talk about anything else?"

Why it succeeds: One primary claim, expressed as a specific operational moment the reader will recognise. A mechanism concrete enough for a skeptical operations director to argue with. Proof as a range across a named population, with the outlier explained rather than quoted. A stated boundary condition that will disqualify some readers — which is the point. And an ask proportionate to the trust earned so far: look at your own data, not book a meeting.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced writers pass the substitution test and then fail a harder one. They produce a claim only they could make — and make it about a capability rather than a consequence the buyer already recognises.

"We surface real-time bank-staff availability at the point of gap creation" is unique to Cadence, technically precise, and unusable, because the reader has to translate it into their own Tuesday morning before it means anything. Most will not do that work, and the ones who do will do it wrong.

The difference is whose language the differentiator is expressed in. A distinctive claim in vendor vocabulary is still a vendor claim; it merely fails more specifically than a generic one.

The correction is to write the claim as something that happens to a named person at a named moment, and then check that a competitor could not truthfully copy it. Both tests, in that order. Passing only the second is how technically excellent messaging goes unread.

The mechanism: Differentiation and comprehension are independent axes, and writers optimise the one their internal reviewers can assess. Colleagues can verify that a claim is accurate and unique; only a buyer can verify that it is legible — and buyers are not in the review meeting.

8. Practice: annotated landing-page rewrite

Exercise goal: rewrite a real page or email so that every claim carries proof, one limitation is disclosed, and every change is annotated with the defect it fixes.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Take a live page or your most-used email. Run the substitution test on every sentence: could a named competitor use it truthfully? Highlight every sentence that passes their test — those are your empty sentences.
  2. Extract the implicit claims. List them and classify each as capability, outcome, comparative, trust, or ease.
  3. For each claim, name the proof you actually hold and where it lives. Mark claims with no proof as unsupported.
  4. Delete or fix every unsupported claim. Deleting is a legitimate outcome and usually improves the page.
  5. Rewrite the primary claim so a competitor could not honestly use it. Under 12 words.
  6. Pull the two real objections from your last ten call recordings — not the convenient ones — and write a block for each.
  7. Disclose one genuine limitation, specifically enough that it will cost you some conversions.
  8. Reduce to one primary call to action, sized to the reader's current commitment.
  9. Annotate every change with the defect it fixes and the evidence supporting the new version.

Completed exemplar (annotation extract, five rows)

Original lineDefectRewriteEvidence held
"The all-in-one platform for modern field service."Substitution test failed by 6 named competitors. Category description, no claim."Your engineers stop making second trips for faults they could have fixed first time."First-time-fix improvement documented in 5 of 6 deployments; internal outcome study, methodology on file.
"Powered by advanced AI."Technology name, not a mechanism. Cannot be argued with, therefore cannot be believed."We match the job's fault code to engineer certifications and van stock before dispatch, not after."Product documentation; a skeptical evaluator can now challenge it, which is the goal.
"Increase productivity by up to 40%."Ceiling presented as typical — substantiation risk. Also no unit: productivity of what?"Jobs completed per engineer per day rose from 5.2 to between 5.9 and 6.4 across five deployments."Five customer datasets, pre/post, same measurement definition. "Seamless integration with your existing systems.""Seamless" now signals the opposite. Ease claim with no proof."Two REST endpoints, median 11 days including your testing. No on-prem AS/400 support."Integration log across 22 deployments; the AS/400 gap is a known roadmap item.

Evaluation rubric: Rigorous substitution test applied to every sentence (20%); claims correctly classified and matched to proof type (20%); unsupported claims deleted rather than softened (20%); one genuine, costly limitation disclosed (15%); real objections used, sourced from recordings (15%); single proportionate call to action (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 1 and 5 only, on a single email. Getting one primary claim past the substitution test is a real day's work and worth more than a full page of atmosphere.

Advanced variation: Build the claim-to-evidence register as a permanent artifact: every public claim, its proof, where the proof lives, who owns refreshing it, and its expiry date. This is what a substantiation defence looks like, and it doubles as the fastest onboarding document a new seller can be given.

Artifact quality rubric

Completing the annotated landing-page rewrite is not the same as producing a good one. Grade your own against these dimensions before you show it to anyone.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. What is the substitution test and why does most B2B copy fail it?

Put a competitor's logo on your page and read it. If every sentence remains true for them, your page contains no claim — only category description and atmosphere. Most copy fails because specific claims can be wrong, and internal review processes systematically remove the specificity where objections attach. The page ends up optimized to survive a stakeholder meeting rather than a buyer's scrutiny, and those are opposite objectives.

2. Why is "up to 40%" both weak persuasion and a regulatory risk?

Persuasively, experienced buyers read "up to" as "this is the best result we have ever seen and probably an outlier," so it lands as weaker than the honest median would. Legally, presenting a ceiling as if it were representative is a classic deceptive-advertising pattern: regulators require that objective performance claims be substantiated before dissemination and that typicality not be implied where it does not exist. A range with the outlier explained is stronger on both counts — "18% to 34% across four deployments; the 40% case had unusually poor baseline data."

3. A customer testimonial says "this platform is the most secure on the market." Can you publish it?

Not safely. It is a comparative claim, and a testimonial does not launder the substantiation requirement — the advertiser is responsible for claims conveyed through endorsements, including implied ones. The customer is also not qualified to verify comparative security across a market they have not evaluated. Publish what they can speak to: their own experience, their own controls, their own audit outcome. If you want a comparative security claim, you need documented evidence under stated conditions, and you should expect a competitor to test it.

4. Why does disclosing a limitation increase conversion quality?

Two mechanisms. First, credibility: a reader who sees you volunteer a weakness stops discounting your other claims by an unknown factor, because they now believe you would have told them. Second, self-selection: a specific limitation lets poor-fit readers disqualify themselves before consuming a sales conversation. Raw conversion usually falls slightly and downstream conversion rises substantially — which is the trade you want, because a demo that was never going to close is more expensive than a visitor who leaves.

5. Your page has four calls to action. What is the likely effect and what should you do?

Choice overload at the point of decision, which raises the cost of deciding and can suppress action entirely — the same mechanism that stalls enterprise deals. Reduce to one primary action sized to the trust earned so far. If genuinely distinct audiences need different next steps — an evaluator wanting documentation versus a buyer wanting a conversation — make one primary and the other a plain secondary link, not a competing button. The goal is that the reader never has to decide which action to take, only whether to take it.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Good commercial writing is a discipline of subtraction and evidence. You are not trying to sound impressive; you are trying to make one claim that is worth checking, support it with proof a skeptic would accept, and be honest enough about the limits that the honesty becomes the reason to believe the rest.

  • Run the substitution test on every sentence. Anything a competitor could truthfully say is not a claim.
  • One primary claim, then mechanism, proof, objection pre-emption, and a single action.
  • Match proof to claim type. Testimonials cannot prove capability; logo walls cannot prove outcomes.
  • Publish ranges with the outlier explained, never "up to."
  • Disclose one real limitation. It costs conversions and buys credibility, and the trade is favourable.
  • Hold substantiation before publication. If you cannot say where the evidence lives, the claim is not ready.

Immediate action: Open your homepage and run the substitution test on the headline alone. If a competitor could use it word for word, rewrite it today into a claim under 12 words that they could not honestly make — and write down the evidence you hold for it before you publish.

07Negotiation

Bargaining without losing.

Negotiation isn't winning at the other side's expense. The best negotiators leave both parties feeling they got the deal they needed — and willing to work together again.

The negotiator's mindset

Three internal shifts to make before any negotiation:

  1. You're not adversaries. You're problem-solvers trying to find a deal that works for both. The other side isn't your enemy — the failed deal is.
  2. You can walk away. The single biggest source of leverage is being willing to leave. If you can't walk, you'll get walked on.
  3. It's not personal. Even if they push hard, their behavior is a tactic, not an attack on you.
He who needs the deal least, wins.

BATNA & ZOPA — the two terms that matter

BATNA = Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. What happens if this deal doesn't happen? Your BATNA is your leverage.

ZOPA = Zone Of Possible Agreement. The range where both sides could plausibly say yes — between your walk-away and theirs.

If seller's minimum is €5K and buyer's maximum is €7K, the deal exists somewhere between. If both walk-aways flip (seller wants >€7K, buyer maxes at €5K) — no ZOPA, no deal, save your breath.

Before any negotiation

Write down: (1) Your ideal outcome. (2) Your walk-away. (3) Your best guess at their walk-away. (4) Your BATNA. Without these you're negotiating blind.

Anchoring

The first number in a negotiation pulls the final number toward it. Studies consistently show the anchor moves the outcome by 30–50%.

Who anchors first?

  • You — if you have good info on the market and they don't
  • Them — if you have no idea what's fair (let them reveal range)

Defending against their anchor

  • Name it. "That's quite high — what's that based on?" — refuses to accept the anchor as legitimate
  • Counter-anchor immediately. Don't accept; bring your own number
  • Range, not point. "We typically see deals close between X and Y" — sets implicit anchor without committing

The concession pattern

How you give ground matters more than how much. Smaller, slower, more reluctant concessions signal you're near your limit.

The trading rule

Never give a concession without getting one. "I can do €X, but I'd need you to commit to a 2-year term." This:

  • Trains them: every ask costs something
  • Lets you walk away while feeling fair
  • Often reveals what they actually care about

Tactical empathy (Chris Voss)

Chris Voss, former FBI hostage negotiator, brought a different lens: emotion isn't an obstacle to negotiation — it's the medium. His core techniques:

Labels

Naming the emotion the other side is feeling. Reduces its intensity by ~50%.

It sounds like you're worried this won't work for your team specifically.
It seems like timing is the real issue here.

Mirrors

Repeat the last 1–3 words they said, with rising tone. Triggers them to elaborate.

Them: "We just don't have budget for this in Q3."

You: "...budget in Q3?"

Calibrated questions

"How" and "what" questions that force them to think and solve with you. Avoid "why" — sounds accusatory.

How am I supposed to do that?
What about this is important to you?
How would your team feel if we did it this way?

The "No" reframe

Most negotiators chase "yes." Voss says "no" is safer for the other side — they feel in control. Ask questions that invite "no" early:

Is now a bad time to talk?
Have you given up on solving this problem?

Power scripts

Phrases that diffuse pressure and create space.

I hear that the price is a stretch. Let me ask — if price weren't an issue, would this be the right solution for you?

This separates two objections: price and fit. You can only solve one at a time.

I appreciate you asking. What part of the offer would we adjust to bring the price down?

Forces a trade. Discounts without trade-offs train them to ask again.

Just want to check — have you decided against moving forward? No is a perfectly fine answer.

Voss-style. Invites the no, often gets a "no, no, we're still thinking..." with the real reason.

It sounds like the timing isn't right. What would need to be true for this to move forward?

Names the stall + opens path. Don't push for "next week" — ask what changes their mind.

That's a good option. Help me understand — what would have to be different here for you to choose us?

Respect competitor, ask what's missing. Often you can solve it.

Most customers we work with at this size invest somewhere between €X and €Y per year. Where you land depends on...

Range anchor with conditional. Sets reference without committing.

It sounds like this isn't the right fit right now. I'd rather you wait until it is. Can I check back in three months?

Cleanest exit. Keeps the door open. Often triggers them to come back faster.

Common mistakes

  • Negotiating against yourself ("I could probably do €X... or even less")
  • Caving the moment they push back
  • Discounting without trading
  • Splitting the difference automatically (it's almost never optimal)
  • Letting emotion drive — yours, not theirs
  • Negotiating without writing down your walk-away first
  • Showing weakness early ("we really need this deal")

Exercises

  1. BATNA audit. Pick a real upcoming negotiation. Write your BATNA, walk-away, and best guess at theirs. Do this before you talk.
  2. Labels drill. In your next 3 conversations, use one label ("It sounds like..."). Notice what happens.
  3. Silent close drill. After stating a price, count to 10 in your head before saying anything more. Practice the silence.
  4. Trade-not-give drill. Next ask for concession, respond with a trade. Track outcomes vs. just saying yes.

25Proof

A demo proves your product exists. Proof needs a criterion.

The feature tour is the most common and least effective sales artifact in existence. This module replaces it with a prescriptive demo built on confirmed problems, agreed success criteria, and a proof plan the buyer helped write.

1. Why the feature tour fails

The default demo shows the product from the top of the navigation to the bottom. It is comprehensive, it demonstrates effort, and it fails for four reasons.

It answers questions nobody asked. Most of what is shown is irrelevant to this buyer, and irrelevance is not neutral — it consumes attention and signals that you did not listen.

It creates surface area for objections. Every feature shown invites a comparison with a competitor who does that feature better. A tour of forty screens hands the buyer forty opportunities to find you wanting.

It makes the seller the protagonist. A demo is a performance in which the vendor demonstrates competence. The buyer watches. Nothing in that arrangement helps them decide.

It has no criterion, so it cannot succeed or fail. Nobody agreed in advance what would count as the product working. A demo without a success criterion is a presentation, and presentations do not resolve doubt — which means the doubt survives into the next meeting, and the next.

The alternative is prescriptive: a short, deliberately narrow demonstration of the specific things this buyer has already told you matter, against criteria they helped define, with everything else cut. It is harder to build and much shorter to deliver, and its purpose is not to impress but to resolve a specific uncertainty.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Map every demo segment to a problem the buyer has explicitly confirmed.

Apply tell–show–tell so that each segment carries meaning rather than motion.

Agree success criteria before any proof activity, and use them to end it.

Distinguish a demo, a proof of concept, and a pilot, and choose the right one.

Produce a demo storyboard and a proof plan with the buyer's own success criteria.

3. The prescriptive demo structure

A prescriptive demo has four parts and one rule. The rule: nothing is shown that does not trace to something the buyer said. Not something you inferred, not something similar buyers usually care about — something they said, which you can quote back.

Table 1: The four parts

PartJobDurationWhat it sounds like
1. The recap and contractRestate their problems in their words, confirm you have them right, and agree what this session is for.3–5 min"You told me three things matter: X, Y, Z. I'm going to show you those three and nothing else. If I've got the list wrong, correct me now — it's cheaper than at the end."
2. The segmentsOne segment per confirmed problem, each in tell–show–tell form.5–8 min eachSee section 4.
3. The honest limitShow or state one thing the product does badly that is relevant to them.2 min"The thing I'd flag is the audit export — it needs a manual step. Here's what that looks like."
4. The criterion and the next stepAgree what would need to be true for this to be proven, and what happens next.5 min"What would you need to see, with your own data, to be confident? Let's write that down now."
The opening question that changes the whole session

Before showing anything: "What would have to be true at the end of this hour for it to have been worth your time?" The answers are frequently not what the demo was built for — and they are always more useful than the agenda you prepared. It also converts the session from a performance into a shared task, which changes how the room behaves.

Two structural choices are worth making deliberately. Start with their hardest problem, not your best feature — a buyer who sees their worst pain addressed first will pay attention to everything after it. And use their data, or their vocabulary, or both. A demo environment full of "Acme Corp" and generic sample records asks the buyer to do the imaginative work of translation, and most will not.

4. Tell–show–tell and the discipline of subtraction

Tell–show–tell is the smallest useful unit of demonstration. It exists because a screen shown without framing is just motion, and motion is not memorable.

Table 2: The structure of one segment

StepPurposeExampleCommon error
Tell (before)Name the problem and what you are about to show, so the buyer knows what to watch for."You said your planners rebuild routes by hand after every overnight change. I'm going to show you what happens here when an order changes at 4am."Starting with the screen and narrating the interface.
ShowDemonstrate the specific thing. Nothing else. No detours.Three clicks. Change lands, plan re-sequences, exceptions surface.Showing adjacent features because they are impressive. Every detour dilutes the point and adds objection surface.
Tell (after)State what just happened in terms of their outcome, then stop and check."So the 40 minutes your planner spends at 6am becomes about 2. Does that match what you were picturing, or have I missed something about how your changes arrive?"Moving to the next segment without checking. Unchecked segments accumulate into a demo the buyer stopped following ten minutes ago.

The harder discipline is subtraction. Most demos are built by addition — someone adds a slide, a feature, a use case, and nothing is ever removed. The test for every element is: which confirmed problem does this address? If the answer is "it shows breadth" or "customers usually like it," cut it.

The cost of showing more

Every additional feature shown does three things: it consumes attention that a relevant segment needed, it creates a comparison point with a competitor, and it raises the buyer's estimate of implementation complexity. Sellers show breadth to demonstrate value and frequently reduce it — a buyer who leaves thinking "that's a big system" has acquired a new objection, not a new reason to buy.

5. From demo to proof: criteria before evidence

A demo shows that a capability exists. Proof shows that it works in the buyer's environment, on their data, against a criterion. Conflating the two is why so many evaluations drift.

Table 3: Choosing the right proof activity

ActivityQuestion it answersDurationUse whenFailure mode
Demo"Does the capability exist and does it fit our workflow?"30–45 minAlways. It is the cheapest way to resolve most uncertainty.Becoming a tour with no criterion.
Technical deep-dive"Will it integrate with our environment?"1–2 hrs, with their engineersA specific architectural doubt exists.Running it before the business case is agreed, so the wrong people are engaged.
Proof of concept"Does it work with our data?"2–4 weeks, narrow scopeData quality, volume, or format is the live uncertainty.Unbounded scope. A POC without an end date becomes a free implementation.
Pilot"Does it produce the outcome, with our people, in production?"6–12 weeks, one team or siteAdoption or organizational fit is the live uncertainty.Being run without a baseline, so nobody can say whether it worked.

Every proof activity needs five things agreed in writing, before it starts:

  1. The specific uncertainty it exists to resolve. If you cannot name one, do not run it.
  2. The success criterion, as a number or an observable state, defined by the buyer.
  3. The baseline — what the current process produces, measured before anything changes. Without it, no result means anything.
  4. The scope and end date, with what is explicitly excluded.
  5. The decision that follows each outcome — including what happens if it fails.
The failure condition is the most persuasive element

Stating in advance what would cause you to withdraw — "if field accuracy is below 88% or handling time is above baseline, we stop and there is no charge" — does two things. It converts an open-ended experiment into a bounded, reversible decision, which is the move that addresses omission bias in a hesitant buyer (Module 09). And it is a costly signal: no vendor with a weak product volunteers a condition under which they lose.

7. Weak versus improved demo opening

Composite Case StudyScenario: The first five minutes of a scheduled demo with four attendees, two of whom the seller has not met.

Weak execution (company slide, then the tour)

Seller: "Thanks everyone for joining! Before we dive in, just a quick bit about us — we were founded in 2016, we're now in eleven countries, and we work with some of the biggest names in the sector. [3 slides of logos.] Right, let me share my screen and take you through the platform. So this is the main dashboard — you can see everything at a glance here, it's fully customisable, and over on the left we've got our main navigation…"

Why it fails: Eight minutes have gone to material the buyer did not ask for and cannot use. The two new attendees have not been asked what they need, so the demo will not address them and nobody will find out until they raise an objection later or, worse, say nothing and block it afterwards. Starting at the navigation means the buyer is being shown the product's structure rather than their own problem. And no criterion has been set, so this session cannot succeed — only end.

Improved execution (contract the session, then start with their hardest problem)

Seller: "Before I show anything — Priya and Tom, we haven't met. Can I ask each of you one question: what would have to be true at the end of this half hour for it to have been worth your time?"
Priya (ward manager): "I want to know whether my team can actually use it. The last system we got was designed for head office."
Tom (IT): "Honestly, whether it talks to our rostering database without a project."
Seller: "That's useful, and it changes what I'll show. Priya, your point is the one I'd have got wrong — I'd built this around head-office reporting, and I'm going to drop most of that.

So here's what I'll do. Three things, from what Sarah told me and what you've just added: same-day absence cover, which is your worst problem; the ward manager's actual daily view, for Priya; and Tom, I'll show you the integration honestly, including where it's awkward. About twenty-five minutes, and I'll stop after each one to check I'm answering the right question.

I'll say now that our reporting is weaker than what you have — you'd lose two custom views. I'd rather flag that at the start than have you find it in month three.

Priya, since your question is the one I'd have missed, let's start with your Tuesday morning."

Why it succeeds: The new attendees were asked what they needed, and one of them redirected the session — which would otherwise have been built for the wrong audience. The agenda was contracted openly, including what was being dropped and why. The honest limit was stated before any doubt attached to it. And starting with the attendee whose need the seller had missed is both good manners and good sequencing: it buys attention for everything that follows.

8. Practice: demo storyboard and proof plan

Exercise goal: rebuild your standard demo as a prescriptive one and produce a proof plan the buyer would co-sign.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Inventory your current demo: every screen and segment, with its duration. Most people are surprised by the total.
  2. For each element, name the confirmed problem it addresses — something a buyer actually said, which you could quote. Mark anything that fails this test.
  3. Cut everything marked. Record what percentage of the demo disappeared; in most cases it is more than half.
  4. Rebuild the survivors as tell–show–tell segments, writing the "tell" sentences out in full. The before-tell and the after-check are the parts people improvise badly.
  5. Order by the buyer's hardest problem first, not by your best feature.
  6. Write the honest limit — one real weakness relevant to this buyer, and how you will show or state it.
  7. Write the opening contract question and the closing criteria question, verbatim.
  8. Build the proof plan: the specific uncertainty, the buyer-defined success criterion, the baseline and how it will be measured, the scope and end date, and the decision that follows each outcome — including the failure condition under which you withdraw.
  9. Time it. If a prescriptive demo runs over 30 minutes, you have not finished cutting.

Completed exemplar (storyboard and proof plan)

Evaluation rubric: Every surviving element traced to a quotable confirmed problem (25%); genuine cut of at least half the original demo (15%); tell–show–tell written out including the after-check (15%); honest limit specific and relevant (15%); proof plan with buyer-defined criterion and a measured baseline (20%); explicit failure condition under which you withdraw (10%).

Beginner variation: Do steps 1–3 only. Simply listing every screen in your standard demo and asking which confirmed problem each addresses is usually enough to cut it in half, and the exercise takes twenty minutes.

Advanced variation: Build three prescriptive variants for your three most common buyer roles from a single component library, so a demo can be assembled in ten minutes rather than improvised. Then measure objections raised per session and win rate after demo, before and after — those two numbers make the case to your team better than any argument.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Why does showing more features often reduce win rate?

Because every additional feature does three unhelpful things: it consumes attention a relevant segment needed, it creates a comparison point where a competitor may be stronger, and it raises the buyer's estimate of implementation complexity. A tour of thirty screens hands the buyer thirty opportunities to find you wanting, and most of them concern capabilities they will never use. Sellers show breadth to demonstrate value, and a buyer who leaves thinking "that's a big system" has acquired an objection rather than a reason.

2. What is the difference between a demo and proof?

A demo shows that a capability exists and fits the workflow; proof shows that it produces a specific result in the buyer's environment, on their data, against a criterion they defined. Conflating them is why evaluations drift — a POC or pilot run without a named uncertainty, a baseline, and an end date becomes a free implementation with no decision attached. Every proof activity needs five things agreed in writing beforehand: the uncertainty, the criterion, the baseline, the scope and end date, and the decision that follows each outcome.

3. Why open a demo by asking what would make it worth the attendees' time?

Because the answers are frequently different from what the demo was built for, and because it converts a performance into a shared task. It is especially valuable with attendees you have not met — a ward manager whose concern is whether her team can use it will not be served by a session built around head-office reporting, and if you do not ask, you will find out either when she objects late or when she silently blocks it afterwards. Redirecting on the spot also demonstrates that you are working on their problem rather than delivering a script.

4. Why show a weakness during the demo rather than waiting to be asked?

Because the weakness will be discovered regardless, and the only variable you control is whether it appears in a room where it can be discussed or in month three when it becomes an escalation. Volunteering it also does the work described in Modules 07 and 19: a buyer who sees you disclose one real limitation stops discounting your other claims by an unknown factor. Show it rather than describing it where you can — "here's what the manual step actually looks like" is far less alarming than an abstract caveat.

5. Why is a stated failure condition the most persuasive part of a proof plan?

Because it is a costly signal and it shrinks the decision. No vendor with a weak product volunteers a threshold below which they withdraw at no charge, so offering one carries information that no assertion could. And it converts an open-ended commitment into a bounded, reversible one — which is precisely the move that addresses omission bias in a buyer who is stalled by fear of choosing wrongly rather than by doubt about the product.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

The prescriptive demo is mostly an exercise in subtraction and in asking. Everything shown must trace to something the buyer said; everything else is surface area for objections you did not need to create.

  • Nothing is shown that does not trace to a confirmed, quotable problem.
  • Ask what would make the session worth their time — especially of attendees you have not met.
  • Start with their hardest problem, not your best feature.
  • Tell before, show narrowly, tell after — then stop and check.
  • Show one honest limitation before doubt attaches to it.
  • Agree success criteria in the room, in the buyer's own numbers.
  • Every proof activity needs an uncertainty, a criterion, a baseline, an end date, and a failure condition.

Immediate action: List every screen in your standard demo and write beside each the confirmed problem it addresses, quoting a real buyer. Cut everything without a quote. Then time what remains — if it is over thirty minutes, cut again.

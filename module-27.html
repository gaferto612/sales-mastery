27Contested Lens · Level 6

A vocabulary for interaction patterns, not for people.

Transactional Analysis offers a usable way to notice when a conversation has slipped into a rescuing, blaming, or deferring pattern. It offers no way to diagnose a person, and its clinical claims are weakly evidenced. This module keeps the first and discards the second.

1. What this lens is for

Transactional Analysis is a mid-twentieth-century framework from Eric Berne, originally a clinical model and later a popular management vocabulary. Its evidential status is limited: systematic reviews of TA-based interventions find a small literature with substantial methodological weakness, and the ego-state model has no validated measurement instrument. It sits in Level 6 for that reason.

Its useful contribution is narrow and real. TA gives you a vocabulary for noticing the shape of an interaction — that a conversation has become one person instructing and another complying, or one person complaining and another rescuing. Those patterns are observable, they genuinely damage commercial conversations, and most sellers have no name for them, which makes them hard to interrupt.

The line to hold is the same as in Modules 20 and 22: describe the interaction, never the person. "This exchange has taken on a parent–child shape" is an observation about a pattern that either of us can change. "He's operating from Child ego state" is a diagnosis, it is unsupported, and — like every diagnosis in this level of the course — it stops you asking.

Read the ego-state vocabulary as shorthand for observable behavior, not as a claim about psychological structure. Used that way it costs nothing and occasionally saves a conversation.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

State the evidential status of TA and explain what that permits and prohibits.

Describe an interaction pattern in behavioral terms without labelling either party.

Recognize the three patterns that most damage commercial conversations.

Reset a conversation toward evidence and shared agency without blame.

Produce a conversation reset script for your own most common difficult exchanges.

3. Ego states as a description of behavior

TA describes three modes of behaving. Treat them strictly as descriptions of how someone is speaking right now, not as types.

Table 1: The three modes, behaviorally

ModeWhat it sounds likeLegitimate useWhere it damages a sales conversation
Parent (instructing, evaluating, protecting)"You should…", "The right way to do this is…", "Don't worry, leave it with me."Genuine expertise offered clearly. A recommendation with reasons (Module 09) is a legitimate version.Condescension; telling a buyer what their problem is; taking over a decision that is theirs.
Adult (observing, reasoning, checking)"Here's what I know and what I don't", "What would we need to see?", "Is that right?"The target mode for nearly all commercial conversation.Rarely damaging. Can read as cold if it never acknowledges that a situation is difficult.
Child (reacting, complying, deferring, protesting)"Whatever you think is best", "That's not fair", over-apologising, over-promising to please.Enthusiasm, curiosity, and genuine spontaneity are real assets and belong here.A seller deferring to avoid friction; a buyer complying without believing; over-promising to keep the room pleasant.
The one rule that keeps this usable

These describe utterances, not people. Everyone speaks in all three modes within a single meeting, and there is no instrument that measures which one someone "is." If you catch yourself categorizing a buyer rather than noticing an exchange, you have crossed the line — and the cost is the same as with every other false diagnosis in Level 6: you stop gathering information exactly when you need it.

4. The three patterns that damage sales conversations

Three complementary patterns account for most of the damage. Each is stable — both parties keep it going — which is why they persist across multiple meetings until someone names them.

Table 2: The damaging patterns

PatternShapeHow it shows up commerciallyWhy it persists
1. RescuingBuyer describes a problem; seller takes ownership of solving it entirely."Leave it with me, I'll handle the internal approvals." The seller ends up running the buyer's project, which fails because they have no authority inside the organization.It feels helpful and generous, and the buyer is relieved. Both parties are rewarded in the moment.
2. InstructingSeller tells the buyer what their problem is and what they must do."What you actually need is…" delivered before diagnosis. The buyer complies verbally and disengages silently.It sounds like expertise, and buyers rarely argue with a confident vendor — they simply stop replying.
3. DeferringSeller avoids friction: accepts vague answers, does not ask for access, does not raise the risk.Accepting "Dan's taking it up" for four months. Not asking for the honest no. Not disclosing the limitation.Every individual deferral keeps the relationship pleasant. The cost lands months later and is attributed to something else.

There is a fourth pattern worth naming because it appears in escalations: the drama triangle, in which participants take turns as victim, rescuer, and persecutor. A customer complaint meeting where the customer is aggrieved, the account manager is defending, and someone is being blamed for an implementation failure has this shape. Naming it internally — "we're in a triangle here, none of us is working on the actual problem" — is often enough to break it, provided you never say it to the customer as an accusation.

The tell for each pattern

Rescuing: you are doing work the buyer's organization should be doing, and you cannot name who on their side owns it. Instructing: you stated their problem before they did. Deferring: you left a meeting without asking the question you knew you should ask. All three are noticeable within minutes if you are looking for them, and all three are invisible if you are not.

5. Resetting toward evidence

The reset is a small set of moves that returns an exchange to shared reasoning and shared agency. None requires naming the pattern to the other person.

Table 3: Reset moves

MoveWhat it doesLanguage
State what you observe, not what you concludePuts a shared fact on the table that neither party has to defend."We've moved the date three times. I'm not sure what that means — do you have a read on it?"
Return agencyUndoes rescuing by giving the decision back."I can build the paper, but I can't take it to your board. Who on your side would own that, and what do they need from me?"
Ask rather than assertUndoes instructing.Instead of "what you need is X": "I might be wrong about this — is the constraint the workflow or the data?"
Say the thing you are avoidingUndoes deferring, and is the highest-value of the four."I've been avoiding asking this: is this actually going to happen, or should I stop chasing it? Either answer is fine."
Name the pattern, not the personFor entrenched cases, and only about the interaction."I think we've got into a rhythm where I chase and you reassure, and neither of us is enjoying it. Can we do something different?"
Return to evidenceEnds the exchange in something checkable."What would we both need to see to know this is real?"

The last move matters most. Every one of these patterns persists because the conversation is running on feelings and impressions — relief, confidence, politeness — rather than on anything checkable. Ending an exchange in a shared piece of evidence, a named owner, or an agreed test is what stops it recurring.

6. Worked example: three conversations reset

Composite Case Study

Scenario: Three stalled situations from one seller's pipeline, each showing one of the three patterns.

The seller's version would very likely have been discounted as vendor material.

Tell: left three meetings without asking the question they knew they should ask."I've been avoiding this because I didn't want to put you on the spot. The common element: in all three cases the seller named their own contribution to the pattern first — "I've been doing this as though," "I told you what your problem was," "I've been avoiding this." That is what makes the reset land without defensiveness, and it is why the move works even though the framework behind it is weakly evidenced. You are not diagnosing anyone; you are changing what you do.

The Brayford thread · Week 16 · the sentence that ended the stall

Naming your own half of the pattern

For ten weeks the Alex–Tom interaction had a stable shape: Alex sent evidence, Tom received it warmly and promised to take it upward, and nothing happened. Both parties maintained it. Alex got the relief of having done something; Tom got the relief of not having to admit a limitation.

The tell was in Alex's own behaviour rather than Tom's: he had left three meetings without asking the question he knew he should ask. That is deferring, and it is the pattern that is most rewarded in the moment and most expensive later.

The reset, at week 16, began with Alex's own contribution rather than Tom's: "I've been working as though you'd take this to Elaine yourself. Is that fair, or am I putting you in an awkward position?"

Tom's answer: he had no route to Elaine and had been embarrassed to say so. Ten weeks of stall resolved in one exchange, and the fix — a two-page brief Tom could forward without personal risk — took an afternoon. Naming your own half is what makes the reset land without defensiveness.

7. Weak versus improved reset

Composite Case StudyScenario: A customer escalation meeting after an implementation slipped. The customer is angry, the implementation lead is defensive, and the account manager needs to change the shape of the room.

Weak execution (entering the triangle)

Customer: "This is the third time your team has missed a date. Frankly it's been a disaster and my exec team is asking why we chose you."
Account manager: "I completely understand, and I want to say I'm so sorry — this is not how we normally operate at all. Let me take full ownership here. I'll personally get on top of this, I'll chase our engineering team every day, and I'll make sure you don't have to worry about it. Leave it with me."
Customer: "Fine. But if it slips again…"

Why it fails: The account manager has taken the rescuer position, which completes the triangle rather than breaking it — someone is now aggrieved, someone is rescuing, and engineering has been quietly cast as the persecutor. "Leave it with me" removes the customer's agency and takes on an obligation the account manager cannot deliver, because they do not control engineering. Nothing checkable was established: no cause, no owner, no date, no criterion. The next slip is now a personal betrayal rather than a project problem.

Improved execution (leave the triangle, return to evidence and shared agency)

Customer: "This is the third time your team has missed a date…"
Account manager: "That's a fair thing to be angry about, and I'm not going to argue with the facts — three dates, three slips. I also don't want to give you another reassurance, because you've had two of those from me and they didn't help.

Can I do something different? I'd like to spend the next twenty minutes on what actually caused each of the three, because I think two of them have the same cause and one is different, and if I'm right that changes what we should do.

Then I'd like to agree three things with you: what the next date is, what has to be true for it to hold, and what we both do if it doesn't — including what your team needs to provide, because at least one of the slips was waiting on data from your side and I've been avoiding saying that.

And if after all that you'd rather escalate above me, I'll set that up myself."

Why it succeeds: The account manager acknowledged the facts without absorbing the rescuer role, and explicitly declined to offer another reassurance — naming that the previous ones failed. The conversation moved to cause, which is checkable, rather than to blame, which is not. Agency was made mutual, including a difficult truth about the customer's own contribution, raised only after the vendor's failures were fully conceded. And offering to arrange the escalation removes the threat's power and signals confidence rather than defensiveness.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced sellers learn to name interaction patterns and then use the naming as a move rather than a correction.

"I think we've got into a rhythm where I chase and you reassure" is a genuinely useful sentence. Deployed as a technique — because it has worked before, because it reliably produces candour — it becomes a lever, and sophisticated buyers detect the difference immediately. The tell is that it arrives smoothly, at a moment of commercial convenience, and is followed by an ask.

The distinction is whether you are prepared for the answer to be about you. A genuine reset invites the possibility that the pattern is your doing and that fixing it costs you something — as at Brayford, where Alex's own deferring was the half that had to change and the fix was to write a brief rather than to press for an introduction.

If naming the pattern is always followed by the buyer conceding, you are not resetting the interaction. You are running a more sophisticated version of it.

The mechanism: A candour move performed for effect is structurally identical to the pattern it claims to interrupt: both are the seller managing the interaction rather than participating in it. Because the technique produces short-term disclosure, it is reinforced — and the seller acquires a reliable instrument for extracting candour while remaining exactly as unavailable to correction as before.

8. Practice: the conversation reset script

Exercise goal: identify the interaction patterns you personally fall into and write the specific language that resets them.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Review three stalled deals or difficult accounts. For each, describe the interaction pattern in behavioral terms — what you do, what they do — without labelling either of you.
  2. Find your default. Most sellers have one of the three: rescuing, instructing, or deferring. Which do you do under pressure?
  3. Use the tells: Am I doing work their organization should own, with no named counterpart? Did I state their problem before they did? Did I leave a meeting without asking the question I knew I should ask?
  4. Write the reset language for each, in your own voice, starting with your own contribution. Not a technique to deploy on them — a change you are making.
  5. Write the "thing I am avoiding" question for one live account, verbatim, and use it this week.
  6. Write your escalation-meeting opening — the one that acknowledges facts without entering the triangle and without offering another reassurance.
  7. Define the evidence close for each script: what would you agree at the end that is checkable?
  8. After using each script, record what actually happened. Some will not work; note which and why.

Evaluation rubric: Patterns described behaviorally with no labelling of persons (20%); own default identified honestly (20%); reset language written in your own voice starting with your own contribution (25%); the avoided question written verbatim and used (15%); evidence close defined for each script (10%); outcomes recorded including failures (10%).

Beginner variation: Do step 5 only. Write the question you have been avoiding on one account and ask it this week. It is the highest-value single action in this module and it takes one sentence.

Advanced variation: Audit a team's stalled deals for the pattern distribution. Teams frequently share a default — often deferring, because it is the one most rewarded by a culture that dislikes bad news in pipeline reviews. If so, that is a management finding (Module 18) about what the team believes it is allowed to say, not an individual coaching finding.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. What is the evidential status of Transactional Analysis and what does that permit?

Limited. Systematic reviews of TA-based interventions find a small literature with substantial methodological weakness, and the ego-state model has no validated measurement instrument. That permits using it as a vocabulary for describing interaction patterns — which are directly observable — and prohibits using it to diagnose a person or predict their behavior. "This exchange has become instructing and complying" is legitimate; "she operates from Child ego state" is not.

2. Why is rescuing a problem when it feels helpful to both parties?

Because it puts you in a position you cannot deliver from. Building the buyer's business case, chasing their finance team, and drafting their board paper means the work is being done by someone with no authority inside their organization — and a paper authored by a vendor reads differently to a board than one from an internal sponsor. It also removes the buyer's agency, which means nobody on their side is invested. The tell is simple: you are doing work their organization should own and you cannot name who on their side owns it.

3. What is the tell for deferring, and why is it the most expensive pattern?

The tell is leaving a meeting without asking the question you knew you should ask — for access, for a decision, for an honest no. It is the most expensive because every individual deferral keeps the relationship pleasant and the cost lands months later, where it is attributed to something else entirely. Four months of accepting "he's taking it up" is not patience; it is a question unasked, and the answer is usually available in one sentence.

4. In an escalation, why does "leave it with me" make things worse?

Because it completes a drama triangle rather than breaking it: the customer is the aggrieved party, you become the rescuer, and someone internal is implicitly cast as the cause. It takes on an obligation you probably cannot deliver, since you do not control the delivery team, and it removes the customer's agency at a moment when their contribution may be part of the problem. Nothing checkable is established — no cause, no owner, no date, no criterion — so the next slip becomes a personal betrayal rather than a project issue.

5. Why should a reset start with your own contribution to the pattern?

Because it removes defensiveness and keeps you on the right side of the line between describing an interaction and diagnosing a person. "I've been running this as if it were my project" or "I told you what your problem was before I'd earned the right to" invites collaboration; anything that begins with what they are doing invites a defence. It also happens to be accurate — these patterns are jointly maintained, and the half you can change unilaterally is your own.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

This lens earns a small, real place: it gives you names for three interaction shapes that quietly ruin commercial conversations, and a set of moves for changing them. It gives you nothing at all about who a person is, and any use in that direction is misuse.

  • TA's evidence base is weak; use it as vocabulary for interactions, never as diagnosis.
  • Ego states describe utterances, not people. Everyone speaks in all three within one meeting.
  • Three patterns cause most of the damage: rescuing, instructing, deferring.
  • The tells: doing their work with no named counterpart; stating their problem first; leaving without asking.
  • Reset by naming your own contribution, returning agency, and asking rather than asserting.
  • In escalations, decline to offer another reassurance and move to cause.
  • End every reset in something checkable — an owner, a date, a criterion, a confirmed problem statement.

Immediate action: Pick your longest-stalled account and write down the question you have been avoiding. Ask it this week, in one sentence, with an explicit statement that either answer is fine. It is the single highest-value action in this module.

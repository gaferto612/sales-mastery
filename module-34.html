1. Use a business case for a decision—not as ROI theater

A business case helps a customer decide whether a change deserves scarce money, attention, and implementation capacity. Sellers, finance partners, operational owners, procurement teams, founders, and account managers use it when the decision is large enough that a verbal value claim is inadequate. It may support a new purchase, renewal, expansion, build-versus-buy choice, or a decision to do nothing.

The work sits between discovery and a commercial decision. Discovery supplies a shared diagnosis; the business case tests whether the proposed change is economically and operationally worthwhile. Doing it badly creates false precision, damaged trust, implementation disappointment, and forecasts based on benefits the customer never accepted.

Three misconceptions cause most problems: value is not the same as seller revenue; every hour “saved” does not become cash; and a spreadsheet with many decimals is not necessarily rigorous. A credible case makes uncertainty visible and can support a smaller solution, a pilot, a delay, or no purchase.

Observable learning objectives

By the end, you can map a value-driver tree, classify evidence, calculate scenarios and payback, challenge double counting, facilitate customer validation, and recommend an appropriate next decision.

2. Build it with the customer

  1. Define the decision. Record the alternatives, decision owner, audience, timing, and what the case must establish.
  2. Confirm the current-state mechanism. Map workflow, volume, error or delay, frequency, consequence, owner, and prior attempts. Do not monetize a symptom whose cause is unknown.
  3. Create the value-driver tree. Link capability → behavior or workflow change → operational metric → realizable financial or strategic effect.
  4. Build a source ledger. For every input record unit, period, owner, source, status, confidence, and validation action.
  5. Model total change cost. Include price, implementation, integration, internal labor, migration, training, disruption, governance, ongoing administration, and opportunity cost where material.
  6. Model ranges. Use downside, base, and upside assumptions. Separate addressable value from expected and realized value.
  7. Test realization. Name the owner, adoption dependency, implementation milestone, measurement method, and action required to turn an operational gain into cash or capacity.
  8. Review with the customer. Ask finance and operational owners to correct assumptions. Preserve disagreement rather than averaging it away.
  9. Choose the next decision. Proceed, pilot, reduce scope, collect evidence, delay, or stop.
Decision rule

If the recommendation changes only when an assumption is slightly adjusted, the case is fragile. Investigate that assumption before presenting a confident conclusion.

For a small local business, a one-page calculation and owner review may be enough. Enterprise work often needs finance, IT, security, procurement, and implementation inputs. Professional services cases must distinguish utilization from actual billable capacity. Public-sector cases may require an approved cost method and cannot assume commercial discretion.

Confidence belongs to an input, not to the seller’s enthusiasm. A customer benchmark can provide a starting range, but it is not the customer’s baseline. Avoid adding revenue growth and labor savings when both describe the same underlying benefit. Do not count freed time as cash unless headcount, overtime, contractor spend, throughput, or avoided hiring will actually change.

4. Worked example: invoice-dispute workflow

Illustrative composite scenario—not a real company result. A 120-person logistics company is evaluating workflow software. Discovery suggests repeated invoice exceptions, but finance has not approved the value claim. The team is deciding between software, a process redesign, and retaining the current workflow.

The rework baseline is 108 × 35 ÷ 60 = 63 hours per month. At €42 per hour, that is €2,646 monthly or €31,752 annually in capacity. Finance says no position will be removed; therefore it is capacity value, not immediate cash. The contractor budget is a better cash driver if the workflow can demonstrably reduce external dispute administration.

Value-driver tree

Validation rules and automated routing → fewer avoidable exceptions and faster ownership → reduced contractor administration plus capacity returned to billing → lower external spend, quicker close, and fewer customer escalations. Adoption, data quality, exception mix, integration reliability, and manager enforcement constrain realization.

Costs and scenario model

Year-zero implementation and internal change cost is €10,000. Recurring license and administration cost is €20,000 per year. The table treats modeled gross benefit as a combined, de-duplicated estimate of realizable contractor reduction and conservatively valued capacity.

Simple payback = initial change cost ÷ monthly net benefit. The simplified NPV is −€10,000 plus three annual net benefits discounted at 8%. A real model would use actual cash timing, tax treatment where appropriate, contract terms, ramp, residual value, and finance-approved discount rate. The downside recommends no rollout. The base case may justify a measured pilot, not an immediate enterprise-wide claim.

Sensitivity and non-financial value

The case breaks even on recurring economics when gross annual benefit reaches €20,000. The highest-leverage uncertainty is exception reduction; rework time and realizable contractor reduction follow. Change one input at a time, then test plausible combinations. Non-financial value—cleaner audit evidence, fewer escalations, and clearer ownership—should be described and measured separately rather than converted into invented euros.

Risks: exceptions may originate upstream; adoption may be low; the integration may create new failures; contractor spend may be fixed; and returned capacity may not be redeployed. The pilot should measure exception mix, adoption, time per case, external invoices, and unintended effects.

5. Finance review: challenge the assumptions

Finance: “You call €31,752 a saving. Which cash line disappears?”
Seller: “None automatically. It is capacity. We should show it separately unless you confirm avoided hiring, overtime, or contractor reduction.”

Finance: “Where does 40% exception reduction come from?”
Seller: “It is an unverified base assumption, not a forecast. We propose a six-week pilot with a downside threshold and the same exception definition.”

Finance: “Why include the contractor budget and labor time—are they the same work?”
Seller: “They may overlap. We need a sample that attributes internal and external work. Until then, the model caps combined benefit to avoid double counting.”

Finance: “What would make you recommend against buying?”
Seller: “If exceptions are mostly caused outside the addressable workflow, adoption stays below the agreed threshold, or recurring gross benefit remains under €20,000.”

6. Weak versus credible execution

Exaggerated claim: “Our AI eliminates 80% of errors and delivers 10× ROI in year one.” It treats a vendor estimate as customer fact, hides total cost and timing, and implies certainty.

Improved claim: “The current logs show 108 exceptions a month. We have not established how many this workflow can prevent. If a pilot confirms a 40% reduction and finance can realize the contractor savings, the base model produces about €8,900 annual net benefit after recurring cost, with roughly 13.5-month payback on implementation. The downside case is negative, so rollout should depend on agreed evidence.”

The improved version is traceable and decision-oriented, but still weak until finance validates cost treatment, operations confirms causality, and the pilot measures adoption and unintended effects.

7. Failure modes and stop rules

  • False baseline: one memorable incident is annualized. Detect it by asking for period and denominator; use a representative sample.
  • Cash-capacity confusion: hours are multiplied by salary. Ask which spend or throughput changes; label capacity separately.
  • Double counting: faster work, labor saving, and revenue gain describe the same effect. Trace every benefit to its mechanism.
  • Missing change cost: license is compared with gross value. Include internal labor, integration, disruption, and ongoing governance.
  • Seller-owned model: the customer sees the sheet only at proposal. Co-create inputs and record disagreements early.
  • Precision without evidence: decimals hide weak assumptions. Round appropriately and show ranges.
  • Ignoring alternatives: only purchase and status quo are compared. Include process change, internal build, smaller scope, delay, and no action.

Stop or reduce scope when the causal mechanism is unverified, the customer will not provide material inputs, the implementation owner is absent, downside risk is unacceptable, or the solution cannot plausibly affect the measured driver.

8. Practice: build a finance-reviewable case

Objective: produce a transparent decision model and recommend proceed, pilot, reduce scope, investigate, or stop.

Scenario and inputs: A construction-services firm processes 800 change orders monthly. Operations reports 12% rework and 50 minutes per reworked order; finance supplies a €48 loaded hourly cost. A proposed tool costs €24,000 annually, €8,000 to implement, and 120 internal hours. The seller assumes 35% rework reduction. Customer delay penalties vary and are not verified.

  1. Define the decision, alternatives, stakeholders, and missing evidence.
  2. Classify each input as observed, reported, calculated, or assumed.
  3. Build a capability-to-value driver tree.
  4. Calculate the baseline without treating capacity as cash.
  5. Create downside, base, and upside scenarios, total cost, payback, and a simple three-year NPV using an explained discount rate.
  6. Run sensitivity on the two assumptions most likely to change the recommendation.
  7. Write five customer validation questions and a pilot measurement plan.
  8. Prepare a one-page recommendation that includes risks and a stop rule.

Required output: source ledger, formulas, scenario table, sensitivity note, proof plan, executive memo, and decision recommendation. Time: 90 minutes plus peer review.

Rubric: evidence classification 20%; formula and unit accuracy 20%; realization logic and de-duplication 20%; scenario/sensitivity quality 15%; decision usefulness 15%; uncertainty and ethics 10%.

Beginner variation: calculate annual capacity only and list what would make it cash-realizable. Advanced variation: add ramped monthly cash flows, implementation delay, probability-weighted scenarios, and finance-approved discounting.

Strong partial response

“The reported baseline implies 80 rework hours per month, but this is capacity, not a €46,080 cash saving. Before modeling purchase value, sample fifty change orders, verify whether the proposed workflow addresses their causes, and ask finance whether overtime, contractor spend, or avoided hiring can change.”

Self-review: Can another person reproduce every formula? Which input could reverse the decision? Did you count any benefit twice? Who owns realization? What evidence would make no purchase the right answer?

10. Field checklist and next action

  • Decision, audience, alternatives, and owner are explicit.
  • Every material input has unit, period, source, status, and confidence.
  • Capacity, cash, revenue, risk, and non-financial value are separated.
  • Total change cost, ramp, downside, and stop criteria are visible.
  • Benefits are not double counted and realization has an owner.
  • The customer can correct the model and reproduce the formulas.

Immediate action: take one value claim in an active opportunity and label every number observed, reported, calculated, or assumed. Ask the customer which assumption should be verified first.

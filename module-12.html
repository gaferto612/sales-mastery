12Professional Ethics

Manipulation is the same techniques with the evidence removed.

Sales ethics is not a set of prohibitions bolted onto commercial practice. It is a decision procedure that also happens to produce better commercial outcomes — and a working knowledge of where regulators have drawn the line.

1. Why ethics is a commercial discipline

Sales ethics is usually taught as a list of things not to do, delivered in a compliance module nobody remembers. That framing fails for a practical reason: real dilemmas are not obviously wrong. Nobody agonizes over whether to commit fraud. They agonize over whether to mention the roadmap item that might not ship, whether to let a buyer's optimistic assumption stand uncorrected, whether to close a deal they suspect will not deliver the promised result.

What makes these hard is that the ethical cost is immediate and the commercial cost is deferred. Disclosing the defect might lose this deal; not disclosing it costs you a renewal, a reference, and a reputation eighteen months from now — outside the window in which you are measured.

Two things make this tractable. The first is a decision procedure that works in the moment, before you have time to rationalize. The second is recognizing that the deferred costs are real and large: Module 10's worked example put the realized-value difference between a pressure-closed and a readiness-closed deal at roughly 6.7×. Ethics and long-run commercial interest are far more aligned than the quarterly report suggests. Where they genuinely diverge, that divergence is usually a compensation design problem — which is Module 41's subject, and which is a better thing to fix than the individual's character.

The organizing principle from Module 02 is worth restating because it does most of the work: influence is legitimate when it compresses evidence the buyer would endorse if they had gathered it themselves, and illegitimate when it substitutes for evidence that does not exist. Manipulation is not a separate toolkit. It is the same techniques with the evidence removed.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Apply four decision tests to a live commercial dilemma and reach a defensible position.

Identify dark patterns in your own organization's flows and name the regulatory exposure each creates.

Distinguish structural pressure from individual misconduct, and address the structural cause.

Disclose a known defect in a way that preserves the relationship and often the deal.

Write a personal ethics charter with pre-committed positions and an escalation path.

3. Four tests that resolve almost every case

These are ordered by speed. The first takes two seconds and settles most cases; run the later ones only when the earlier ones do not resolve.

Table 1: The four tests

TestThe questionWhat it catchesExample application
1. Disclosure test"Would I be comfortable if the buyer could see exactly what I am doing and why?"Concealment of motive. The fastest and most reliable of the four.A countdown timer that resets fails instantly. A real capacity constraint passes.
2. Informed-consent test"Is the buyer making this decision with the material information a reasonable person would want?"Omission — the most common form of sales dishonesty, because it requires no false statement.Knowing the integration they need is on a roadmap with no committed date, and letting them assume it exists.
3. Reversal test"Would I accept this being done to me, or to someone I am responsible for?"Rationalizations that sound fine from the seller's chair.An auto-renewing contract with a 90-day cancellation window buried in clause 14.3.
4. Publication test"Would this survive being described accurately by a journalist, a regulator, or a competitor's counsel?"Practices that are technically defensible and indefensible in plain language."Our unsubscribe requires calling during business hours" is legal in some jurisdictions and unpublishable in all of them.
The test that resolves the most common real dilemma

The everyday dilemma is not whether to lie; it is whether to volunteer. The informed-consent test handles it: ask what a reasonable buyer would want to know, not what they happened to ask. If they would want to know it and you are staying quiet because telling them costs you the deal, that is the answer. The commercial consolation is genuine — Module 07 and Module 09 both show that volunteered limitations raise credibility and downstream conversion, because a buyer who sees you disclose one thing stops discounting everything else by an unknown factor.

4. Dark patterns and the regulatory line

Class L. What was once a design debate is now an enforcement area. The FTC's Bringing Dark Patterns to Light and equivalent European and UK actions have moved a set of common commercial practices from "aggressive" to "actionable."

Table 2: Dark patterns, mechanism, and exposure

PatternHow it worksWhere you will find it in your own organizationFix
Drip pricing / hidden costsThe total is revealed only at the final step, after effort is invested.Shipping, setup fees, mandatory add-ons, per-seat minimums surfacing at contract.Show the total early. It is also the largest documented cause of cart abandonment (~48%), so this fix pays for itself.
Roach motelEasy to enter, hard to leave.Online signup with phone-only cancellation; 90-day notice windows; auto-renewal with a buried opt-out.Cancellation must be at least as easy as signup. Several jurisdictions now require this explicitly.
ConfirmshamingThe decline option is worded to induce guilt."No thanks, I don't care about my team's productivity."Neutral decline language. It fails the reversal test on sight.
False scarcity / urgencyFabricated deadlines or stock levels.Recurring "final day," "only 2 left" that never depletes, quarter-end-only pricing that reappears next quarter.Use real constraints only. See Module 02 for why the expected-value case also fails.
Pre-selectionConsent or add-ons ticked by default.Marketing consent checkboxes; auto-added support tiers.Opt-in must be affirmative. Under GDPR, pre-ticked consent is not consent at all.
Obscured comparisonPricing structured so that alternatives cannot be compared.Non-comparable units, undisclosed overage rates, "contact us" tiers that prevent evaluation.Publish the comparison basis even where you do not publish the price.

Two of these deserve special attention from sellers rather than designers. Drip pricing often enters B2B through the contract rather than the website — a per-seat minimum or an overage rate the buyer discovers at signature is functionally the same practice. And roach-motel terms are frequently negotiated into deals by sellers under pressure, because a 90-day notice period improves a retention metric. It improves it by removing the customer's choice, which is precisely the definition.

5. Where the pressure actually comes from

Most sales misconduct is not committed by dishonest people. It is committed by ordinary people responding rationally to a system, and treating it as a character problem guarantees it recurs.

The most useful thing an individual can do

When you catch yourself rationalizing, ask what would have to be true about the system for this to be the obvious right thing to do? The answer is usually a design flaw you can name — and naming it, in writing, to someone who can change it, is more valuable than personally resisting it in silence. Individual virtue does not scale; a changed compensation plan does.

The Brayford thread · Week 6 and week 27 · two disclosure decisions

What honesty cost, precisely

Twice in this deal Cadence disclosed something it was not asked about, and the course claims neither was free.

Week 6. Alex disclosed that Cadence's audit-trail export needs a manual reconciliation step — roughly two hours a month — and that the fix had already slipped once. Three weeks later Tom Beckett wrote the requirements with reporting weighted at 25%, a criterion Stafford wins. The disclosure measurably cost ground.

Week 27. With the contract at four sites, Alex told Elaine that the Marlow estate's legacy system had no API and that Cadence could not deliver the promised mechanism there at any price until it was replaced. That removed fourteen sites from the deal.

What the disclosures bought: during the six-week stall at weeks 14–20, when Alex told Priya that more evidence was not the problem, she believed him. A seller who had never volunteered anything against their own interest has nothing to spend at that moment. The return arrived eight weeks after the cost, which is exactly why the trade is hard to see from inside a quarter.

7. Weak versus improved handling of a known defect

Composite Case StudyScenario: Late-stage deal. The seller knows the reporting module cannot produce the audit export the buyer's compliance team will need, and that the fix is at least two quarters away. The buyer has not asked about it.

Weak execution (technically true, materially misleading)

Buyer: "And reporting is all handled in the platform, right?"
Seller: "Yes, absolutely — there's a full reporting suite, you can export to CSV and Excel, and the dashboards are configurable. Our customers do a lot with it."
[Signs. Month 3: compliance discovers the audit export requires a manual reconciliation step. Escalation, credit demanded, reference refused, renewal at risk.]

Why it fails: Every individual statement is true. The composite is misleading, because the seller knew the specific thing the buyer would need and answered a broader question instead. This fails the informed-consent test cleanly. It also fails commercially: the defect was going to be discovered in month three regardless, so the seller purchased eleven weeks of a signed contract at the cost of the relationship, the reference, and probably the renewal.

Improved execution (disclose, quantify, offer the exit)

Buyer: "And reporting is all handled in the platform, right?"
Seller: "Mostly, and there's one part I need to be direct about because I think it matters to you specifically. Standard reporting and dashboards, yes. But the audit export your compliance team will want — the one with the full change history in a single file — currently needs a manual reconciliation step at the end. It's about two hours a month for one person. It's on the roadmap for Q3 and I'm not going to promise you that date, because we've moved it once already.

I'd rather you knew now than in month three. There are three ways to handle it: you accept the manual step and we credit you if Q3 slips again; we scope a small custom export as part of implementation at our cost; or if this is genuinely a blocker for compliance, you should weight it heavily and it's reasonable to choose someone else. Which of those do you want to explore?"
Buyer: "Let's get compliance on a call and see how bad two hours actually is."

Why it succeeds: The disclosure is specific and quantified rather than a vague hedge — "two hours a month for one person" is something the buyer can evaluate. The roadmap uncertainty is stated honestly, including the previous slip. Three options are offered including the one that loses the deal, which is what makes the other two credible. And the problem moves from a month-three escalation, where the seller has no leverage and no goodwill, to a pre-signature negotiation where it can be priced.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

The experienced failure is not lying. It is the timing arbitrage: disclosing true things late enough that they no longer threaten the deal. The seller who discloses the integration gap after the security review, or the roadmap slip after the business case is approved, has said nothing false and has fully preserved the outcome.

It feels like judgment rather than deception — "I didn't want to derail the conversation before they understood the value" — and it is the most common rationalisation in commercial selling, because it is partly true.

The test that catches it is not about content but about counterfactual: would you have disclosed this if it had been in your favour? Sellers volunteer good news immediately and schedule bad news, and the asymmetry in your own timing is the measurement. If every disclosure that helps you happens in week 2 and every disclosure that hurts you happens in week 20, you are running an arbitrage regardless of how you would describe it.

The mechanism: Sequencing converts a disclosure obligation into a negotiating position. Information withheld until the buyer has invested effort, political capital, and internal advocacy is information delivered when the cost of acting on it is highest — which extracts a decision the buyer would not have made at week 2, without any false statement having been made.

8. Practice: your personal sales ethics charter

Exercise goal: pre-commit to positions on the dilemmas you will actually face, before you face them under pressure.

Applies to your real situation
  1. List the five dilemmas you actually encounter — from your own experience, not a textbook. If you cannot name five, ask two colleagues; theirs will prompt yours.
  2. Run each through the four tests and write your position as a rule you can apply in the moment.
  3. Write your disclosure list: the specific things you will always volunteer even when not asked. Be concrete — "our mobile app is weaker than X's" beats "product limitations."
  4. Write your refusal list: the things you will not do regardless of pressure. Keep it short enough to be real. Three items you will hold beats twelve you will not.
  5. Audit your own organization against Table 2. Name every dark pattern you find, including in contracts, and identify who owns each.
  6. Identify one structural pressure from Table 3 operating on you, and write the change you would propose to whoever can make it.
  7. Write your escalation path: who you go to, in what order, if asked to do something on your refusal list — and what you will do if that path fails.

Evaluation rubric: Dilemmas drawn from real experience (15%); positions derived from the four tests and stated as applicable rules (20%); disclosure list specific rather than generic (20%); refusal list short and genuinely holdable (10%); organizational audit naming real patterns and owners (20%); structural pressure identified with a concrete proposal (15%).

Beginner variation: Write only the disclosure list — five specific things you will always volunteer. Then use it for a month and note what happened to your conversations. Most people find it changes the questions buyers ask them.

Advanced variation: Run the dark-pattern audit as a team exercise across marketing, product, and contracts, and present the findings with the regulatory exposure attached to each. Bring the fixes rather than the accusations; the aim is a changed flow, not a chastened colleague.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. What distinguishes influence from manipulation?

The evidence behind the claim, not the technique. Influence compresses evidence the buyer would endorse if they had gathered it themselves — naming three comparable organizations and their measured results, or stating a genuine capacity constraint. Manipulation uses the identical technique with the evidence removed: unnamed customer counts, a deadline that does not exist. This is why "which tactics are manipulative?" is the wrong question; almost every tactic can be either, and the test is whether the underlying fact is true, relevant, and checkable.

2. A buyer makes an incorrect assumption in your favour and does not ask about it. Must you correct them?

Yes, if a reasonable buyer would want to know — which is the informed-consent test, and it does not depend on whether you were asked. Omission is the most common form of sales dishonesty precisely because it requires no false statement, which makes it easy to rationalize. There is also a strong practical argument: the assumption will be discovered, usually in month three when you have no leverage and no goodwill, and the cost then exceeds the deal you protected. Correcting a buyer's error in your own favour is also one of the fastest credibility-building acts available.

3. Your company's contract has a 90-day cancellation notice that improves reported retention. Is this a dark pattern?

Yes — it is a roach-motel term. It improves the metric by removing the customer's ability to leave rather than by giving them a reason to stay, which is the defining mechanism. The diagnostic question is whether cancellation is as easy as signing up; if entry takes an online form and exit takes three months of notice, the asymmetry is the pattern. It also fails the reversal test immediately. Worth noting: when organizations shorten these periods they frequently find retention unchanged, which reveals that the term was protecting nothing but the number.

4. Why is treating sales misconduct as a character problem a mistake?

Because it guarantees recurrence. Most misconduct is ordinary people responding rationally to incentives: commission paid entirely at signature makes a churning deal profitable for the seller; a quarterly cliff makes manufactured urgency the obvious move; measuring meetings booked makes loose targeting rational. Ethics training does not change any of these payoffs. The productive move when you catch yourself rationalizing is to ask what would have to be true about the system for this to be obviously right — and then name that design flaw, in writing, to someone who can change it.

5. Disclosing a defect will probably lose you this quarter's deal. What is the argument for disclosing anyway, beyond ethics?

That the deal is not actually what is at stake. The defect will surface in implementation regardless, at which point you face an escalation, a credit demand, a refused reference, and a renewal at risk — so the choice is not between winning and losing but between a pre-signature negotiation where the problem can be priced and a month-three crisis where it cannot. Beyond that, disclosed limitations measurably raise downstream conversion and credibility, because a buyer who sees you volunteer one weakness stops discounting all your other claims by an unknown factor. The honest deal is usually the larger one, just later.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Ethics in selling is not a constraint on effectiveness. It is a decision procedure that keeps you able to be believed, in a profession where being believed is the entire asset — and it is far more commercially aligned than a quarterly report can show.

  • Manipulation is the same techniques with the evidence removed. Test the evidence, not the tactic.
  • Run the disclosure test first; it settles most cases in two seconds.
  • The informed-consent test governs the real everyday dilemma, which is omission rather than falsehood.
  • Dark patterns are now an enforcement area, not a design debate. Audit contracts as well as interfaces.
  • Most misconduct is structural. Name the incentive, propose the fix, and put it in writing.
  • Disclose defects specifically and quantified, and offer the option that loses you the deal — it is what makes the others credible.
  • Pre-commit to your hard cases in a calm week, because you will not reason well in a bad one.

Immediate action: Write your disclosure list today — five specific things you will always volunteer even when not asked. Then find one dark pattern in your own organization's flows or contracts and raise it in writing, with the fix attached rather than the accusation.

12Ethics & Pitfalls

Persuasion vs. manipulation.

Sales gets a bad reputation because some sellers earn it. The techniques in this course are powerful — and the same techniques used differently produce either a long career or a short, ugly one.

Persuasion vs. manipulation

Both use the same psychological tools. The difference is in who benefits and what's being hidden.

✓ Persuasion

Buyer is better off after the sale
All material facts are available to them
You'd be proud to tell them everything you know
Buyer makes the decision, freely
If they say no, that's fine
You'd sell it to your mother / best friend

✗ Manipulation

Seller is better off, buyer isn't
Material facts are hidden, distorted, or buried
You'd be embarrassed if they knew what you know
Buyer is pressured past their judgment
"No" is treated as an obstacle to overcome
You'd never recommend it to people you love
Persuasion is helping someone decide what's already in their interest. Manipulation is making them decide what's in yours.

The mirror test

Run this checklist before any deal where you have doubts.

Would I be comfortable if this buyer knew everything I know?
Will they be glad they bought, in 6 months?
Would I recommend this to my own family in the same situation?
Am I hiding any information that would change their mind?
Am I using any pressure that comes from my needs, not theirs?
Would this sale survive being reported on the front page of a newspaper?

If you can't honestly check every box, you're crossing a line. The career cost compounds — every shady deal makes the next one easier to do, until you find yourself somewhere you didn't mean to be.

Dark patterns to recognize and avoid

Fake scarcity

Pretending you're running out when you aren't. Countdown timers that reset. "Only 3 left!" on a digital product.

Cost: buyer eventually notices. You become a brand they don't trust. They tell others.

Fake social proof

Fabricated testimonials, fake review counts, AI-generated quotes from people who don't exist.

Cost: when discovered (and someone always discovers), reputational damage is permanent.

Buried terms

Hiding auto-renewals, cancellation friction, hidden fees in fine print.

Cost: legal exposure, chargebacks, regulatory attention, customer hatred.

Drip-pricing

Showing a low price upfront, then adding mandatory fees at checkout (booking fees, service charges, taxes that should've been included).

Cost: high cart-abandonment rates, brand reputation as deceptive.

Confirm-shaming

"No thanks, I don't want to save money" / "I'd rather pay full price." Manipulative opt-outs.

Cost: customers experience this as condescending. Conversion uplift comes with brand damage.

Emotional exploitation

Targeting people in crisis (illness, grief, financial desperation) with high-pressure sales.

Cost: this is the line between a salesperson and a predator. Sometimes legally — always morally.

Forced urgency

"Decide now or you lose this price forever" — when the offer is renewed weekly.

Cost: sophisticated buyers spot it instantly. Trains your audience to never trust your timing claims.

Bait and switch

Advertising one thing, delivering another. Pricing model shifts after signup.

Cost: chargebacks, churn, lawsuits in regulated industries.

Career-ending mistakes

Things that end sales careers — not in one move, but compounding:

  • Lying about the product — when discovered, you're done at this company and several others (sales is a small world)
  • Misrepresenting numbers — fudged results, inflated case studies, made-up metrics
  • Burning customers for short-term commission — they'll tell others; the industry remembers
  • Bad-mouthing competitors — signals insecurity, not strength
  • Burning bridges with internal teams — engineering, support, finance will refuse to back you eventually
  • Failing to follow through — every broken promise is a deposit in the trust account, withdrawn
  • Selling things you wouldn't buy yourself — destroys conviction; buyers feel it

When to walk away from a deal

Sometimes the right move is no deal.

  • The buyer needs something your product can't deliver
  • The fit is wrong; they'll churn or be unhappy
  • The buyer is asking you to misrepresent something
  • The terms are abusive — you're losing money to win revenue
  • Your gut says no
The paradox

Walking away from a bad-fit deal often wins the deal. Buyers trust someone willing to say "we're not right for you." That trust closes the next deal — and gets you referrals.

The long game

Every interaction is two transactions: the immediate deal and the reputation deposit.

You can win nearly every immediate deal by being aggressive, manipulative, and short-term focused. You will lose every reputation deposit, and over a 20-year career that's the entire game.

The salespeople who become legends:

  • Have customers who follow them across companies
  • Get referrals so often they barely need to prospect
  • Are trusted by buyers and their internal teams
  • Move up because everyone wants to work with them
  • Sleep well at night
Play the long game. The short game ends quickly — and usually badly.

Exercises

  1. Mirror-test a recent deal. Run the 6-question checklist above on a deal you closed (or are considering). Honestly — where would you not check the box?
  2. Dark-pattern audit. Visit 5 e-commerce or SaaS sites. Spot the dark patterns. Which would you refuse to use? Where would you draw lines?
  3. Walk-away role-play. With a partner, role-play declining a deal that isn't a fit. Notice how hard or easy it feels. Practice the language.
  4. Letter to your future self. Write the kind of seller you want to be in 10 years. Specific behaviors. Keep it.

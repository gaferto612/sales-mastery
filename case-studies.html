Case 01 · Featured

The dropshipper who lost on margin and won on retention.

An ambitious 24-year-old launches a dropshipping store selling phone accessories. Spending €5,000/month on Meta ads at €15 customer acquisition cost. Revenue is growing. They feel like they've cracked it.

The collapse

At month 6, ad costs rise (auction inflation), iOS privacy changes break attribution, and CPM doubles. CAC climbs to €28. AOV is €31. Margin is gone. They're now paying €5K/month to break even, and any dip in ad performance pushes them into losses. They burn out and consider quitting.

The turnaround

A mentor asks: "What's your repeat rate?" They check. It's 4%.

The mentor says: "You're not running a business. You're running a paid traffic experiment with leaking buckets."

Over the next 3 months they:

  • Add a post-purchase email sequence (6 emails over 30 days)
  • Bundle a high-margin add-on at checkout (+€8 AOV)
  • Launch a 90-day loyalty/restock email targeting first-time buyers
  • Improve the unboxing experience with a hand-written-style thank-you card

Repeat rate climbs to 18%. AOV climbs to €38. Now each customer is worth €50 lifetime, not €31. CAC of €28 finally makes sense. The business becomes profitable.

Lessons
  • Module 3 — Revenue = traffic × conversion × AOV × repeat rate. They only had one lever working.
  • Module 10 — Retention is more powerful than acquisition. They were running 80/20 on acquisition; should have been 50/50 by month 3.
  • Module 1 — Selling isn't just the first transaction. It's the whole arc.

B2BEnterpriseMEDDIC
The setup
A SaaS startup runs a beautiful 4-month sale into a Fortune 500. Champion loves them. Demo goes great. Procurement approves price. They send the contract expecting signature within a week.
The collapse
The contract hits the customer's legal team. They flag 3 clauses around liability, data residency, and IP indemnification. The startup's standard contract doesn't accommodate enterprise requirements. Back-and-forth begins. Each round takes 2–3 weeks. The champion gets reassigned. New champion is lukewarm. The deal stalls for 4 more months and quietly dies.
The retrospective
The startup learned the hard way that legal/procurement is its own phase, not a formality. They had no visibility into when legal would engage, who was reviewing, or what objections to expect. They were single-threaded — only knew the champion. When she left, the relationship had no ballast.
The fix going forward
  • Multi-thread every enterprise deal: at minimum 3 contacts (champion, end-user, executive sponsor)
  • Added a "legal-friendly MSA" alongside their standard contract for big customers
  • Map decision processes explicitly during discovery: "Walk me through what happens from us shaking hands to a signed contract — who's involved, what stages?"
  • MEDDIC scoring now includes a "Paper Process" line item
Lessons
  • Module 5 — MEDDIC's P (Paper Process) exists because of exactly this scenario.
  • Module 5 — Single-threading is fragile. Always know 3+ stakeholders.
  • Module 11 — Set expectations honestly: "Enterprise contracts can take 2–3 months in legal — let's start that process now even while we finalize commercials."
RetailDiscoveryOffline
The setup
A retail associate in an electronics shop. Decent at her job. Average commission. She's been told to ask "Can I help you find anything?" — buyers usually say "just looking" and she leaves them alone.
The shift

She changes her opening to:

New opener"What are you trying to figure out today?"

Suddenly buyers tell her things. They're "trying to figure out" if a laptop is right for their college kid, or if a TV will fit a specific living room, or which camera to buy for a trip in 6 weeks. These are huge buying signals, and the original question never surfaced them.

She also learns to hand them the product rather than describe it. And to ask "What will you be using it for?" before recommending anything.

The result
Close rate doubles within 90 days. Average ticket goes up because she upsells appropriately to actual needs. Her referral rate — returning customers who ask for her by name — becomes the highest in the store.
Lessons
  • Module 4 — Open with observation/question, not pitch.
  • Module 4 — Endowment effect: let them hold it.
  • Module 5 — Better discovery → better presentation → better close.
  • Module 1 — Outcome > features. Asking what they'll use it for surfaces the outcome.
CopywritingOutboundB2B
The setup
A small B2B service offers consulting at €5,000/project. Their cold emails get a 1% reply rate. They consider lowering the price to attract more buyers.
The diagnosis

A consultant rewrites their cold email:

Original (1% reply)"Hi [Name], I hope this email finds you well. We provide consulting services for businesses like yours. We help with [3 buzzwords]. Would you be open to a 30-minute call to discuss how we can help?"
Rewrite (12% reply)"Hi [Name] — saw your team just expanded into Germany. Most companies hit a snag around month 6 with VAT compliance — usually it's [specific tax filing issue] that costs €15–40K to clean up. Worth a quick 10 minutes to see if you'll trip on it?"

The new email names a specific event (expansion), names a specific pain (VAT compliance), quantifies the cost, and asks for a low-commitment 10 minutes.

The result
Reply rate jumps from 1% to 12%. Booked calls go from 2/month to 25/month. They don't need to drop their price — they need to be relevant.
Lessons
  • Module 6 — Write to one person. Specificity wins.
  • Module 1 — Buyer awareness: pitch the pain, not the service.
  • Module 5 — Outbound only works when the angle is tight.
  • Module 11 — Solving for the right problem beats discounting on the wrong problem.
NegotiationBATNAFreelance
The setup
A freelance designer is negotiating with a startup. The startup wants a 6-month engagement at €60K. Designer's market rate would put it at €78K. Designer's BATNA: a competing offer at €72K from another company. Reservation price: €68K.
The negotiation
Startup (anchoring low)"Our budget is €50K."
Designer (doesn't counter directly)"I appreciate that's where you are. Help me understand — what's driving the €50K? Is that a hard budget cap or a starting point?"
Startup"Well, it's what we've put aside, but we have some flex."
Designer (resets the anchor)"Okay. The number I'd typically work at for a 6-month scope like this is €80K. Let me walk you through what's included so we can see where it lands."

Walks through scope. Pauses. Lets them respond.

Startup"€80K is a stretch. Could you do €60K?"
Designer (trades variables)"€60K is too low for what we've discussed — I'd be working below my market rate and that's not sustainable. What if we did €72K but scoped down the deliverable count from 12 to 9?"
Startup"Could we do €68K and keep the 12?"
Designer (pauses)"I can do €70K and 12. That's my best."
Startup"Done."
The math
  • Designer aimed at €80K, landed at €70K — better than reservation (€68K)
  • Trading variables (scope) was on the table the whole time
  • Silence after each offer did work
  • Walk-away alternative (€72K competing offer) kept designer calm
Lessons
  • Module 7 — Don't accept their anchor. Reset with your own.
  • Module 7 — Trade variables, not just price.
  • Module 7 — Know your BATNA before negotiating.
  • Module 9 — The pause is a tool. Silence after a number works.
  • Module 11 — Holding firm on real worth doesn't lose deals — it preserves them.
OnlineLTVRetention
The setup
A course creator sells a €197 online course on a niche skill. They have 1,000 buyers and are pleased with the income. They decide to "scale" by running aggressive paid ads. CAC climbs as ad audiences saturate. Refund rate jumps (lower-quality buyers). They burn through €30K in ads in a month and barely break even.
The pivot

They stop ad spend. Look at their existing 1,000 buyers. Notice that:

  • 200 of them deeply loved the course (high engagement, completion, positive feedback)
  • Of those 200, about 60 expressed wanting "more" — coaching, community, advanced material
  • Their email open rate is high among past buyers

They build a €1,500 group coaching cohort with the deeper content. Email the 200 deep fans. 40 sign up in the first cohort. Revenue: €60,000 in 4 weeks, from people who already trusted them, with near-zero acquisition cost.

The principle
Their growth lever was never "more buyers." It was "deeper relationship with existing buyers." They were running an LTV problem and trying to solve it with a CAC tactic.
Lessons
  • Module 10 — Existing customers are the highest-leverage growth source.
  • Module 1 — Selling isn't just chasing new — it's deepening with current.
  • Module 3 — Funnel ladder: the buyer of a €197 product is the qualified lead for a €1,500 product.
  • Module 11 — Hard things first (great product, great relationships); scaling comes after.
B2BDiscoveryClosing
The setup
A SaaS AE has a hot prospect — a Director who attended a webinar, downloaded a whitepaper, and replied to a cold email. AE jumps on a discovery call and is so excited about the buying signals that within 15 minutes is sharing pricing and asking to send a contract.
The collapse
Buyer goes cold. Stops responding. AE follows up 5 times over 3 weeks. Eventually buyer replies: "We're going in a different direction."
The diagnosis

The AE never:

  • Understood the buyer's actual situation in depth
  • Asked about other stakeholders (there were two)
  • Asked about timeline (it was 6 months, not "now")
  • Asked about competition (they were also looking at 2 other vendors)
  • Built any emotional investment in the AE's product

The buyer was researching, not buying. The AE's premature close said "I just want your money," and the buyer pulled back.

The fix

On the next similar opportunity, the AE:

  • Spends 30 minutes on discovery, no pitch
  • Asks every SPIN question they prepared
  • Names 3 stakeholders by the end of the call
  • Maps the decision process
  • Sets a follow-up to talk to the other stakeholders
  • Closes 4 months later, with all stakeholders aligned
Lessons
  • Module 5 — Discovery is the deal. Pitching too early kills.
  • Module 9 — You haven't earned the right to close until you've earned it.
  • Module 8 — Buying signals on a single channel aren't the same as a buying decision.
  • Module 7 — Calibration of pace: match the buyer's, not your enthusiasm.
RetentionChurnCSM
The setup
A B2B SaaS customer paying €4,000/month, in year 2 of usage. Their usage has dropped 40% over 6 months. The CSM notices but doesn't act. Renewal comes up. Customer says: "We're not going to renew — we're not getting value."
The save attempt (too late)

CSM scrambles. Offers a discount. Offers training. Asks what they can do.

Customer"We made the decision two months ago. It's too late."
What should have happened

The signs were obvious in the usage data. A simple "I notice your usage has dropped — anything we should know about?" two months earlier could have surfaced the cause:

  • A champion left their company
  • Their workflow changed
  • They didn't know about a feature that would have helped

Any of these is solvable while it's a concern. None is solvable after they've emotionally decided.

The systemic fix
The CSM team now has automated alerts for usage drops, account changes, and lapsed engagement. They reach out within 7 days. Their save rate on at-risk accounts is now 70%.
Lessons
  • Module 10 — Churn is preventable when you catch it early.
  • Module 10 — Don't wait for the renewal call — that's the funeral, not the ER.
  • Module 11 — Honest service > clever retention pitches.
OnlineDTCOwned audience
The setup
A small skincare brand posts a TikTok of their founder showing how the product fixed her skin. The video goes viral — 4 million views. Orders explode. They sell out of 6 months of inventory in 4 days.
What goes wrong
  • No email capture set up on the product page
  • Shipping system can't handle the volume — orders delayed 6+ weeks
  • Refund rate climbs as people forget they ordered
  • Comments turn from "I want this" to "scam, never delivered"
  • Future TikToks underperform; the algorithm has moved on
  • No way to reach the 4 million people who saw them, because they don't own the audience
What should have happened

Even mid-virality:

  • Capture every visitor's email in exchange for a "back in stock" alert
  • Communicate transparently about delays
  • Manage expectations on the product page
  • Build the email list aggressively while attention was free
  • Run retargeting ads to TikTok engagers
Lessons
  • Module 3 — Rented attention is fragile. Always convert it to owned audience.
  • Module 10 — The cost of a single bad fulfillment is a long stream of lost LTV.
  • Module 11 — Transparency during a crisis preserves trust. Silence destroys it.
Cold callSDRB2B
The setup
A 22-year-old SDR cold-calls a VP of Engineering at a mid-market tech company. The VP picks up, irritated.
The call
VP"Yes? Make it quick."
SDR (resists pitching)"I'll be honest, I know you weren't expecting this call. Most VPs of Eng I talk to are dealing with [specific problem]. Is that something you'd take 60 seconds on, or is now genuinely a bad time?"
VP (pauses)"...what's the problem you mean?"

SDR explains briefly, asks two questions, doesn't pitch. Gets permission to send a short follow-up. Books a 15-minute call for next week.

The deal closes 7 months later at €1.4M ARR.

Why it worked
  • The opening showed self-awareness — a pattern interrupt for cold calls
  • Asked permission instead of barreling through
  • Named the actual pain, not the product
  • Earned 60 seconds rather than demanding it
  • Didn't pitch when the VP showed interest — qualified instead
Lessons
  • Module 4 — Pattern interrupt + permission + relevance > scripted pitch.
  • Module 5 — The first call's job is to earn the second call, not close.
  • Module 2 — Tactical empathy works even in 60-second windows.
  • Module 11 — Honesty ("I know you weren't expecting this") builds more trust than slick.
CopywritingStorytelling
The setup
From 1975 to 2003, the Wall Street Journal ran a single direct mail sales letter. It generated an estimated $2 billion in subscriptions and is considered the most successful sales letter in history.
The breakdown

The letter opens with a story of two young men who graduate from the same college, are equally smart, and work for the same company. 25 years later, one is a manager, the other is the president.

The letter asks: "What made the difference?"

It wasn't intelligence, talent, or luck. It was knowledge, and how they applied it. The WSJ positions itself not as a newspaper, but as the source of that knowledge.

Lessons
  • Module 6 — Sell the outcome (becoming president), not the product (newsprint).
  • Module 2 — Stories bypass logical filters.
B2BEnterpriseMultithreading
The setup
A 6-month, €1.2M SaaS deal. The AE navigated a 12-person buying committee using advanced MEDDPICC.
The breakdown

The AE realized early on that their initial Champion didn't have access to the Economic Buyer (the CFO). They carefully built a parallel relationship with the VP of Engineering (Technical Buyer) who had the CFO's ear. They ran a proof-of-concept that tied technical success directly to a €3M cost saving (Metrics), which satisfied the CFO.

Lessons
  • Module 5 — Multithreading is mandatory in enterprise.
  • Module 5 — If your Champion can't access the Economic Buyer, you need a new Champion.
E-commerceRetention
The setup
A DTC coffee brand faced high CAC on Meta ads. They couldn't drop their price, so they had to fix their LTV.
The fix

They built a replenishment funnel. Based on consumption data, they knew a bag of coffee lasted 22 days. On day 18, the customer received an automated SMS offering a 1-click text-to-reply restock with free shipping. No website visit needed.

Repeat purchase rate skyrocketed from 15% to 42%, solving their CAC problem permanently.

Lessons
  • Module 10 — Reduce friction to zero for returning buyers.
  • Module 3 — LTV solves CAC problems.
SaaSAI ToolsPositioning
The setup
A developer built an AI-powered resume builder wrapper over OpenAI's API. They launched on Product Hunt, acquired 5,000 free users, but could only convert 0.5% to the $15/month paid plan.
The fix

They audited their landing page and realized they were selling the technology: "We use GPT-4 to generate your resume." They changed the headline to sell the outcome: "Get 3x more job interviews this month."

They also dropped the subscription model, realizing users only need a resume builder when looking for a job. They introduced a $20 one-time fee for a 3-month access pass. Conversion jumped to 4%.

Lessons
  • Module 14 — Sell the outcome, not the AI.
  • Module 14 — Pricing models must align with usage patterns. Subscriptions don't work for infrequent tasks.
Founder SalesB2BValidation
The setup
A founder built an internal wiki tool for remote teams. They reached out to their network and successfully onboarded 15 companies onto a 3-month "free pilot."
The collapse

During the pilot, the companies asked for dozens of custom features, which the founder spent weeks building. At the end of the 3 months, the founder tried to transition them to a $99/mo paid plan. 14 out of 15 companies churned immediately.

They realized the companies liked the product because it was free, not because it solved a critical pain point worth paying for.

The fix

The founder stopped offering free pilots. Instead, they asked the next 5 prospects for a $500 setup fee to act as a "Design Partner," which guaranteed priority support and a voice in the roadmap.

Three prospects immediately said no (saving the founder months of wasted time). Two said yes, validating that the pain was real. These two eventually converted to long-term enterprise contracts.

Lessons
  • Module 15 — A free user is not validation. Only money validates pain.
  • Module 15 — The Consultancy Trap: Never build custom features for unpaid customers.
SaaSProduct-Led SalesEnterprise
The setup
A sales rep at a collaborative design software company noticed that 30 individual designers at a major bank were using the free version of their tool. Five of them had paid for pro accounts using personal credit cards.
The fix

Instead of cold-calling the CIO of the bank, the rep emailed the five "power users" who were already paying. They offered to jump on a quick call to help them unlock an enterprise feature (Assist motion).

During the call, the rep learned that the designers were frustrated they couldn't share secure prototypes internally. The rep used this information to craft a targeted pitch to the VP of Design, showing them that 30 of their employees were already using the tool, but without bank-level security.

The VP immediately signed a $150k enterprise contract to consolidate the shadow IT and secure the data.

Lessons
  • Module 16 — The Consolidate Motion: Use "shadow IT" usage as leverage to sell to executives.
  • Module 16 — Never pitch an executive until you have interviewed the power users.

How to read case studies

The point of these isn't to memorize the stories. It's to see the principles operating in the wild so you can recognize them when they apply to your own situations.

Whenever you observe a sale — your own, someone else's, an ad you saw, a salesperson at a shop — ask:

  • Which module of this course does this map to?
  • What did they do right?
  • What did they do wrong?
  • What would I do differently?
After 100 observations done this way, your sales intuition will be unrecognizably sharper.

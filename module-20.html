1. What this lens is and what it is not

This module sits in Level 6 — advanced and contested lenses — and the framing matters more than the content. Mimetic theory, developed by René Girard, is a work of literary and anthropological interpretation. It is not an experimental finding, it has no effect size, and it cannot be tested in the form Girard stated it. Treating it as science is a category error.

So why include it? Because it is a good hypothesis generator about a phenomenon that is empirically supported. Social proof is the strongest-replicating of the influence principles — Bond's meta-analysis of 125 Asch-type studies found a weighted mean effect around d = 0.89 — and its most important boundary condition is that the effect depends on the referent group being seen as genuinely similar. Mimetic theory offers a vocabulary for thinking about who a buyer treats as similar, and why some references move a decision while others are politely ignored.

The rule for this entire level of the course: a lens generates questions you would not otherwise have asked. It never generates conclusions about a person. The moment you find yourself saying "she's mimetically driven by the CFO," you have stopped using a hypothesis and started applying a label — and labels stop you asking.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

State what mimetic theory claims and what evidential status that claim has.

Distinguish the empirically supported phenomenon (social influence, comparability-dependent) from the interpretive framework laid over it.

Identify a buyer's actual reference group from evidence rather than from assumption.

Select references by comparability rather than by prestige.

Produce a social-influence hypothesis map with disconfirming tests attached.

3. The model, stated precisely

Girard's claim is that desire is not primarily autonomous but triangular: we want things because a model — someone we regard as similar-but-slightly-ahead — wants or has them. The model is neither a stranger (whose choices are irrelevant to us) nor a distant idol (whose choices are unreachable), but a near peer.

Table 1: The model's components and their commercial reading

ConceptGirard's claimCommercial reading (as hypothesis)How it becomes stereotyping
Triangular desireDesire is mediated by a model rather than arising directly from the object.Buyers' criteria are shaped by what comparable organizations are seen to be doing. "What are others like us doing?" is a real question buyers ask.Concluding a specific buyer has no independent judgment.
Proximity of the modelMediation is strongest when the model is close in status.The most useful part. A reference works when the buyer sees themselves in it; prestige without similarity does nothing.Assuming you know who someone's peer group is without asking.

Only the second row does reliable commercial work. The rest is interesting and largely unfalsifiable in a sales context, and should be held very loosely.

4. What the empirical literature does and does not support

Table 2: Separating the supported from the interpretive

ClaimStatusEvidence
People's judgments are influenced by what similar others do.R — well supportedConformity meta-analysis across 125 Asch-type studies, weighted mean around d = 0.89; a 2023 replication reproduced rates close to the original ~33%.
The effect depends on perceived similarity to the referent.R — supportedConsistent moderator across the social-proof literature. This is the boundary condition that matters commercially.
Organizations imitate peer organizations under uncertainty.R — supported in a different literatureInstitutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell): mimetic pressure is a documented organizational phenomenon, particularly where outcomes are hard to evaluate.

Specific individuals can be identified as mimetically driven.X — do not do thisNo instrument exists. This is personality attribution with a literary vocabulary, and it will make you worse by replacing questions with a label.
The one genuinely useful transfer

Institutional isomorphism is the better-evidenced sibling of this idea and is directly applicable: organizations copy peer organizations most strongly when outcomes are hard to evaluate. That is precisely the condition in most enterprise software purchases — the buyer cannot know in advance whether it will work. It predicts, correctly, that "who else like us has done this?" becomes more important as the decision becomes harder to assess on its merits, not less.

5. Legitimate commercial use: reference selection

The practical payoff is narrow and real: reference selection is a matching problem, not a prestige contest. Most vendors lead with their largest logo. Most buyers discount it, because the largest logo is usually the least comparable.

Table 3: Dimensions of comparability, ranked by how much they usually matter

DimensionQuestionWhy it matters
1. Structural similaritySame shape of operation — site count, workflow, system landscape, regulatory regime.Determines whether the result plausibly transfers. Usually the strongest dimension.
2. Constraint similaritySame binding constraint — budget, headcount freeze, legacy system, deadline.A buyer identifies with someone who was stuck in the same way, more than with someone in the same industry.
3. Status proximityA peer they would compare themselves to — not a giant, not a minnow.The mimetic insight. Too far above is aspirational and dismissible; too far below is irrelevant.
4. Role similarityThe reference contact holds the same job as the person you are speaking to.Buyers trust their functional counterparts more than they trust a vendor or a C-level quote.
5. IndustrySame sector.Usually over-weighted by vendors. Two logistics firms with different operating models are less comparable than a logistics firm and a distributor with the same model.

Two rules follow. Ask rather than assume: "Whose approach do you pay attention to?" and "Who do you benchmark against?" produce a buyer's actual reference group in one question, and it is regularly not the one you would have guessed. And handle direct competitors carefully: naming a close rival as a customer can motivate or can trigger defensiveness and confidentiality fears. Ask before deploying it — "would it be useful to hear how [rival] approached this, or would you rather I stayed off that?" — and respect the answer.

Where this lens turns into manipulation

Manufacturing rivalry to force a decision — "your competitors are already doing this, you're falling behind" — is fabricated social pressure when you have no evidence it is true, and manufactured urgency when you do. It fails the Module 02 compression test and the Module 12 disclosure test simultaneously. The legitimate version is factual and neutral: "three organizations with your structure have done this; here is what happened, including one where it did not work."

6. Worked example: choosing the right reference

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A vendor is selling to a 900-staff regional building society. Four references are available. The AE tests which one the buyer actually finds comparable, rather than assuming.

What the test revealed: the vendor's proudest reference was the least persuasive, and the insurance broker — dismissed internally as "wrong industry" — was rated highly once the shared regulator was named. Industry, the dimension vendors weight most, was the least predictive of the five.

The question that produced this: the AE simply asked, before offering anything: "When you're deciding something like this, whose approach do you actually pay attention to?" The buyer named two societies and, unprompted, said "and honestly, anyone under the same regulator — the sector matters less than the rulebook." That sentence reordered the entire reference strategy for the segment.

The Brayford thread · Week 6 · which reference Brayford actually wanted

The logo Cadence declined to use

Cadence's largest customer is a 60-site national operator. It is the reference the deck leads with, and Alex did not use it at Brayford.

The question asked first, before offering anything: "when you're weighing something like this, whose approach do you actually pay attention to?" Priya's answer was two named regional operators of similar size — and then, unprompted: "and honestly, anyone who's just been through an acquisition. The size matters less than the mess."

That sentence reordered the reference strategy. The national operator scored high on prestige and low on every dimension that mattered: different scale, a dedicated change function Brayford does not have, and no post-acquisition dual-estate problem. Using it would have signalled that Cadence had not listened.

What was used instead: a 12-site operator eighteen months post-merger, and — deliberately — the operator where it did not work, whose bank records had never been reconciled. Declining the impressive reference on comparability grounds is a costly signal, and it is why the negative case was believed when it followed.

7. Weak versus improved use of a peer signal

Composite Case StudyScenario: The seller wants to use social proof with a cautious buyer.

Weak execution (manufactured rivalry and prestige name-dropping)

Seller: "Honestly, most of the market has already moved on this. We work with some of the biggest names in financial services — I can't say who, but you'd recognise them. Your competitors are getting ahead here, and I'd hate to see you left behind. Nobody wants to be the last one still doing this manually."

Why it fails: "Most of the market" has no denominator. "Biggest names" is prestige without comparability, and to a 900-person society it signals the opposite of relevance. "Your competitors are getting ahead" is an assertion with no evidence, presented to create pressure — fabricated social proof, which fails the compression test. And "nobody wants to be the last one" is confirmshaming, which the FTC treats as a dark pattern. The buyer's most likely internal response is that this seller does not know anything specific about their situation.

Improved execution (ask for the reference group, then match on comparability)

Seller: "Before I offer you references — when you're weighing something like this, whose approach do you actually pay attention to? I'd rather give you someone you find relevant than someone I find impressive."
Buyer: "Societies our size, mostly. And honestly anyone under the same regulator — the rulebook matters more than the sector."
Seller: "That's useful, and it changes what I'd show you. I'd skip our largest customer entirely — they've got a hundred-person change function and I don't think their experience transfers to you at all.

Two that do: a society at about your headcount, same regulator, and their compliance officer had exactly your problem — she was doing evidence collection by hand with no additional headcount coming. And an insurance broker, which I know sounds off-sector, but they're under the same regulator with the same evidence requirements, and their workflow is closer to yours than the bank's is.

I'd also mention a third who got nothing out of it — their document management was on three systems and we couldn't bridge them. Worth checking whether that's you before anything else.

On your direct competitors — we do work with one. Would it be useful to hear how they approached it, or would you rather I stayed off that?"
Buyer: "Stay off it. But yes to the other two."

Why it succeeds: The reference group was elicited rather than assumed, and the buyer's answer contradicted what the vendor would have guessed. The seller then declined to use their most impressive logo, on comparability grounds — which is a costly signal of honesty. The negative case was volunteered as a diagnostic. And the competitor question was asked rather than deployed, and the answer respected.

Where this fails

Where even experienced sellers get this wrong

Experienced sellers ask about the reference group and then treat the answer as stable. It is elicited once, recorded, and used for the rest of the cycle.

Reference groups shift with the question being decided. Priya's peer group when assessing whether the problem is real was other operators her size. When the question became whether the implementation would survive a governance review, the relevant referent changed entirely — she wanted to know what happened at organisations with an information-governance function as cautious as Dev Osei, which is a different population and one Cadence had never characterised.

The seller who keeps citing the week-6 references into week 20 is answering a question nobody is asking any more.

Re-ask when the decision changes hands. "Now that Dev is involved, whose experience would he find relevant?" costs one sentence and produces a different, better-targeted set — and it is the kind of question that makes a veto holder feel considered rather than processed.

The mechanism: Perceived similarity is relative to the judgement being made, not to the organisation as a whole. A referent that is comparable on operational scale may be irrelevant on governance posture — so a fixed reference set drifts out of relevance as the deal moves between stakeholders with different questions, while continuing to look well-chosen.

8. Practice: the social-influence hypothesis map

Exercise goal: identify a buyer's real reference group from evidence, select references by comparability, and attach a disconfirming test to every hypothesis.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Take a live account. Write down who you assume their reference group is, and why. Be specific — this is the hypothesis you will test.
  2. Ask. Use two questions in your next conversation: "Whose approach do you pay attention to?" and "Who do you benchmark yourselves against?" Record the answer verbatim.
  3. Compare the answer to your assumption. Note where you were wrong; that gap is the finding.
  4. Score your available references against the five comparability dimensions in Table 3 for this specific buyer.
  5. Identify one reference you would normally lead with that you should not use, and say why.
  6. Select the negative case you will volunteer — a comparable customer where it did not work, and the reason.
  7. Decide your position on naming a direct competitor, and write the question you will ask before doing so.
  8. Write the disconfirming test for each hypothesis: what would you observe if this reference group is not actually influential here?

Evaluation rubric: Assumption written before asking (15%); reference group elicited with the buyer's verbatim answer (25%); gap between assumption and answer honestly recorded (15%); references scored on all five dimensions (20%); one prestige reference deliberately excluded with a reason (10%); disconfirming test written (15%).

Beginner variation: Ask the two elicitation questions in your next five conversations and record the answers. The variance across buyers in the same segment is usually larger than expected, and it is the whole practical content of this module.

Advanced variation: Aggregate the elicited reference groups across twenty accounts in one segment. If a pattern emerges — as with "same regulator matters more than same sector" — it is a positioning finding (Module 32) and a reference-portfolio decision, not just a per-deal tactic.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. What is the evidential status of mimetic theory, and how should that change how you use it?

It is an interpretive philosophical framework (class F), not an empirical finding — it has no effect size and cannot be tested as stated. So it may be used to generate questions ("who does this buyer treat as a peer, and why?") and never to produce conclusions about a person. What is well supported is the underlying phenomenon it gestures at: social influence, with perceived similarity to the referent as the critical moderator.

2. Why does a vendor's largest logo often fail as a reference?

Because prestige is not comparability, and social proof depends on the referent being seen as similar. A 900-person society hearing about a global bank concludes the reverse of what the vendor intends — that the bank's hundred-person change function makes their experience irrelevant. Structural similarity, constraint similarity, and status proximity all predict better than scale or fame; industry, the dimension vendors weight most heavily, is frequently the weakest of the five.

3. What single question replaces most guessing about a buyer's reference group?

"When you're weighing something like this, whose approach do you actually pay attention to?" — optionally paired with "who do you benchmark yourselves against?" The answers are regularly not what the vendor would have assumed: a manufacturer may name their largest customer's audit requirements rather than sector peers; a regulated firm may name anyone under the same rulebook regardless of industry. It costs one sentence and it routinely reorders an entire reference strategy.

4. When does using a competitor as a reference become manipulation?

When it is asserted without evidence or deployed to manufacture pressure — "your competitors are already ahead, you're falling behind." That is fabricated social proof if untrue and manufactured urgency if true, and it fails both the compression test and the disclosure test. The legitimate version is factual, neutral, and consented: ask first ("would it be useful to hear how they approached this, or would you rather I stayed off that?"), respect the answer, and present what happened including where it did not work.

5. Which better-evidenced idea does the commercially useful part of this lens overlap with?

Institutional isomorphism (DiMaggio & Powell) — the documented tendency of organizations to imitate peer organizations, most strongly when outcomes are hard to evaluate. That condition describes most complex software and services purchases precisely, and it yields a testable prediction: the harder a decision is to assess on its merits, the more weight "who else like us has done this?" will carry. That is a claim you can act on and check, which is more than mimetic theory itself offers.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

This lens earns its place for one insight: references work by identification, not by impressiveness. Everything else in it should be held loosely, and none of it should ever be applied as a description of a person.

  • Mimetic theory is interpretive, not empirical. Use it to generate questions, never conclusions.
  • The supported phenomenon underneath is social influence, moderated by perceived similarity.
  • Rank references by structural and constraint similarity and status proximity. Industry is usually over-weighted.
  • Elicit the buyer's reference group rather than assuming it. The answer often contradicts your assumption.
  • Declining to use your most impressive logo, on comparability grounds, is a costly and credible signal.
  • Never manufacture rivalry. Peer facts, stated neutrally, with the negative case included.
  • Organizations copy peers most when outcomes are hard to evaluate — which is most complex purchases.

Immediate action: In your next conversation ask "whose approach do you actually pay attention to?" before offering any reference. Then compare the answer to the reference you were about to use — in a surprising number of cases, you were about to use the wrong one.

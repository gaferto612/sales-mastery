06Complex B2B

You get 5% of their time. Design for the other 95%.

Enterprise buying groups run six to ten people and spend roughly 17% of the purchase cycle with any supplier at all. This module covers buying-centre roles, the major discovery methodologies graded honestly, and the artifacts that sell for you when you are not in the room.

1. The structural facts of complex buying

Three findings define the terrain, and every technique in this module is a response to one of them.

The decision is collective. Gartner's buying research puts typical enterprise buying groups at six to ten people, rising past fifteen in large or regulated purchases. Webster & Wind established the conceptual foundation for this in 1972 and it has only intensified since. A "yes" from your champion is not a decision; it is one input to a process you cannot see.

Your access is minimal. The same research finds buying groups spend around 17% of total purchase time meeting with potential suppliers — and once that is divided across a shortlist, a single vendor may receive around 5%. Most of the decision happens in meetings you are not in, using documents you did not write.

The constraint is comprehension, not information. Roughly 55% of buyers report encountering an overwhelming volume of trustworthy-but-conflicting information. Sending more material to an overwhelmed group makes their problem worse. The scarce good is not information; it is a way to reconcile it.

These are class V/F findings from survey research with an enterprise-technology skew, and the exact percentages should be held loosely. The direction is not in doubt, and the design implication is severe: the artifact you leave behind does more of your selling than you do. A seller who optimizes only for the meetings they attend is optimizing 5% of the process.

2. Observable learning objectives

By completing this module you will be able to demonstrate the following:

Map a buying centre by role rather than by job title, and identify which roles are unrepresented in your current coverage.

Select and apply a discovery methodology appropriate to the deal, understanding what each one is good at and what it omits.

Distinguish a champion from a coach and from an enthusiastic contact, using behavioral evidence.

Build a decision-support artifact designed to be circulated without you present.

Produce a stakeholder and decision map that exposes coverage gaps and unsurfaced disagreement.

3. The buying centre: roles, not job titles

The single most common enterprise error is mapping the org chart instead of the decision. Roles are functions in a process; one person may hold three, and three people may share one. Titles tell you almost nothing — a "Director of IT" may be the economic buyer at one company and a gatekeeper at another.

The champion test — behavioral, not emotional

A real champion does three things you can observe: (1) they give you access to people above them, (2) they tell you inconvenient truths — the competitor's advantage, the budget risk, the internal opposition — and (3) they take action when you are not there and can describe what they did. Someone who loves your product, replies quickly, and will not introduce you to their VP is a coach: valuable for information, useless for advocacy. Confusing the two is the single most common cause of a confident forecast on a dead deal.

Map coverage explicitly: for each role, name the person, the last direct contact, and the evidence for their position. Roles with no named person are your risk register. In most stalled enterprise deals the missing entry is the veto holder or the economic buyer — and it was missing for months before anyone noticed.

4. The methodologies, graded honestly

Sales methodologies are class F: structures for organizing work. They are neither true nor false. The professional question is which one fits the deal in front of you, and what each one systematically ignores.

The professional posture is to hold two or three of these and switch by situation. A team that adopts one methodology as doctrine will systematically miss whatever that methodology omits — most commonly consensus (MEDDIC), diagnosis (Challenger), or the possibility that the buyer simply cannot decide (everything except JOLT).

5. Sense-making and the artifact that travels

If you get 5% of the buying group's time, the highest-leverage thing you can build is not a better meeting. It is a document that survives circulation without you — one your champion can forward to the CFO, the security lead, and the two people you have never met, and that does not degrade when they read it alone.

Table 3: What a circulating artifact must contain

ElementWhy it must be thereTest of adequacy
The problem in their wordsReaders who were not in your meetings must recognise their own situation, not your category.Contains at least three phrases quoted directly from the buyer's own descriptions.
The options considered, including doing nothingA document that presents only your solution reads as a brochure and is discounted accordingly.Do-nothing is costed, and at least one alternative is described fairly enough that its advocate would agree.
Decision criteria with weightsGroups disagree because they are optimizing different things without saying so. Weights surface it.The weights were set by the buying group, not by you.
Assumptions, ranges, and what would change the answerA single-point estimate invites a fight about the number. A range invites a conversation about the assumption.Every financial figure has a stated source and a sensitivity.
Honest weaknessesEvery group contains someone whose job is to find the flaw. Better they find it in your document.Names at least one thing your solution does badly, and what to do about it.
The decision the reader is being asked to makeChoice overload and omission bias both feed on ambiguity about what is actually being decided.One sentence, near the top: what is being approved, by whom, by when, and what it costs to reverse.
The test that most vendor material fails

Give your document to someone who has never met you and ask them to explain the decision back. If they can only describe your product, the artifact is a brochure and it will not travel. If they can describe the choice, its stakes, and the honest arguments on each side, you have built something that works in the 95% of the process you cannot attend.

6. Worked example: mapping a stalled enterprise deal

Composite Case Study

Scenario: A £680k workforce-management deal at a hospital group has been at 80% forecast confidence for two quarters. The champion is enthusiastic and responsive. The AE cannot explain why nothing moves. A deal review maps roles rather than titles.

Diagnosis: the deal was never at 80%. Three critical roles were unnamed, and the "champion" was a coach who had been quietly unable to move it. Forecast corrected to 20%.

Actions taken: the AE asked the coach directly — "I've been treating you as the person driving this, and I think that's unfair to you; who actually owns the budget line, and what would make it easier for you to introduce me?" The answer: the coach had no standing to approach the Director of Ops and had been embarrassed to say so. The AE built a two-page decision brief the coach could forward without personal risk. That brief reached the Director of Ops in nine days, surfaced an information-governance review nobody had scheduled, and moved the deal into a real — slower — process. It closed at £510k eleven months later. The reduced value and extended timeline were the accurate picture, and having it early was worth more than the optimistic one.

7. Weak versus improved multi-threading

Composite Case StudyScenario: The AE needs access to the economic buyer, and the single contact is protective.

Weak execution (demanding access, or going around)

AE: "I really need to get in front of your Director of Operations before we can move forward. Can you set that up this week?"
Contact: "She's extremely busy. Let me handle the internal side — I'll take it to her."
AE: "Okay, no problem." [Two weeks later, emails the Director directly, copying the contact.]

Why it fails: The request is framed entirely around the seller's need — "before we can move forward" — which gives the contact no reason to spend political capital. When it is refused, the AE goes around, which is the one move that reliably converts a supportive contact into a blocker. Worse, it never asked why the contact was reluctant, so the actual obstacle — that they lacked the standing to make the introduction — remained invisible.

Improved execution (make the introduction serve them, and surface the real obstacle)

AE: "Can I check an assumption? I've been working as though you'd be taking this to the Director of Ops yourself. Is that a fair thing for me to be assuming, or am I putting you in an awkward position?"
Contact: "Honestly — I don't really have a route to her. It'd go through my head of department first."
AE: "That's really useful and I'd rather know now. Two things then. First, what does your head of department need to see to be willing to take it up? Second — the risk I'd flag is that projects like this usually get stopped late by information governance rather than by budget, and if we surface that now it's a three-week conversation instead of a three-month one at the end. Would it help if I wrote a two-page brief you could forward without it looking like a vendor document — problem in your words, the options including doing nothing, the IG questions we'd expect, and what we're weak at?"
Contact: "That would actually help a lot."

Why it succeeds: The AE tested an assumption rather than making a demand, which let the contact admit a limitation without losing face. It reframed access as a risk-reduction step for the buyer, not a stage gate for the seller. It named a specific, credible late-stage risk — the veto holder — which gives the internal case urgency that does not come from the vendor's quarter. And it offered to do the work in a form the contact could use safely.

8. Practice: stakeholder and decision map

Exercise goal: produce a role-based map of a live enterprise opportunity that exposes coverage gaps, unsurfaced disagreement, and a corrected forecast.

Applies to your real situation
  1. Take a live deal over your median value. List every buying-centre role from Table 1, with named people, last direct contact date, and the evidence for their position. Write "unknown" where it is unknown.
  2. Apply the champion test to your primary contact. Record which of the three behaviors they have actually demonstrated, with an example. If fewer than two, they are a coach — relabel them.
  3. Identify the veto holders. In regulated environments there are usually at least two. State when you will engage each and what they will need.
  4. Write the decision criteria as the buying group would write them, with weights. Then mark which weights you know versus assume.
  5. Name one point of genuine disagreement inside the buying group. If you cannot name one, you do not have enough access — that is the finding.
  6. Draft the two-page circulating artifact against the six elements in Table 3, including the honest weakness.
  7. Restate your forecast confidence in light of the map, and write one sentence explaining the change.

Completed exemplar (manufacturing ERP add-on, £340k)

Veto: InfoSec (engaged, questionnaire returned) and Works Council (not identified until this exercise). Blocker: plant manager at site 2, prefers the incumbent.

Evaluation rubric: Role coverage completeness including honest "unknown" entries (20%); rigorous behavioral application of the champion test (20%); veto-holder identification and engagement plan (20%); criteria weights with known/assumed marking (15%); a genuinely named internal disagreement (15%); artifact quality including the honest weakness (10%).

Beginner variation: Map roles for a purchase your own organization made recently, interviewing two people who were involved. Seeing a buying centre from the inside is the fastest way to understand how little the vendor could see.

Advanced variation: Build the map for three deals in the same segment and look for the pattern in which role is habitually unmapped. That pattern is a team-level coaching finding, and it is usually more valuable than any individual deal review.

9. Applied knowledge check

1. Your contact loves the product, replies within an hour, and will not introduce you to their VP. What are they?

A coach, not a champion. The behavioral test has three parts — grants access upward, tells you inconvenient truths, and acts independently when you are absent. Someone failing the access test is almost always failing it for a reason worth knowing: they may lack standing, may have burned capital on a previous project, or may not actually believe the case survives scrutiny. Ask directly and without blame. Coaches are genuinely valuable for information; treating one as a champion is what produces confident forecasts on dead deals.

2. Why does sending more material to an overwhelmed buying group make things worse?

Because their constraint is comprehension, not information. Around 55% of buyers report already facing an overwhelming volume of trustworthy but conflicting information, and adding to it raises the cost of deciding — which, combined with omission bias, pushes the group toward doing nothing. The useful move is sense-making: helping the group reconcile what they already have, name where sources genuinely conflict, and see which differences actually matter for their situation.

3. A deal is at 90% and dies suddenly at security review. What was the mapping error?

A veto holder was left unmapped and unengaged. Security, legal, compliance, works councils, and clinical or information governance functions can stop a deal unilaterally, and they are rewarded for caution rather than for enabling purchases. They also cannot be persuaded late — a security team asked to approve in week 40 of a 42-week cycle will default to no, because they have no time to do the work properly and every incentive not to take the risk. Engage them early, treat their questionnaire as a real deliverable, and expect a genuine review.

4. Should you adopt one sales methodology across your team?

Adopt one for inspection and several for execution. A shared qualification vocabulary — MEDDPICC or equivalent — makes forecasts and deal reviews objective, and that consistency is worth a lot. But a team that treats one methodology as doctrine will systematically miss whatever it omits: MEDDIC is silent on how to run discovery, Challenger says little about consensus, and nearly all of them assume the buyer's problem is being unconvinced rather than being unable to decide. Teach the grading in Table 2 so sellers can name what their default framework is not telling them.

5. What single test tells you whether your proposal document will survive circulation?

Give it to someone who has never met you and ask them to explain the decision back to you. If they can only describe your product, it is a brochure and it will be skimmed and discarded in the meetings you do not attend. If they can describe the choice, the stakes, the honest arguments on each side, and what would change the answer, it is a decision-support artifact — and it works in the roughly 95% of the buying process where you are not present.

10. Practical conclusion & next action

Complex B2B selling is the discipline of being useful to a group you cannot fully see, in meetings you will not attend, through documents you do not control once you send them. Everything else follows from taking that seriously.

  • Map roles, not titles. Unmapped roles are your risk register, and the missing entry is usually the veto holder or the economic buyer.
  • Test the champion behaviorally: access upward, inconvenient truths, independent action. Two of three or it is a coach.
  • Engage veto holders early. They cannot be persuaded in the final fortnight and are not incentivized to try.
  • Hold two or three methodologies and switch by situation; adopt one only for inspection.
  • The constraint is comprehension, not information. Reconcile rather than add.
  • Build the artifact that travels — options including do-nothing, weighted criteria, ranges, and an honest weakness.

Immediate action: Take your largest open deal and write down the named person for every role in Table 1, plus the date you last spoke to each directly. Every "unknown" and every date older than six weeks is a forecast risk you are currently carrying without pricing it.
